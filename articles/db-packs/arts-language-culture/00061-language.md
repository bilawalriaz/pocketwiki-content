# Language

## Core idea

Language is a structured system of communication built on grammar and vocabulary, used by humans in spoken, signed, and written forms. Two properties define it: **productivity**, the ability to generate an unlimited number of sentences from a finite set of elements, and **displacement**, the capacity to refer to things not physically present. Humans acquire language through social learning rather than by instinct alone, and estimates of living human languages range from 5,000 to 7,000, with Ethnologue cataloging 7,170 as of 2026. The scientific study of language, linguistics, draws on philosophy, neuroscience, anthropology, and computation, because language underpins human culture, identity, social organization, and thought.

## How scholars have defined language

Three definitions persist. As a **mental faculty**, language is largely innate, an idea traced to Kant and Descartes, formalized in Chomsky's Universal Grammar and Fodor's innatism, and supported by children's untaught acquisition and the spontaneous emergence of creoles and Nicaraguan Sign Language. As a **formal symbolic system**, language follows rules analyzed in Saussure's structuralism and Chomsky's generative grammar, while Tarski and Russell developed logical accounts of meaning. As a **tool for communication**, language is explained by its function, associated with pragmatics, sociolinguistics, and the later Wittgenstein, Austin, Grice, Searle, and Quine.

## How language differs from animal communication

Hockett's "design features" mark out human language: it is open-ended, productive, built on a dual code of meaningless elements combining into meaningful units, recursive (phrases inside phrases), grammatically categorized, and modality-independent. Some animal systems partially meet these criteria. Chestnut-crowned babblers rearrange acoustic elements into distinct calls; pied babblers distinguish calls by repetition count; the bonobo Kanzi learned lexigrams. No animal matches a 4-year-old human's vocabulary and grammar. Displacement exists in bees, which signal nectar locations, but its human degree is unique.

## Origins

Continuity-based theories, the majority view, hold that language evolved gradually from primate cognition or communication, with predecessors emphasizing animal cognition (Pinker), gestural and vocal cooperation (Tomasello), or music (Rousseau, Herder, Darwin; Mithen today). A March 2024 report dates the beginnings of human language to ~1.6 million years ago, placing proto-language well before *Homo sapiens*; full modern language is often tied to the Upper Paleolithic revolution less than 100,000 years ago. Discontinuity theories, such as Chomsky's, propose a random mutation reorganized the brain, "implanting a language organ."

## Structure

Language comprises signs, meanings, and a code linking them. **Semantics** relates sign forms to meanings by convention; all languages feature predication. **Phonology** studies phonemes, the minimal contrastive sound units such as English /b/ and /p/ in *bat* and *pat*, and their variants (allophones), like aspirated and unaspirated /p/, which contrast in Mandarin but not English. Inventories range from 10 (Pirahã) to 141 (Taa). **Grammar** includes morphology, the study of roots, affixes, inflection, and derivation, and syntax, which governs word order and phrase structure. Languages range from isolating (Chinese) through fusional (Latin) and agglutinative (Turkish) to polysynthetic (Yupik). **Typology** classifies word order (SVO, SOV) and alignment types (nominative-accusative, ergative, tripartite).

## Brain and acquisition

Lesions in Broca's area cause expressive aphasia (comprehension intact, fluent speech impaired); Wernicke's area lesions cause receptive aphasia (fluent but meaningless speech). Both affect sign language analogously, showing the deficit is linguistic, not motor. FOXP2 is the only gene definitely implicated in language. Children acquire a first language without teaching: responding to speech from birth, babbling by ~6 months, ~50 words at 18 months, then holophrases, telegraphic speech, and adult-like fluency by ages 3–5. Second-language acquisition rarely yields native-like fluency.

## Social life, change, and diversity

Language signals group identity through accents, registers, and social deixis such as Dyirbal mother-in-law speech or Thai and Javanese honorifics. Writing emerged c. 3400–3200 BC in Sumerian cuneiform and Egyptian hieroglyphs. Language change includes regular sound change (Grimm's law, the Great Vowel Shift), erosion (Latin *mea domina* → *madame*), and grammaticalization (*going to* → *gonna*). Contact produces borrowing, language areas (Balkans, Mesoamerica, Ethiopia), pidgins, and creoles (Tok Pisin, Haitian Kreyòl, Michif). The language/dialect boundary is often political: the Serbo-Croatian split and Chinese "dialects," which are more distinct than Swedish and Norwegian, illustrate this. Indo-European has 46% of speakers, Sino-Tibetan 20%, Niger-Congo 6.9%, Austronesian 5.5%; Papua New Guinea leads with 830 tongues. Of 6,000–7,000 languages, 50–90% may be extinct by 2100; UNESCO defines five endangerment levels, and revitalization efforts (Welsh as a model for Māori and Hawaiian programs) continue.
