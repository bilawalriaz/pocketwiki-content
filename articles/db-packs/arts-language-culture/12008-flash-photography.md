# Flash (photography)

A photographic flash is a device that produces a brief, intense burst of artificial light, typically a few thousandths of a second, at a colour temperature near 5500 K (close to daylight). It illuminates dark scenes, freezes fast motion, or modifies existing light. The word *flash* refers either to the burst itself or to the unit that produces it.

## Core mechanism

A modern electronic flash charges a high-capacitance capacitor to several hundred volts, then dumps that stored energy through a xenon-filled flash tube when the shutter's sync contact fires. The result is an almost instantaneous flash, shorter than typical shutter speeds, which makes it easy to synchronise the peak brightness with a fully open shutter. Many cameras include a small built-in flash, and others accept an external unit on a standardised accessory mount called a *hot shoe*. Multiple units can be linked: a *master* fires synchronously with the shutter and *slave* units trigger from its light, a flash sync cable, or a radio signal.

## Historical evolution

Photographers first burned magnesium ribbon or *flash powder*, a magnesium-and-potassium-chlorate mixture, in open troughs. Flash powder produced a brilliant burst but also smoke, noise, and serious injury risk, especially when damp. *Flashbulbs* solved the safety problem by sealing magnesium, later zirconium, filaments inside glass bulbs filled with oxygen. Each bulb was used once and discarded. Commercial flashbulb production began in Germany in 1929, and slow-burning bulbs forced cameras to use shutter speeds of 1/10 to 1/50 second. Later bulbs (PF1 in 1954, AG-1 in 1958) ignited faster, enabling shorter synchronised exposures.

Harold Eugene Edgerton introduced the electronic xenon flash tube in 1931. Because each pulse lasted only a tiny fraction of a second, he could freeze events invisible to the eye, such as a bullet piercing an apple. The technique is still called *strobe* (short for *stroboscopy*) in the United States. Electronic flash was expensive and bulky at first, but by the early 1970s amateur units cost under $100. The technology then displaced flashbulbs in amateur work.

## Flash duration and intensity

Duration is reported as two figures: t0.5, the time above 50% of peak intensity, and t0.1, the time above 10%. A flash might have t0.5 of 1/1200 and t0.1 of 1/450; both values determine whether a moving subject is frozen. Smaller units shorten the pulse by trimming the discharge time when output is reduced; larger studio units instead reduce the capacitor charge, which lengthens t0.5 and t0.1 at lower settings. Output is normally dialled in stops (full, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 and so on). Power is quoted either as a *guide number*, an exposure-setting shorthand, or, for studio *monolights*, in watt-seconds.

## Synchronising with focal-plane shutters

Many cameras use focal-plane shutters, two curtains that travel across the sensor. At very fast shutter speeds only a narrow slit ever exposes the sensor, so the entire frame is uncovered only when the shutter speed is no shorter than the curtain travel time. A typical full-frame shutter takes about 2.4 ms to cross the sensor; with a roughly 1 ms full-power flash, the minimum exposure that still covers the whole frame evenly is about 3.4 ms, around 1/290 s. Most modern cameras cap ordinary flash sync at 1/200 or 1/250 s. To shoot faster than that, dedicated units offer FP-sync (High Speed Sync), pulsing the tube repeatedly while the slit traverses the sensor; each pulse carries only a fraction of the total energy, so the guide number falls by roughly √(exposure time / shutter traverse time), for example from 100 to about 32 when going from 1/200 to 1/2000 s.

## Lighting techniques

Direct on-camera flash gives harsh, frontal light and produces the *red-eye effect*, a red glow caused when light reflects off the retina straight back to the lens. Pre-flash red-eye reduction works by contracting the subject's irises. Softer results come from a *bounce flash*, where the head is angled at a ceiling or umbrella so light reflects onto the subject, or from a *softbox* or diffuser over the lamp. *Fill flash* supplements ambient light, balancing a back-lit subject with the background; the aperture is set for the subject while shutter speed exposes the background. Studio work adds *modelling lights*, continuous lamps beside the flash tube that preview the lighting, with LEDs now replacing older incandescent bulbs. Coloured gels (often CTO for tungsten correction) match flash colour to other sources.

## Limitations

Small built-in flashes reach only a few metres before output falls off, after which pictures become dark and noisy. On-camera flash flattens shadows and irritates subjects, and in crowded venues the combined firing of many cameras can distract performers while illuminating nothing useful. Some museums prohibit flash outright. Studio units require mains power and careful rigging, including sandbagged stands, because an umbrella-topped lightstand is easily toppled by a breeze.

High-current LEDs are now used as flashes in smartphones and some cameras. They run on low voltage, are smaller and more efficient than xenon tubes, and double as autofocus aids, video lights, or ordinary torches, though they remain dimmer than xenon flashes.
