# Performing arts

Performing arts are arts performed for an audience rather than fixed as physical objects, distinguishing them from visual arts like painting or sculpture. The major branches are theatre, dance, music, opera, and film, with supporting forms including circus, puppetry, mime, magic, improv, and stand-up. Every known society has developed them: music and dance reach back to prehistoric times, while theatrical storytelling emerged independently in Ancient Greece, India, and China. Live performance depends on the presence and reactions of an audience, which is why recording and streaming imitate but never replace the shared moment.

## The main branches

**Theatre** tells stories through actors who combine speech, gesture, music, dance, and visual spectacle. It includes scripted plays, musicals, opera, improvisation, and performance art. Venues range from a formal proscenium (a framed arch with a curtain separating stage from audience) to intimate black-box rooms, thrust stages that push into the audience, and improvised "found" spaces.

**Dance** is rhythmic human movement, usually to music, shaped into audience entertainment. Definitions range from communal folk forms to virtuoso techniques like ballet. Choreography is the art of composing dances and deciding how movement carries meaning. Modern dance arose in the early twentieth century against ballet's strict technique, prizing freer movement and personal expression, and later spawned contemporary, hip-hop, and postmodern styles.

**Music** organises timbre, pitch, rhythm, and dynamics into structured sound across genres such as classical, jazz, folk, rock, and hip-hop. Jazz fuses written parts with improvisation, while classical Western concert music tends to follow the score. Performers adjust timing and expression in response to the audience, and live concerts create a sense of togetherness that recorded listening rarely matches.

**Film** grew out of Edison's kinetoscope in the 1890s and became a mass medium through the Hollywood studio system. Camera acting is a distinct craft: the lens magnifies, the final performance is shaped in the edit, and subtlety matters more than the projection needed on stage.

**Opera** tells a drama almost entirely through singing with orchestral accompaniment, merging music, drama, and spectacle. Born in late sixteenth-century Italy, it became the prestige entertainment of European courts and then the middle class. Wagner's *Gesamtkunstwerk* ("total artwork"), uniting dance, music, and poetry, deeply shaped the modern stage.

**Other forms** include circus (acrobatics, clowning, aerials, object manipulation), puppetry, mime, magic, performance art, improv, and stand-up. Contemporary circus, exemplified by Cirque du Soleil, weaves narrative around traditional skills.

## How the tradition developed

The earliest known play text, the *Dramatic Ramesseum Papyrus*, dates to Egypt around the 20th century BCE; Chinese drama began during the Shang dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BCE). In fifth-century BCE Athens, Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides forged tragedy, and Aristophanes built comedy. India's *Natya Shastra*, a Sanskrit treatise on dramaturgy attributed to Bharata and possibly compiled 200 BCE–200 CE, still influences classical Indian dance and theatre, alongside playwrights such as Kalidasa and Bhavabhuti. Kerala's Koodiyattam is among the oldest surviving theatrical forms (UNESCO Masterpiece, 2001).

In medieval Europe, the Christian Church used mystery plays to dramatise Bible stories, and travelling jongleurs provided secular entertainment. In West Africa, griots, hereditary musicians, preserved oral history, genealogies, and praise songs. The Islamic world developed *ta'ziyeh* shadow puppetry, a Shi'a ritual drama about the death of Husayn ibn Ali.

The fifteenth-century Italian Renaissance revived classical forms, and Domenico da Piacenza is credited with coining the term *ballo*, ancestor of *ballet*. The first ballet recognised as such, the *Ballet Comique de la Reine* (1581), was choreographed by Balthasar de Beaujoyeulx. *Commedia dell'arte* spread across Europe, and Shakespeare's England built a working theatre industry. The first opera, *Dafne*, was performed in 1597.

The Romantic era produced grand opera (Meyerbeer, Verdi), Wagner's total artwork, and pointe work in Romantic ballet, culminating in *Giselle* (1841). Diaghilev's Ballets Russes (1909–1929) fused choreography, music, and visual design and remade twentieth-century ballet. Stanislavski's "System" introduced psychological realism, asking actors to find truthful inner motivation. Postmodernism in the 1970s–1980s blurred boundaries between dance, theatre, and visual art, and COVID-19 lockdowns in 2020 pushed the industry into streaming and hybrid formats, prompting ongoing debate about whether digital substitutes can sustain live performance.

## Traditions outside Europe

Africa hosts more than 3,000 ethnic groups, nearly all with traditions that fuse dance, song, music, and mime, usually built on call-and-response between performer and audience. Iran has naqqali storytelling, *ta'ziyeh* ritual drama, and folk comedic and puppet forms; naqqali was once performed in coffeehouses, drawing on the *Shahnameh*. India's classical dance canon includes Bharatanatyam, Kathak, Odissi, Kathakali, and others, while Bollywood synthesises dance, music, and theatre into one of the world's most influential entertainment industries. China's Yuan dynasty developed a four- or five-act dramatic structure that fed regional styles, most famously Beijing Opera. Thailand's *Ramakien*, drawn from the Indian *Ramayana*, is a major theatrical tradition. Japan's three great classical theatres, Noh (fourteenth century, combining masks, chanting, and stylised movement), Bunraku puppetry, and Kabuki (both seventeenth century), are all UNESCO-recognised. In the Americas, Aztec and Maya ritual calendars structured ceremonial dance, and Northwest Coast peoples such as the Haida, Tlingit, and Kwakwaka'wakw developed mask carving, storytelling, and potlatch ceremonies with masks that transform between human and animal forms.

## Industry and technology

Popular music runs on commercial models; orchestras, opera houses, and ballet companies depend on nonprofit funding and state subsidies. Performers train in conservatories focused on vocational craft, or in universities that pair practice with academic study. Digital tools, especially motion capture, computer-driven sound, and online streaming, have expanded creation and distribution, though uneven internet access and short home-viewing attention remain real limits. Live performance survives because performer and audience need each other: the art is the shared moment, not the sounds and images that record it.
