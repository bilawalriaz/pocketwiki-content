# Cyrillic script

The Cyrillic script is a writing system created in the 890s at the Preslav Literary School in the First Bulgarian Empire by disciples of the Byzantine missionaries Cyril and Methodius. It adapts the Greek uncial alphabet, supplemented with characters from the older Glagolitic script that Cyril and Methodius had invented in the 860s, to represent Slavic sounds that Greek lacked. The script is named for Saint Cyril as an honour, not as authorship: Cyril and Methodius created Glagolitic, and their Bulgarian followers later produced Cyrillic. Over the following millennium Cyrillic was reformed for secular use and adapted for over fifty languages across Eurasia. By 2019 roughly 250 million people used it as an official national script, and Bulgaria's 2007 accession to the European Union made Cyrillic the EU's third official script after Latin and Greek.

## Origins and early design

The First Bulgarian Empire under Tsar Simeon I (893–927) sponsored the Preslav Literary School, where Naum of Preslav, Constantine of Preslav, Joan Ekzarh, and Chernorizets Hrabar shaped the new alphabet. They took Greek uncial letterforms as a base and added ligatures and consonants from Glagolitic to represent sounds such as *š*, *č*, *ž*, and *jat’*. The rival Ohrid school kept using Glagolitic; Preslav scholars preferred Greek models. The earliest datable Cyrillic inscriptions survive at Preslav, Patleina Monastery, Ravna Monastery, and Varna Monastery, in modern Shumen and Varna provinces. By the 12th century Cyrillic had displaced Glagolitic as the standard script for Church Slavonic, the literary language of the Orthodox Slavs.

## Spread and variants

Old Church Slavonic literature spread north from Bulgaria, making Cyrillic the script of religious and administrative writing across the Balkans and Eastern Europe. It was also used for non-Slavic languages, including Romanian until the 1860s, and for Catholic and Muslim Slavs. A distinct Bosnian variant, *poljičica*, is attested from the 10th or 11th century on the Humac tablet and survived until the 18th century. In the 14th and 15th centuries the Tarnovo Literary School, led by Patriarch Evtimiy of Tarnovo, Gregory Tsamblak, and Constantine of Kostenets, carried out orthographic reforms whose influence on Russian, Serbian, Wallachian, and Moldavian writing is known in Russia as the *second South-Slavic influence*.

## Major reforms

In 1708–1710 Tsar Peter the Great introduced a *Civil script* (*гражданский шрифт*) in Russia. Letterforms were westernised to resemble Latin type, archaic letters were abolished, new letters were designed, and the upper-case/lower-case distinction was introduced. The older *poluustav* bookhand continued in Church Slavonic and is still used for archaic or decorative effect.

In the early 19th century the Serbian reformer Vuk Stefanović Karadžić removed graphemes for sounds no longer pronounced in the vernacular and added five new letters, Љ, Њ, Ђ, Ћ, and Џ, to represent Serbian sounds, pulling the alphabet away from Church Slavonic and toward a sound-based principle.

In the Soviet period Cyrillic was imposed on many minority languages across the Caucasus, Central Asia, and Siberia. After the USSR dissolved in 1991, Azerbaijan, Turkmenistan, and most of Moldova switched to Latin; Uzbekistan uses both scripts; Kazakhstan completed its own transition to Latin in 2025. Russia requires Cyrillic for all federal public communication, a policy contested by groups such as Chechen separatists who use Latin.

## Letterforms and typography

Early Cyrillic manuscripts did not distinguish upper-case from lower-case, and letters doubled as numerals with values inherited from Greek. Ligatures were common: the letter *Yeri* (Ы) originated as a joining of Ъ and І, while *iotation* used ligatures such as Ꙗ, Ѥ, Ю, Ѩ, and Ѭ. Ornamental *vyaz’* used tall, narrow, interlocked letters and moved directly from a medieval style to late Baroque, skipping a Renaissance phase. Peter's Civil script then imposed Latinate proportions. In modern Cyrillic italics, called *kursiv* from German *Kursive*, some letters diverge sharply from their upright, or *pryamoy shrift*, forms: ⟨т⟩ can look like a small-capital T, and ⟨д⟩ can resemble Latin ⟨g⟩. Serbian, Macedonian, and Bulgarian standards allow italic shapes that reflect handwriting and rounded arcs.

## Coverage and currency

Cyrillic is the standard script for the Slavic languages Belarusian, Bulgarian, Macedonian, Pannonian Rusyn, Russian, Rusyn, Serbo-Croatian (Bosnian, Montenegrin, Serbian), and Ukrainian. It is also used for non-Slavic languages of the former Soviet sphere, including Tatar, Chechen, and Chuvash in Russia; Abkhaz and Ossetian in the Caucasus; and Kazakh, Kyrgyz, and Mongolian in Central Asia. The first Cyrillic-derived alphabet for a non-Slavic language was Abur for Komi, followed by the Molodtsov alphabet and many Caucasian systems.

Several currency signs derive from Cyrillic: the Ukrainian hryvnia (₴) from cursive *he*, the Russian ruble (₽) from *Р*, the Kyrgyzstani som from *С*, the Kazakhstani tenge (₸) from *Т*, and the Mongolian tögrög (₮) also from *Т*.

## Encoding and transliteration

Unicode 17.0 distributes Cyrillic across seven blocks, headed by Cyrillic at U+0400–U+04FF, with Supplement, Extended-A through Extended-D, and two phonetic and combining ranges. Precomposed accented letters appear mainly where they are distinct alphabet letters, such as Й, Ў, Ё, Ї, Ѓ, and Ќ, or where they distinguish homonyms such as Ѐ and Ѝ in Bulgarian and Macedonian. Legacy 8-bit encodings (Windows-1251, KOI8-R, KOI8-U, CP866, ISO/IEC 8859-5, MIK) remain in use.

Transliteration systems convert Cyrillic to Latin: a Scientific system based on Serbo-Croatian, per-language UN recommendations, ISO 9:1995, ALA-LC for North American libraries, BGN/PCGN for geographic names, and GOST 7.79-2000. The reverse process, writing a non-Cyrillic system with Cyrillic letters, is *cyrillization*.
