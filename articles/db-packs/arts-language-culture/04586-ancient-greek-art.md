# Ancient Greek art

Ancient Greek art spans roughly a thousand years, from the Iron Age around 1000 BC to the Roman conquest of Greece in 146 BC. Its central achievement was naturalistic yet idealized depictions of the human body, especially the nude male, which became a foundation for Western art. The tradition is conventionally divided into four overlapping periods: Geometric (c. 1000–750 BC), Archaic (c. 750–500 BC), Classical (c. 500–323 BC), and Hellenistic (323–146 BC). Stylistic change was gradual and uneven across the Greek world, so these dates mark rough shifts in taste rather than clean breaks.

Geometric art used abstract, repetitive patterns on pottery. The Archaic period introduced monumental stone sculpture, the kouros (a standing nude male youth) and the kore (a standing draped female), often with a slight upturned mouth known as the "archaic smile." These figures drew on Egyptian models for their stiff, frontal poses. The Classical period brought naturalistic movement, balanced proportions, and the beginnings of individual portraiture, visible in works like the Parthenon Marbles. The Hellenistic period, beginning after Alexander the Great's reign (336–323 BC), spread Greek style across Eurasia and produced more emotional, diverse, and technically virtuosic work, including multi-figure compositions and scenes of everyday life.

Pottery is the most abundantly surviving art form, with over 100,000 complete vessels known. It served daily life, trade, and burial. Two figural techniques dominated. In black-figure, painters coated the red clay with black slip to form figures, then incised details. Around 530 BC, Athenian workshops reversed the process in the red-figure technique, leaving figures in the red of the clay against a black background, which allowed finer drawing of anatomy and drapery. Corinth pioneered large-scale pottery production, but by the 5th century BC Athens dominated Mediterranean export, and vase painting declined as a major art form as production industrialised.

Sculpture centred on the human form, treated as both sacred and secular. Archaic kouroi and korai stand stiff and frontal; Classical sculpture introduced contrapposto, a weight-shifted stance that made figures look relaxed and alive. Hellenistic sculptors pushed expression further, adding dramatic gestures, deep emotion, and everyday subjects. Materials included marble, bronze (prized in antiquity but rarely surviving), and chryselephantine, a luxury technique layering gold and ivory over a wooden frame for cult statues. Nearly all sculpture was originally painted in bright colours (polychromy), traces of which survive on some pieces.

Architecture was primarily stone-built on a post-and-lintel system, meaning upright columns supporting horizontal beams. Three column styles, or orders, defined the vocabulary: Doric, austere and plain; Ionic, slimmer with scrolled capitals; and Corinthian, the most ornate, with acanthus-leaf capitals. Temples of limestone or marble survive best, though stoas (covered walkways), theatres, and agoras (public squares) were equally central to civic life. Decorative motifs such as the meander (a Greek-key border) and the palmette recurred across architecture, pottery, and other arts, giving the tradition a shared visual language.

Panel and wall painting were highly regarded but almost entirely lost; knowledge comes from literary descriptions, Roman copies, and the evidence of vase painting. Techniques included encaustic, using heated wax as a binder, and tempera. Mosaics spread in the Hellenistic period, first from natural pebbles and later from cut tesserae (small uniform cubes of stone or glass). Fine metalwork and engraved gems survive mainly from the edges of the Greek world.

Some questions remain open. Pottery's social role is debated, since many complete vessels come from graves, leaving open whether most production served the living or the dead. The relationship between vase painting and other arts is contested; some scholars argue vases imitated lost metalwork rather than leading the visual culture. The continuity between Bronze Age Minoan and Mycenaean painting and later Greek traditions is unclear, and whether Hellenistic sculpture developed a distinct Alexandrian style is still questioned. Most surviving sculpture looked nothing like the white marble we picture today, because it was once vividly painted.
