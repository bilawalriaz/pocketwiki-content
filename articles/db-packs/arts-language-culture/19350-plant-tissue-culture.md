# Plant tissue culture

Plant tissue culture is a collection of techniques used to maintain or grow plant cells, tissues, or organs under sterile conditions on a nutrient medium of known composition. It exploits **totipotency**: many plant cells retain the capacity to divide and differentiate into every specialised cell type, so a single cell, a wall-less cell (a protoplast), or a small piece of leaf, stem, or root can regenerate a whole plant given the right nutrients and **plant hormones** (chemical regulators of growth).

## What it does

The main use is mass-producing clones, genetically identical copies of a parent plant, through a process called **micropropagation**. Practical advantages over traditional propagation include:

- Producing exact copies of plants with desirable flowers, fruits, or traits.
- Generating mature plants quickly and in large numbers inside a small space.
- Multiplying plants that rarely set seed or that lack pollinators.
- Regenerating whole plants from genetically modified cells.
- Raising plants in sterile containers, which sharply reduces the spread of diseases, pests, and pathogens during transport.
- Propagating seeds that germinate poorly, such as orchids and *Nepenthes*.
- Cleaning virus-infected stock (such as sugarcane and potatoes) and multiplying the cleaned plants.
- Reproducing recalcitrant species needed for land restoration.
- Storing genetic material as a safeguard for native species.

Because cultured plants are clones, any susceptibility of the mother plant to a pathogen or environmental stress is inherited by the whole crop; beneficial traits are preserved in the same way.

## Starting a culture

Plant surfaces naturally carry microorganisms, so preparation must be aseptic. Work is performed in a **laminar flow cabinet** supplied with HEPA-filtered air. Tissue samples, called **explants**, are first surface-sterilised, typically by brief immersion in alcohol followed by sodium or calcium hypochlorite, then placed on a sterile solid medium or into a sterile liquid medium (used for cell suspension cultures).

Solid and liquid media share a base of inorganic salts plus a few organic nutrients, vitamins, and plant hormones. Solid media are made by adding a gelling agent, usually purified agar.

## How hormones shape the outcome

The two most important hormone classes are **auxins**, which promote root formation, and **cytokinins**, which promote shoot formation. Their relative balance steers development:

| Hormone balance | Typical outcome |
|---|---|
| Excess auxin | Root proliferation |
| Excess cytokinin | Shoot proliferation |
| Auxin equals cytokinin | Unorganised mass of cells, a **callus** |

Medium composition, especially hormones and the nitrogen source, has profound effects on the morphology of the tissue that grows from the initial explant. As a culture grows, pieces are sliced off and transferred to fresh medium (subcultured) to keep growth going or to redirect morphology. Skill matters in judging which pieces to keep. Shoots that emerge can be excised and rooted with auxin to produce plantlets, which are then transferred to potting soil and grown as ordinary plants.

## Regeneration pathways

Three pathways are common:

1. **Shoot or nodal culture.** Propagation from pre-existing **meristems** (regions of actively dividing cells such as stem tips, axillary bud tips, and root tips). Because the cells are already organised, the risk of **somaclonal variation** (unwanted genetic change in culture) is minimal, so this is the preferred method for commercial micropropagation.
2. **Organogenesis.** Formation of adventitious organs or axillary buds directly or indirectly from explant tissue. Useful for studying the regulation of plant development.
3. **Non-zygotic embryogenesis.** Formation of embryos that resemble zygotic (seed) embryos but develop from somatic cells, often single cells. Because they originate from one cell, they are favoured for micropropagation, ploidy manipulation, gene transfer, and synthetic seed production. They can also give higher multiplication rates, especially in liquid **bioreactors**.

Regeneration efficiency is usually a quantitative trait, varying between species and between cultivars or ecotypes within a species, so a single protocol rarely works across genotypes.

## Choice of explant

Explants can come from shoots, leaves, stems, flowers, roots, or undifferentiated cells, provided the cells retain living cytoplasm and a nucleus and can de-differentiate and resume division. Explants from different organs often grow and regenerate at different rates; some will not respond at all. The explant also determines whether the resulting plantlets are haploid or diploid and influences the risk of microbial contamination.

Two practical obstacles shape explant choice. Root tips are hard to isolate and carry soil microflora, including organisms that live inside the root or bind tightly to soil particles; these microbes tend to overrun the medium before the plant tissue grows. Aerial parts also carry microflora, but these are easier to rinse off and kill by surface sterilisation. Mosaic patterns, discolouration, or localised necrosis on an explant signal a tight microbial association that surface sterilisation is unlikely to remove.

An alternative is to surface-sterilise seeds and germinate them aseptically; the seed coat tolerates harsher sterilants (such as hypochlorite) than soft vegetative tissue, so the resulting seedlings give cleaner explants.

If a culture grows slowly or suffers necrosis, the usual responses are to optimise the medium or to switch to a more responsive variety or tissue type.

## Applications beyond micropropagation

Tissue culture is used in plant science, forestry, and horticulture to:

- Mass-produce potted, landscape, and florist plants through meristem and shoot culture.
- Conserve rare or endangered species.
- Screen cultured cells for traits such as herbicide resistance and to detect chimeras early.
- Grow plant cells in **bioreactors** for valuable secondary metabolites (such as caffeine from *Coffea arabica*, nicotine from *Nicotiana rustica*, or phenolic acids from *Echinacea purpurea*) and recombinant biopharmaceuticals.
- Cross distantly related species by fusing protoplasts and regenerating the hybrid, or by rescuing embryos that would otherwise abort (embryo rescue).
- Double chromosomes using antimitotic agents such as colchicine or oryzalin to produce doubled haploids, tetraploids, and other polyploids.
- Regenerate transgenic plants from transformed tissue.
- Produce clean material from virus-infected stock, as in sugarcane, potatoes, and many soft fruits.
- Produce large numbers of identical sterile hybrids.
- Make artificial seeds through somatic embryogenesis.

## Worked examples

- **Sugarcane 'Ono'** is a somaclonal variant resistant to Fiji disease.
- **Citronella java 'Bio-13'** yields 37% more essential oil.
- **'Kaveri Vaman'** is a tissue-cultured banana mutant that tolerates heavy rains.
- In bamboo species whose single flowering event can be separated from germination by more than 50 years, tissue culture can be used to induce flowering on a useful timescale.

The medium most often used to support these cultures is the **Murashige and Skoog** formulation, a defined salt mixture.
