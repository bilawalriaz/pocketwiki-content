# Evolutionary psychology of religion

Evolutionary psychology of religion applies the logic of natural selection to religious belief, asking why minds that produce gods, rituals, and group worship spread at all. Because the brain is an organ with a genetic basis, the same selective pressures that shaped other cognitive functions also shaped the machinery underlying religion. The field divides into competing accounts, with a third position holding that different features of religion need different explanations.

## The central debate

Most researchers accept that a capacity for religious behaviour evolved early in human history. The **adaptationist** school argues religion itself was selected for because it raised group survival and reproduction. The **by-product** school argues religious belief emerged as a side effect of other adaptive cognitive traits and was never directly selected.

Religious behaviour is paradoxically costly: economic sacrifice, celibacy, dangerous rituals, and time spent otherwise. Natural selection should act against such behaviour unless religion delivered offsetting benefits, which is what each school tries to specify.

## Religion as adaptation

If religion is an adaptation, its main payoff is social. Group membership provides coordination advantages and allows humans to solve the free-rider problem without relying on kinship. Richard Sosis tracked 200 nineteenth-century US communes: 39% of religious communes survived 20 years, versus 6% of secular ones, and the number of costly sacrifices demanded by a religious commune predicted its longevity. Jonathan Haidt later cited this as the best evidence that religion is an adaptive solution to cooperation among non-kin.

Several mechanisms extend the argument:

- **Costly signalling**: painful or dangerous rituals are hard to fake, making commitment to a group publicly credible. Sosis, Kress, and Boster showed cross-culturally that men in warring societies submit to the costliest rituals.
- **Sacralised norms**: anthropologist Roy Rappaport argued that rituals and laws are more effective when treated as sacred.
- **Social selection**: Randolph Nesse and Mary Jane West-Eberhard argue that altruistic individuals were preferentially chosen as partners, gradually making humans an unusually cooperative species.
- **Eusociality**: Edward O. Wilson proposes that humans, ants, and bees replicate genes through group-level competition, and religion emerged from interplay between group and individual selection in large-brained humans.

A contested literature claims direct health benefits. Koenig and Cohen reviewed 100 studies and reported 79% showed a positive effect of religion on well-being. Critics, including Richard Sloan of Columbia, note that even if the correlation holds, less complex mechanisms than religion could deliver the same placebo-like benefits.

## Religion as by-product

A second tradition argues that religious belief is not itself adaptive but rides on cognitive systems that were.

Stephen Jay Gould treated religion as an exaptation (a "spandrel"), a trait shaped by features evolved for other reasons, including the large human brain whose consciousness forced early humans to confront personal mortality. Several specific cognitive mechanisms have been proposed:

- **Agent detection**: inferring organisms, often threatening, behind ambiguous stimuli. The system is biased toward false positives because mistaking a vine for a snake is cheaper than missing a real predator.
- **Causal reasoning and theory of mind** allow humans to imagine purposeful agents behind unexplained events such as thunder, illness, or planetary movement.
- **Minimally counter-intuitive concepts**: Pascal Boyer argues religious ideas are memorable because they slightly violate ordinary expectations. A god who is mostly human-like but vastly more powerful sticks in memory, while a purely abstract theological god does not. Experiments show even religious believers mentally represent their god in anthropomorphic terms.
- **Hazard-precaution system**: Pierre Lienard and Pascal Boyer propose that humans evolved a system for detecting environmental threats. Ritual features such as the prevention of danger, harm from non-performance, and detailed prescriptions trigger this system and channel private anxiety into coordinated group action.
- **Hyperactive Agency Detection Device (HADD)**: Justin Barrett argues that belief in a supreme being is natural because it uses mental tools shared by all humans, and that an over-sensitive agency detector would tend to generate belief in ghosts and spirits.
- **Terror Management Theory**: awareness of death was a disruptive by-product of self-awareness, so any successful culture had to manage that terror. Religion did so by providing self-esteem, symbolic immortality through cultural legacy, and literal immortality through belief in an afterlife.

## From belief to organised religion

Richard Dawkins proposed in *The Selfish Gene* (1976) that cultural units called memes evolve like genes, and argued in *The God Delusion* (2006) that religions spread as "mind viruses" because they discourage questioning.

A complementary account traces how supernatural concepts grow into organised religion. Ideas about invisible agents cross-connect with intuitive moral feelings. The persistent mental presence of the dead creates cognitive tension that the mind resolves by treating the deceased as still present and in need of negotiation, motivating ritual. As groups grow, certain individuals acquire specialist status as ritual performers. Competition between groups then selects for more abstract, broadly acceptable versions of these practices, and the specialists eventually consolidate into a guild with its own political goals: what is recognisably a religion.
