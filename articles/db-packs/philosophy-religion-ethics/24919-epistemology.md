# Epistemology

Epistemology is the branch of philosophy that studies the nature, sources, and limits of knowledge. The term was coined in the 19th century from the Greek *episteme* (knowledge) and *logos* (study). Epistemology asks what knowledge is, how humans acquire it, and how far it can extend. Unlike psychology, which describes how people actually form beliefs, epistemology is normative: it evaluates which beliefs people ought to hold.

## What knowledge is

Epistemologists follow the **traditional analysis** of propositional knowledge (knowledge-that, expressed as "Ravi knows that kangaroos hop") as justified true belief. In 1963, Edmund Gettier published short counterexamples showing that a justified true belief can still fail to be knowledge. In the fake-barn case, a driver forms a justified true belief that the building they stop beside is a real barn, unaware that the area is full of facades. The belief is justified and true, but rests on luck rather than a real epistemic connection to the fact, so most epistemologists deny that it counts as knowledge. Later theories try to repair the definition: the causal theory requires that the fact cause the belief; reliabilism requires that the belief be produced by a reliable process; sensitivity requires that the believer would not still hold the belief if it were false; defeasibility requires that the justification cannot be undermined. Timothy Williamson has argued that knowledge is a primitive mental state that cannot be analysed into components at all.

Knowledge comes in several other forms. Bertrand Russell distinguished **knowledge-how**, a practical skill like knowing how to ride a bike, and **knowledge by acquaintance**, direct familiarity like knowing a person or a taste. A separate split divides **a posteriori** knowledge (empirical, e.g. "the sun is shining") from **a priori** knowledge (independent of experience, e.g. "2 + 2 = 4"). The related analytic/synthetic distinction says a sentence is analytically true if its truth depends only on word meanings ("all bachelors are unmarried") and synthetically true if it also depends on facts about the world ("snow is white"). Quine attacked this distinction, arguing that no sentence is purely analytic.

## The three ingredients: belief, truth, and justification

A belief is a mental state that affirms a proposition. It is true if the proposition matches reality, which is the correspondence theory of truth, or, on the rival coherence theory, if it fits coherently into a system of beliefs. Justification is what separates well-founded belief from superstition: a belief is justified when the believer has adequate evidence or reasons for it. Justification can be propositional (good reasons are available) or doxastic (the believer actually holds the belief on the basis of those reasons). The five standard sources of justification are perception, introspection, memory, reason, and testimony, though philosophers dispute how much weight each deserves.

## Major debates

**Skepticism** holds that knowledge is unattainable in some or all domains. Global skeptics argue no knowledge exists at all; more moderate skeptics target specific areas such as knowledge of the external world or of other minds. The *dream argument* points out that while dreaming one cannot tell one is dreaming, casting doubt on any perceptual claim. Descartes used *methodological doubt*, systematic doubting of every belief, to seek indubitable foundations, finding certainty only in "I think, therefore I am." **Fallibilism** accepts the skeptic's point that certainty is impossible but denies that knowledge requires certainty: well-supported scientific theories still count as knowledge.

**Empiricism** (Locke, Hume) holds that all knowledge derives from sense experience; the mind begins as a *tabula rasa*, or blank slate. **Rationalism** (Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz) holds that some knowledge, especially of logic and mathematics, is accessible through reason alone, via innate ideas or rational intuition. Kant identified a form of knowledge overlooked by Hume: synthetic a priori principles, such as spatial and temporal relations, that structure all experience without depending on it.

**Foundationalism** says some basic beliefs are justified directly, without resting on other beliefs, and non-basic beliefs derive their justification from that foundation. This faces *Agrippa's trilemma*: justification must stop somewhere, go in a circle, or stretch to infinity. **Coherentism** rejects the basic-belief split, holding that a belief is justified when it coheres with the rest of one's belief system, like a link in an interconnected web. **Infinitism**, a minority view, accepts that no belief is basic and argues that beliefs can be supported by an infinite, non-circular chain of further beliefs.

**Internalism** says justification depends only on factors accessible to the believer through reflection. **Evidentialism**, the leading internalist view, requires that a justified belief rest on adequate evidence the believer possesses. **Externalism** denies that justification is purely mental, pointing out that reliable eyesight, a reliable environment, and a reliable belief-forming process all matter. Reliabilism, a leading externalist theory, requires that the belief-forming process reliably produces true beliefs. Virtue epistemology, another externalist approach, says a belief is justified when it results from intellectual virtues such as open-mindedness and good perceptual faculties.

## Knowledge in context

Specialised branches study knowledge in particular domains. The epistemology of science asks how individual observations can justify universal laws, the problem of induction. The epistemology of mathematics investigates whether proofs alone justify mathematical knowledge. The epistemology of perception debates direct versus indirect realism, whether we perceive objects directly or through internal sense-data, a distinction crucial for explaining illusions. Social epistemology focuses on how knowledge is produced and shared in groups, while feminist, postmodern, and decolonial approaches critique whose knowledge counts and how Western frameworks shape what is taken to be known.

The history of epistemology runs from Plato's distinction between knowledge and true opinion, through Aristotle's empiricism, the Indian *pramāṇa* tradition examining sources of valid knowledge, medieval debates over reason and faith, the modern rationalist–empiricist split, and 20th-century developments including Gettier's challenge to the traditional analysis, Quine's naturalized epistemology, and Goldman's reliabilism.
