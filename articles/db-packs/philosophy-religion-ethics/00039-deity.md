# Deity

A deity is a supernatural being believed to hold authority over some aspect of the universe or life, generally considered sacred and worthy of worship. Belief in at least one deity who may interact with the world is theism. The concept varies widely, from an infinite transcendent creator to a finite being of special significance, and no universally accepted definition exists.

## Etymology

"Deity" derives from Old French *deité* and Latin *deitatem* ("divine nature"), coined by Augustine of Hippo from *deus* ("god"). *Deus* traces to Proto-Indo-European (PIE) *deiwos*, yielding Sanskrit *Deva* ("shining one"), Greek *dios* and *Zeus*, and Latin *deus*. "God" comes from Proto-Germanic *guthan*, from PIE *ghut-* ("that which is invoked"), originally neuter but becoming masculine under Christian influence.

## Classifications of Belief

- **Polytheism**: worship of multiple deities in a pantheon.
- **Henotheism**: one supreme deity accepted without denying others, viewed as aspects of one divine principle.
- **Monolatry**: many deities exist, but only one may be worshipped.
- **Monotheism**: one deity exists, often described as omnipotent, omnipresent, omniscient, omnibenevolent, and eternal, though none is essential.
- **Deism**: a non-intervening creator. **Pantheism** equates the universe with God; **pandeism** proposes a creator who became the universe; **panentheism** holds divinity pervades yet transcends the universe.
- **Agnosticism** and **atheism** reject knowledge of or belief in any deity.

## Prehistoric Evidence

Scholars infer probable prehistoric deities from inscriptions and cave drawings, but figurines cannot be conclusively identified as deities; the earlier practice of interpreting nearly every female figurine as a primordial goddess has been discredited. Probable representations have been found at 'Ain Ghazal and Çatalhöyük.

## Ancient Near Eastern Religions

Egyptian sources name more than 1,400 deities; most represented natural or social forces. Major figures included Min, Neith, Anubis, Horus, Isis, Ra, and Osiris. Levantine polytheism featured chief god El, his consort Asherah and their seventy sons, the storm god Baal, Anat, and Astarte. Israelites and Judahites originally worshipped these alongside Yahweh; Josiah's 621 BCE reforms enforced monolatry, and during the Babylonian captivity some taught that other deities did not exist. In Mesopotamia the Anunnaki were the principal gods; after Sargon of Akkad's conquest, deities were syncretized, Inanna became Ishtar, and Marduk rose to creator of heaven, earth, and humankind.

## Indo-European Religions

In Norse mythology the Æsir were the principal gods and the Vanir were associated with fertility; the Æsir–Vanir War ended in truce. Worship continued in Scandinavia into the 12th century. Greek religion was polytheistic with no centralized church; the Twelve Olympians included Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Athena, Apollo, Artemis, Aphrodite, Hermes, Demeter, Dionysus, Hephaestus, and Ares. Walter Burkert described Greek deities as "persons, not abstractions," with unique expertise and flawed personalities. The Roman pantheon combined Greek-syncretized deities with figures like Janus and Fortuna, and remained revered past the 313 CE Edict of Toleration.

## Native American and Polynesian Religions

Inca Viracocha was a pre-creation abstract deity, while Inti (sun) and Mama Qucha (sea) ensured prosperity. Maya Kukulkan was honored at spring-equinox step pyramids, and Aztec Quetzalcoatl resembled him; Aztec concepts remain poorly understood because records were written by Christian missionaries. The Māori supreme being Io was the uncreated creator; Polynesian theology also featured Tangaloa, Tu-Matauenga, and Rangi.

## Abrahamic Religions

Christianity's mainstream accepts the Holy Trinity, three cosubstantial persons (Father, Son, and Holy Spirit). Origen first described them as *homooúsios*; Chalcedon declared Christ unites "fullness of deity and fullness of humanity." Islam is strictly monotheistic; the shahada affirms Allah as the sole deity. Judaism affirms one God (Yahweh), revealed through the Exodus, though modern scholarship notes idolatry resurfaced multiple times. Mandaeism venerates Hayyi Rabbi, with Yushamin as the "Second Life."

## Asian Religions

Hinduism spans henotheism through monism, with *Deva* and *Devi* as god and goddess. Jainism rejects a creator God but includes *devas*. Buddhism has no creator deity, yet devas are essential to cosmology and saṃsāra; rebirth in their heavenly realm results from ethical living and good karma. Shinto venerates *kami*, which scholars find "quite unsatisfactory" to translate as "god" or "spirit"; kami are said to number eight million, signifying infinity. Taoism divides deities into "gods" and *xian* immortals. Zoroastrianism's Ahura Mazda is the sole God, opposed by the nearly equal evil twin Angra Mainyu, and became fully aniconic by the end of the Sasanian Empire.

## Skeptical Interpretations

Democritus argued deities arose from natural phenomena; Euhemerus held that gods were deified mortal kings. Freud proposed God-concepts project the father, and psychologists note an evolved overactive agency-detection system. Durkheim viewed gods as extensions of social life; Matt Rossano suggests God-concepts enforce morality and build cooperative communities.
