# Hard problem of consciousness

The hard problem of consciousness is the question of why physical processes in the brain are accompanied by subjective experience at all. Philosopher David Chalmers coined the phrase at a 1994 Tucson conference and developed it in his 1995 paper "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" and 1996 book *The Conscious Mind*. The problem rests on a contrast. The "easy problems" concern discrimination, perception, verbal report, and motor control, all open to functional and mechanistic explanation in the way a clock's timekeeping follows from its clockwork. Once every such fact is in hand, Chalmers argues, a further question remains: why is the performance of these functions accompanied by experience?

Consciousness, here, is phenomenal consciousness, the felt character of pain, the taste of coffee, the redness of red. Thomas Nagel calls this "what it is like" to be a conscious creature; Chalmers uses Nagel's definition and treats consciousness as synonymous with experience.

Chalmers argues the hard problem is irreducible to the easy problems because experience is not logically entailed by physical facts. A perfect physical duplicate of a conscious being could, without contradiction, lack experience entirely, a "philosophical zombie." Or it could have an inverted visible spectrum, where red looks the way green looks to us, while behaving and processing identically. Knowing everything about H₂O is knowing everything about water, because water is nothing more than H₂O. But knowing everything about the brain is not knowing everything about consciousness: the felt character is left out. In 1983, Joseph Levine named this the "explanatory gap," the sense that the link between physical and experiential descriptions is contingent rather than necessary.

The hard problem threatens physicalism, the doctrine that everything is physical and so in principle physically explainable. If physical facts do not entail experiential facts, physicalism fails to be complete. Chalmers, though he rejects physicalism, remains a naturalist and treats consciousness as a fundamental feature of reality. Other responses: accept the problem and pursue a non-reductive theory, deny it is real, or hold that human cognition may be permanently closed to a solution.

Philosophers divide by their response. Type-A materialists, including Daniel Dennett, Patricia Churchland, Keith Frankish, and Thomas Metzinger, hold the hard problem does not exist. For them, phenomenal consciousness is either fully captured by functional accounts (strong reductionism) or does not exist at all (eliminativism, or illusionism in Frankish's term). Dennett compares the apparent mystery of consciousness to stage magic, whose trick dissolves under analysis. Type-B materialists, including Joseph Levine, accept that zombie arguments reveal a real gap, but treat it as a gap in our concepts, not in nature. Type-C materialists grant an explanatory gap and trust science to close it. Dualists (in the modern "property" sense) hold that consciousness is a non-physical property of the physical. Panpsychists, defended recently by Philip Goff and Galen Strawson, place experience in every fundamental constituent, avoiding emergence. Cosmopsychists place it in the universe as a whole; idealists make mind fundamental. Colin McGinn's new mysterianism argues that human minds are cognitively closed, in Fodor's sense of modular limits, to the relation between brain and experience, so the problem may be unsolvable by creatures like us. Steven Pinker places his bet on this weaker form.

The 2020 PhilPapers survey found 62.4% of philosophers accept the hard problem as real and 29.7% reject it. The split tracks whether thought experiments like zombies, Nagel's bats, and Mary's Room, a scientist who knows all physical facts about colour vision but learns something new on seeing red for the first time, expose a genuine metaphysical gap or merely trade on bad intuitions about what consciousness must be.
