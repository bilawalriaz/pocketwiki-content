# Philosophy of science

Philosophy of science studies the foundations, methods, and implications of science. It asks what separates science from non-science, how scientific theories earn trust, and what science is for. The field reasons about scientific practice and also studies how scientists actually work, drawing on cases from physics, biology, medicine, statistics, and the social sciences. Most of its central questions remain contested, including whether science can reveal truths about unobservable entities such as electrons, and whether inductive reasoning, drawing general conclusions from particular observations, can be justified at all.

## What counts as science

Karl Popper named this the *demarcation problem* and proposed *falsifiability*: a claim is scientific only if some possible observation could prove it false. Earlier logical positivists had tried to ground science in direct observation, treating unverifiable statements as meaningless. Popper's criterion has competitors, and no single account has won acceptance. Richard Feynman called work that mimics science's outward form while lacking honesty in self-testing *cargo cult science*. Pseudoscience is non-science that presents itself as science.

## What counts as a good explanation

Carl Hempel and Paul Oppenheim proposed the *deductive-nomological* model: a phenomenon is explained when it can be deduced from a scientific law. The model struggled with chance events and irreducible probabilities. Wesley Salmon responded by requiring explanations to be statistically relevant to what they explain. Others argued the key is unifying disparate phenomena or identifying a causal mechanism.

## Can induction be justified

David Hume showed that no number of past sunrises guarantees the next one. One reply accepts that induction yields probability, not certainty. Another treats science as *abduction*, inference to the best explanation, judged by criteria like simplicity. Nicholas Maxwell argued that *unity*, the way a theory ties together otherwise separate phenomena, matters more than simplicity, and that no theory-independent measure of simplicity exists.

## Are observations theory-free

No. The same image of the Einstein cross reads as five objects under ordinary optics and as two objects whose light has been gravitationally lensed under general relativity. Observations are *theory-laden*: perception and cognition together interpret raw sensory data through existing concepts, so what counts as seeing already presupposes a framework.

## What science is trying to do

*Scientific realists* hold that successful theories are approximately true, including about unobservables. *Anti-realists* argue science need not deliver literal truth, since its success can be explained without that commitment. *Instrumentalists* say truth is beside the point, since science aims to predict and to enable technology. Realists point to the predictive power of current theories; anti-realists point to the long history of successful but discarded theories.

## History

Plato and Aristotle distinguished exact from approximate reasoning and catalogued abductive, deductive, and inductive inference. The eleventh-century Arab polymath Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen) introduced controlled experimentation in optics, influencing Roger Bacon. Francis Bacon's *Novum Organum* (1620) argued for eliminating theories by experiment. René Descartes (1637) grounded knowledge in reason rather than experience. Newton's *Principia* (1713) declared that hypotheses have no place in experimental philosophy. Hume then attacked the rationality of causal and inductive inference, which Kant tried to rebut. Auguste Comte and John Stuart Mill developed influential nineteenth-century accounts of method.

Logical positivism dominated from the 1920s to the 1960s, with the Vienna and Berlin Circles as main centres, insisting only verifiable statements are meaningful. It failed to settle its own criterion and softened into *logical empiricism* led by Carl Hempel. Its two most influential successors were Popper, who replaced verification with falsifiability, and Thomas Kuhn.

## Kuhn and paradigms

In *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962), Kuhn argued that science does not accumulate steadily. Most work is *normal science*, puzzle-solving within a shared *paradigm*, the constellation of beliefs, methods, and exemplary achievements that defines a field. When enough anomalies pile up, a *paradigm shift* replaces the old framework with a new one that handles them better, as when Copernican astronomy supplanted Ptolemaic. Kuhn described the transition as social as well as rational, but explicitly rejected relativism.

## Coherentism and its critics

*Coherentism* holds that claims are justified as parts of a mutually supporting whole. The Duhem–Quine thesis sharpens this: no theory is tested in isolation, only together with background assumptions. When Newtonian theory failed to predict Uranus's orbit, scientists kept the law and revised the auxiliary assumption that the solar system had seven known planets, which led to the discovery of Neptune. Paul Feyerabend argued that no single method captures what scientists do, and that the only principle not obstructing progress is "anything goes."

## Particular sciences

General problems reappear with new force in specific domains. *Reduction* asks whether one science can be expressed in the terms of another, such as chemistry in physics. Philosophy of statistics recasts the problem of induction as a debate between frequentist methods, which report the probability of data given a null hypothesis, and *Bayesian inference*, which assigns probabilities to hypotheses themselves. Philosophy of medicine turns demarcation into a life-or-death question about which treatments to trust. Philosophies of biology, psychology, and the social sciences ask whether the study of human beings can be value-neutral, or whether it is inevitably shaped by the values of its practitioners.
