# Knowledge Graph (Google)

Google's Knowledge Graph is a private knowledge base that lets the search engine answer questions directly. Instead of returning only ten blue links, Google pulls a structured summary of the entity behind a query, a place, person, business, or object, and renders it as an infobox beside the results. The user often sees the answer before clicking anything. Each entity, such as "Thomas Jefferson", is stored with typed properties (birth date, presidency, spouse) that the system can retrieve in a single lookup rather than by scraping and re-ranking ten different articles.

## Scale and growth

The graph launched in English on May 16, 2012. Within seven months the data set had tripled, reaching 570 million entities and 18 billion facts. By mid-2016 Google reported 70 billion facts and that knowledge boxes answered roughly one-third of the 100 billion monthly searches it processed. By May 2020 the figures had reached 500 billion facts on 5 billion entities.

## Sources and construction

Google has never published official documentation of how the graph is built. The company states that its information is retrieved from many sources, including Wikipedia and the CIA World Factbook (the Factbook was discontinued in February 2026). Early on the graph was powered in part by Freebase, the crowd-sourced knowledge base Google later donated to the Wikimedia Foundation, which transformed it into Wikidata. A 2014 research effort called Knowledge Vault experimented with a different approach: automatically extracting and merging facts from across the web without relying on human contributors. By 2014 it had collected over 1.6 billion facts, of which 271 million were classified as "confident" (more than 90 percent likely true). Google later clarified that Knowledge Vault was a research report, not a shipped product, and that the company was experimenting with numerous models for gathering meaning from text.

## Language coverage

By December 2012 the graph had expanded to Spanish, French, German, Portuguese, Japanese, Russian, and Italian. Bengali support arrived in March 2017.

## Where the graph appears

The graph feeds Google's Knowledge Panel, the boxed summary that appears on the right on desktop or top on mobile of search results for entities such as individuals, organizations, locations, or objects. The same data also answers direct spoken questions in Google Assistant and Google Home voice queries.

## Criticisms

Source attribution. By May 2016 knowledge boxes were appearing for roughly one-third of the 100 billion monthly searches Google handled. Dario Taraborelli, head of research at the Wikimedia Foundation, has argued that omitting sources in these boxes undermines users' ability to verify information and form informed opinions. Press coverage has described the boxes as "frequently unattributed" and as presenting claims "as unsourced and absolute as if handed down by God".

Wikipedia traffic. The Register reported in 2014 that direct answers in knowledge panels caused significant readership declines on Wikipedia, one of the panel's underlying sources. The Wikimedia Foundation said it "welcomed" the functionality while investigating the traffic drops. In his 2020 book, Dariusz Jemielniak argued that because most users do not realise answers come from Wikipedia, the panels reduce the encyclopedia's visibility, fundraising reach, and ability to attract volunteers.

Bias and accuracy. Critics have noted that because the graph aggregates data from websites with strong search-engine optimisation, it can surface biased or inaccurate information. Examples cited include the absence, as of 2014, of a panel for Jesus despite panels for Moses, Muhammad, and Gautama Buddha, and a June 2021 knowledge box that labelled Kannada "the ugliest language in India", prompting outrage, a threatened lawsuit by the state of Karnataka, and a formal apology from Google after the snippet was changed.
