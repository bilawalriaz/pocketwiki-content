# Environmental ethics

Environmental ethics is the branch of practical philosophy that asks which natural entities deserve protection and how humans ought to use natural resources. The competing paradigms are anthropocentrism (humans as the central concern), ecocentrism or physiocentrism (value rooted in nature itself), and theocentrism (value rooted in God). The field influences environmental law, environmental sociology, ecotheology, ecological economics, ecology, and environmental geography.

## Origins

The field emerged in the late 1960s and 1970s. Rachel Carson and Murray Bookchin raised philosophical questions that mainstream philosophy had ignored; the first Earth Day in 1970 marked a turning point. Aldo Leopold's *A Sand County Almanac*, containing the 1949 essay "The Land Ethic," was republished by Ballantine Books and the Sierra Club before that Earth Day. Two *Science* papers were foundational: Lynn White Jr.'s "The Historical Roots of our Ecologic Crisis" (1967), blaming Western religion for the crisis, and Garrett Hardin's "The Tragedy of the Commons" (1968), showing how self-interested individuals deplete shared resources. Arne Næss coined "deep ecology" in 1972, arguing that all non-human entities have intrinsic value. The journal *Environmental Ethics* launched in the US in 1979; *Inquiry* had earlier served as the first international outlet.

## Marshall's three categories

Alan Marshall sorts environmental positions into three groups. *Libertarian extension* applies equal-rights thinking to non-humans; Andrew Brennan's eco-humanism and Næss's deep ecology sit here. Peter Singer fits too, arguing the "expanding circle of moral worth" must include non-human animals and rejecting speciesism, though he remained doubtful about moral status for non-sentient entities. *Ecologic extension* values whole ecosystems for their biological interdependence; Holmes Rolston takes this view, and James Lovelock's Gaia hypothesis, treating Earth as a self-regulating system, fits the category. *Conservation ethics*, sometimes called "shallow ecology," values nature only for its usefulness to present and future humans; this framing underwrote arguments at the 1992 Rio Earth Summit and the 1997 Kyoto summit.

## Normative frameworks applied to nature

*Normative ethics* asks how one ought to act; three classical frameworks structure environmental debate.

**Consequentialism** judges actions by outcomes. Act utilitarianism maximises well-being but tends to be "instrumentalist," treating nature as a means. Leopold's Land Ethic tries to escape this by declaring a thing right when it preserves the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community, wrong otherwise. Singer extends concern to all sentient beings while treating non-sentient entities as merely instrumental. Mattia Cecchinato names the "No Difference Problem": if one person's choice barely affects the climate, that person has no act-utilitarian reason to change. Responses appeal to tipping points, knock-on effects, or virtuous character.

**Deontology** judges actions by duties to what is right, not by consequences. If nature has intrinsic value, harming it is wrong as such, not merely as a bad calculation. Paul W. Taylor uses a Kantian framework, calling all living things "teleological centres of life" deserving respect, and tests actions by whether their maxims can be willed as universal law. Val Plumwood argues this remains duty-based rather than genuinely respectful. Christine Korsgaard's 2018 *Fellow Creatures* is the leading contemporary statement, holding that sentient animals, like humans, are ends in themselves.

**Virtue ethics** focuses on character. The Golden Mean holds that virtue lies between deficiency and excess; applied to nature, the question becomes which traits to cultivate. Philip Cafaro's environmental virtue ethics builds care for nature into ordinary virtues rather than treating it as a separate human-centred concern. Virtue ethics can dissolve inconsequentialism: someone driving a gas-guzzler weekly harms nature negligibly in measurable terms yet exhibits vices of selfishness and indifference. Val Plumwood adds ecofeminist virtues such as respect, gratitude, and sensitivity, suited to ecological diversity and indigenous worldviews. Ronald Sandler links human vices like greed and arrogance to environmental vices like apathy and pessimism.

## Religion and the human place

Christian theology holds humans accountable to God for resource use, motivated by love of neighbour and creation; harvest thanksgiving symbolises this in many countries. Since around 1900, theological environmentalism has split: one side, represented by Calvin DeWitt, treats religion as the basis of stewardship; the other, closer to Lynn White's diagnosis, sees theology as historically used to justify consumption. John Muir and the Sierra Club framed wilderness as sacred and helped establish early public nature preserves.

Peter Vardy distinguishes *strong* anthropocentrism (humans rightly at the centre of reality) from *weak* anthropocentrism (reality can only be interpreted from a human standpoint). Bryan Norton's environmental pragmatism refuses the dichotomy, arguing strong anthropocentrism underestimates the many instrumental values nature provides humans. Michael Mautner's biotic ethics frames humans as the only species able to secure life's future beyond Earth, potentially for trillions of years, giving human existence a cosmological purpose.

## Animal ethics and environmental ethics

Whether wild animals should be protected for their own sake (animal ethics) or as parts of ecosystems valued as wholes (environmental ethics) is contested. Faria and Paez argue the two are incompatible, since duties to individual sentient beings can conflict with duties to species or ecosystems. J. Baird Callicott argues they can be reconciled by treating wholes and their members as morally considerable together. The dispute turns on whether moral consideration is owed to individuals, to collectives, or to both.
