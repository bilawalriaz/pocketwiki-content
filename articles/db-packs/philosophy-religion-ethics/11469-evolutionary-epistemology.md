# Evolutionary epistemology

Evolutionary epistemology is not one idea but a small family of related claims that sit at the crossroads of philosophy and evolutionary biology. All of them borrow Darwinian vocabulary to describe either how minds came to work the way they do, how knowledge changes over time, or how new abstract concepts enter human thought. The label is ambiguous, so the first task is to keep its three meanings separate.

## Three meanings

1. The biological evolution of cognitive mechanisms in animals and humans. The mind is partly genetically determined, and its structure reflects adaptation, a nonteleological fit between organism and environment produced by natural selection rather than design.
2. Knowledge itself evolves. Units of knowledge, especially scientific theories, are treated as if they undergo variation and selection. A theory such as germ theory becomes more or less credible as the surrounding body of knowledge shifts, and a theory that has survived every available test is still treated as only provisionally true.
3. The historical discovery of new abstract entities, such as abstract number or abstract value, that necessarily precede the individual learning of those abstractions. This branch is sometimes called phylogenetic epistemology, the species-level counterpart to Jean Piaget's ontogenetic (child-development) approach.

## Cognitive mechanisms as adaptations

A cognitive trait that raises inclusive fitness in a population should spread; a trait that prevents its carriers from passing on genes should become rarer. The mind, on this view, is shaped by the same blind process that shapes anatomy. No foresight is required.

## Knowledge as an evolving population

The more striking move is to treat theories themselves as replicators that vary, compete, and get selected. Empirical testing alone does not justify a theory's pragmatic value; social and methodological processes select the theories with the closest fit to a given problem. Karl Popper illustrated the provisional nature of even the most confirmed theories with Newtonian physics, thoroughly tested and still overturned by Einstein's work on space-time. For the evolutionary epistemologist, no amount of past success predicts survival of future tests.

The phrase was coined in 1963 when Donald T. Campbell wrote a letter to Karl Popper. Popper gave the first comprehensive treatment in his 1970 article "Sketch of an Evolutionary Epistemology"; Campbell published his own version in 1974. Piaget had alluded to the idea in 1968 and described one of its possible forms as early as 1936, in *The Origins of Intelligence in Children*.

## Piaget's missed third option

Piaget assumed there is a parallelism between the logical progress of knowledge and the formative psychological processes of the child, so he chose to study ontogenesis (child development) because the biogenesis of human thinking in species like Neanderthal man was poorly documented. The source argues Piaget was too quick to dismiss phylogenetic epistemology, because considerable historical data exists about the origins and evolution of the notational systems that reify different kinds of abstract entity, exactly the data Piaget claimed was missing.

## Popper's scheme and its critics

Popper's basic cycle is:

`P₁ → TT → EE → P₂`

A problem (P₁) prompts tentative theories (TT), which undergo error elimination (EE), and the surviving errors become new problems (P₂).

Two criticisms recur. First, the problem with which the cycle begins is not defined, so the outcome cannot be predicted. Second, human language is left out of the cycle. If all organisms are problem solvers and problems arise with the emergence of life, then death is the absolute problem, and every derivative problem (P₁, P₂, ..., Pₙ) is a later specification. Including language, the scheme becomes:

`L₁ → P₁ → TT → EE → L₂ → P₂`

L₁ is the language that lets a problem become visible at all; L₂ is the new language that opens new spaces of problems. The example used is Planck units, which can only be described in the language of quantum mechanics, not by correcting errors inside classical mechanics. Popper's original four-step cycle cannot represent such qualitative transitions, because it has no slot for the language that makes a new kind of problem available in the first place.
