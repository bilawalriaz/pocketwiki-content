# Knowledge cutoff

A knowledge cutoff (also called a data cutoff) is the date beyond which a large language model has no training data. Pretraining happens once, on a static snapshot of internet text, books, and other sources collected up to that date. After pretraining ends, the model's knowledge is frozen. It has no live connection to the world and cannot learn new facts on its own, so anything that happened after the cutoff is absent from its weights.

A model that never saw an event cannot know about it. When asked, it still produces an answer by predicting the most probable next word, and that answer can be confidently wrong. In machine learning, this kind of confident-but-false output is called a hallucination, and an outdated cutoff is one of its main causes.

The problem extends beyond missing facts. A study by Cacioli et al. at Oregon State University tested models on two versions of the IDSA's COVID-19 treatment guidelines using a 363-question benchmark. GPT-3.5-Turbo and Llama-2, whose cutoffs predated the newer guideline, scored 76.03% and 25.26%. Models with later cutoffs, like GPT-4o and Llama 3.3, exceeded 90%. The authors concluded that recency of information should be treated as a safety-critical attribute on par with alignment or interpretability, especially in clinical decision-making where outdated guidance can cause real harm.

A model's official cutoff is also not the whole story. A study by Pęzik et al. at the University of Łódź found that the effective cutoff, the date up to which a model can actually answer reliably, varies by topic. Training data is unevenly distributed, so some subjects reflect later knowledge than the headline date while other subjects may be missing information that nominally predates the cutoff.

Retraining is the obvious fix but rarely happens, because training a frontier large language model can cost over a billion dollars. Three workarounds are common, each with limits.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) connects the model to an external knowledge base or search engine so it can fetch current information at query time and cite it. Grounding answers in retrieved sources reduces hallucinations and improves accuracy, but it does not fully solve the problem. The external source may be outdated or biased, the model may misinterpret the prompt or pull from low-quality sources, and its underlying reasoning still reflects the original training data. Even with browsing, the model's baseline assumptions remain anchored to what it learned during pretraining. Reinforcement learning from human feedback, which aligns a model's outputs with human preferences, can further improve reliability on top of this.

Continual learning takes a different approach. Instead of one big pretraining run, new data is fed in continuously to extend the model's knowledge without paying for a full retraining cycle. One method is fine-tuning, which precisely adjusts a model's behavior, often using efficient variants like Low-Rank Adaptation. The catch is catastrophic forgetting, the tendency for a model to abruptly lose previously learned capabilities as its weights shift toward the new data. Continual learning also does not give real-time awareness.

Current cutoff dates for major models include GPT-4 at September 2021, GPT-4 Turbo at December 2023, GPT-OSS at May 2024, Llama 4 at August 2024, and GPT-5 at September 2024. Some of these models also expose integrated search tools that surface more recent information, which makes it ambiguous whether an answer came from the frozen weights or a live lookup.
