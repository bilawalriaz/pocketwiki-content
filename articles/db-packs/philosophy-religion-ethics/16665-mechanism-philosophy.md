# Mechanism (philosophy)

Mechanism is the view that natural wholes, especially living things, are like complicated machines: parts with no intrinsic relation to one another, whose behaviour is fixed by their arrangement and by the laws governing matter in motion. The doctrine appears in two forms, and most confusion comes from mixing them up.

## Two doctrines

*Universal mechanism* is a global metaphysical thesis: everything in nature is reducible to the motion and collision of matter under mechanical laws, which produces a thoroughgoing determinism (every earlier and later state is fixed by the present one). *Anthropic mechanism* is a narrower local thesis: everything about human beings, including the mind and will, can be fully explained mechanically, as certainly as a clock or an engine can. Universal mechanism is largely a historical position. Anthropic mechanism is still live in philosophy of mind.

## Why mechanism implies determinism

If every phenomenon is wholly fixed by matter moving under physical laws, then the universe's state at any moment determines every earlier and later state. Pierre Simon de Laplace gave the canonical statement: an intellect that knew all the forces in nature and the position of every being, and was vast enough to analyse the data, could condense the motion of the greatest bodies and the lightest atom into a single formula, and for it nothing would be uncertain, the past and future alike lying before its eyes.

## The mechanical philosophy of the Scientific Revolution

Mechanical philosophy is the early modern European programme that compared the universe to a large machine. One of its first expositions is the opening of Thomas Hobbes's *Leviathan* (1651), whose second chapter invokes the principle of inertia (the tendency of a body to keep moving or stay at rest unless acted on).

The natural philosophers who built the programme were mostly French, with English and Dutch connections: Pierre Gassendi, Marin Mersenne, René Descartes, Kenelm Digby, Hobbes, Walter Charleton, and Isaac Beeckman. Robert Boyle used "mechanical philosophers" for both corpuscularian (atom-based) theorists like Gassendi and Descartes and non-corpuscularian ones, united by the clockwork-universe picture. In France the philosophy spread through private academies and salons, in England through the Royal Society, with less early impact in English universities than in continental ones.

Descartes was a mechanist but also a *substance dualist* (he held that reality consists of two fundamentally different kinds of stuff: extended matter and immaterial mind). He identified matter with spatial extension and denied that empty space (a vacuum) can exist, then argued that the conscious mind cannot be explained as extended matter in motion, since mechanism cannot yield mental experience. His biology was nevertheless fully mechanistic: in *Treatise on Man* he wrote that passion, memory, and imagination follow from the arrangement of the machine's organs "every bit as naturally as the movements of a clock or other automaton follow from the arrangement of its counter-weights and wheels."

Isaac Newton weakened strict mechanism by tolerating action at a distance in gravity (gravity pulling across empty space without any touching mechanism, contradicting the idea that forces only act by contact), yet his predictive success carried the programme forward. Philosophers inspired by his example, chiefly the French materialists Julien Offray de La Mettrie and Denis Diderot, kept the banner aloft; de la Mettrie's *Man a Machine* (1748) is the most influential statement of anthropic mechanism.

## Anthropic mechanism and its opponents

The core debate is about the human mind and free will. Anti-mechanists argue that if matter is intrinsically devoid of mental properties, consciousness cannot arise from mechanistic processes, and that mechanistic determinism is incompatible with the experience of free will. Contemporary defenders include Norman Malcolm and David Chalmers.

Anthropic mechanists reply in two ways. *Eliminative* replies grant the conflict with common-sense intuitions and revise those intuitions away: Paul Churchland takes this route, embracing eliminative materialism (the view that our ordinary concepts of mind will be discarded as science progresses) in philosophy of mind and hard determinism (every event, including every human choice, is fully caused by prior events) on free will. *Non-eliminative* replies argue that the supposed incompatibilities are specious: consciousness and free will, properly understood, are compatible with mechanism. J. J. C. Smart and Daniel Dennett defend this route, pairing physicalist theories of mind (theories that treat the mind as a physical system, nothing more) with compatibilism (the view that free will and determinism can coexist because free will only requires choices to follow from one's own desires and reasoning, not to be uncaused).

A subtler tradition tries to refute mechanism through Gödel's incompleteness theorems, arguing that since human mathematicians can see truths that no consistent Turing machine (a simple abstract computing device that follows fixed rules) can prove, the mind is not a finite machine. Gödel himself raised this disjunction in his 1951 Gibbs Lecture: either the mind is not a consistent finite machine, or there exist Diophantine equations (equations in whole numbers, like x² + y² = z²) whose solvability it cannot decide; he regarded the second horn as implausible. J. R. Lucas (1961) and Roger Penrose (*The Emperor's New Mind*, 1989; *Shadows of the Mind*, 1994) developed the argument further. Hilary Putnam (1960) objected that Gödel's technique requires the system to be consistent, and human reasoning may be inconsistent, so the argument does not go through. The modern consensus in mathematics and AI is that Gödelian anti-mechanist arguments fail, since the theorems are perfectly consistent with the computationalist thesis (the claim that minds are, in principle, computational systems).

## Criticisms of universal mechanism

Colin Murray Turbayne identified three procedural errors in Descartes and Newton. They exported the certainty of deductive reasoning into nature itself, as if "nature obeys the logic of the deductive method." They artificially split nature into causes (gravity, resistance) and effects (bodies at rest and in motion), then treated those causes as inherent forces without direct observational evidence, violating their own empirical method. They also assumed that the scientific method essentially requires calculation and differential equations, an unnecessarily narrow definition of science.

Religious critics charge that the philosophy makes God's continuous involvement in creation unnecessary, slides toward Deism (the view that God created the world but does not intervene in it), abandons classical ontological categories such as essence, form, and potential, and cannot account for spiritual experience or the immaterial aspects of reality.
