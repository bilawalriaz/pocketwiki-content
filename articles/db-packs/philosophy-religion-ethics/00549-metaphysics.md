# Metaphysics

Metaphysics is philosophy's investigation of the most general features of reality: what exists, what kinds of things exist, how they relate, and how change, causation, mind, and possibility fit together. Aristotle called it "first philosophy" because it examines what everything shares ("being qua being") rather than any restricted domain, the way physics or biology do.

A persistent divide shapes the field. *Metaphysical realism* treats metaphysics as a study of a mind-independent world. *Kantian and Strawsonian* approaches reframe it as mapping the conceptual categories (quantity, quality, relation, modality) that structure human experience, asking how thought carves reality rather than what reality is in itself. *Revisionary metaphysics* tries to improve those schemes; *descriptive metaphysics* records them. The boundary between metaphysics and the special sciences is contested: metaphysics aims at maximum generality, often treating science's ontology as evidence for its own claims (Quine's naturalized metaphysics).

## Core Branches

- **Ontology (general metaphysics):** the most fundamental categories of being: substances, properties, relations, facts, and how they depend on one another.
- **Special metaphysics:** metaphysical cosmology (the universe as a spatiotemporal whole), rational psychology (mind and freedom), natural theology (a divine first cause).
- **Applied metaphysics:** using ontological frameworks in ethics, AI, economics, or psychiatry.
- **Meta-metaphysics:** the metatheory of metaphysics itself, its methods, scope, and status.

## Existence and Categories

Existence is the most basic metaphysical concept, yet its logical form is disputed. Frege and Russell treat existence as a second-order property: an object exists if it instantiates some property. Meinong treats it as a first-order property, permitting reference to "nonexistent objects" like Pegasus. Plato distinguished degrees of reality, with immutable Forms more real than mutable matter. Aristotle's ten categories placed *substance* (man, horse) as the primary kind, with quantity, quality, and place as dependents. Kant reframed categories as twelve *a priori* concepts of the understanding. The concrete/abstract distinction cuts across these schemes: concrete objects (rocks, people) exist in spacetime, change, and enter causal relations; abstract objects (numbers, sets) are spatiotemporally non-located, immutable, and causally inert.

## Particulars and Universals

A *particular* is a non-repeatable individual (Socrates, the Eiffel Tower, the number 2). A *universal* is a repeatable feature, such as redness or taller-than, that can be instantiated by many particulars. The "problem of universals" asks what ontological status universals have:

- **Platonic realism (ante rem):** universals exist independently, even uninstantiated.
- **Aristotelian realism (in re):** universals exist only when instantiated by particulars.
- **Nominalism:** only particulars exist; universals are names or linguistic conventions.
- **Conceptualism:** universals exist as mental concepts used to classify experience.

A parallel question concerns the structure of particulars. *Substratum theory* (Locke) posits a bare particular plus properties. *Bundle theory* (Hume) reduces particulars to bundles of properties, sometimes with a *haecceity*, an individual essence that makes them this thing rather than that. *Mereology* studies part-whole relations: *Universalism* says any collection composes a whole; *Nihilism* denies composite wholes altogether.

## Modality, Space, Time, and Change

Modal metaphysics concerns what could or must be the case. David Lewis's *modal realism* treats possible worlds as concrete, spatiotemporally isolated realities on par with the actual one, with "Possibly p" meaning p is true in at least one such world and "Necessarily p" meaning p is true in all. Alternatives treat worlds as abstract objects or useful fictions. A deeper question is whether modality is a fundamental feature of reality or reducible to non-modal facts about language or imagination.

Three positions on spacetime compete: *substantivalism* treats it as a mind-independent container; *relationism* (Leibniz) reduces it to spatial and temporal relations among objects; *Kantian idealism* treats it as a mental framework. On time's passage, the *A-theory* holds that time flows and events have dynamic tense (past, present, future), while the *B-theory* treats time as a static order of earlier-than and later-than relations, with *eternalism* holding all times equally real and *presentism* restricting reality to the present. Persistence splits into *endurantism* (objects are wholly present at each moment, gaining and losing properties) and *perdurantism* (objects are 4D "worms" composed of temporal parts, so change is qualitative difference between parts).

## Causation, Mind, and Identity

Causation has been analyzed as deterministic necessitation, as probabilistic raising of chances (Anscombe), as constant conjunction (Hume), as nomic regularity grounded in laws, as counterfactual dependence, or as a primitive unanalyzable relation. *Eliminativism* denies causation exists.

The mind-body problem pits *substance dualism* (Descartes: distinct mental and physical substances) against *materialism* (mind reduces to brain states or functional roles), *idealism* (only mental exists), and *neutral monism* (fundamental reality is neither). The "hard problem" of consciousness asks how physical processes yield phenomenal experience. Free-will debates split *incompatibilists* (free will requires indeterminism), *hard determinists* (no free will), *libertarians* (free will, so no determinism), and *compatibilists* (free will as acting on one's desires is consistent with determinism). *Numerical identity* (Morning Star = Evening Star) and *diachronic identity* (the Ship of Theseus) extend these concerns across time.

## Methods and Limits

Metaphysics has used *a priori* intuition and deduction, *thought experiments* (philosophical zombies test intuitions about consciousness), *conceptual analysis*, *phenomenology* (Husserl's eidetic variation, Heidegger's fundamental ontology), and *naturalized* approaches that read ontology off our best scientific theories, using existential quantification ("∃x Horse(x)") to track ontological commitment. Theory choice is guided by parsimony (Ockham's Razor), explanatory power, and fit with established belief.

The field faces persistent criticism. Skeptics hold that humans lack access to ultimate reality (Hume, Kant). The logical positivists (Carnap) argued metaphysical statements are meaningless because they fail empirical verification. Deflationists (Hirsch, Chalmers) treat many disputes as merely verbal, such as "tables exist" versus "particles arranged table-wise exist." Critics also point to lack of progress and pragmatic irrelevance. Defenders reply that at least some disputes track objective structure, even if others collapse into language.

Metaphysics is now entangled with empirical science (Relativity reshaped spacetime metaphysics), with formal ontology in computer science (SUMO, BFO), and with logic as a tool of precision. The Quinean criterion reads ontological commitment directly from the existential quantifiers of our best formalized theories.
