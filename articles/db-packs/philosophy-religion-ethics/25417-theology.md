# Theology

Theology is the rational study of religious belief, with particular focus on the nature of divinity. The word comes from Greek *theologia* (*theos*, "god," plus *logia*, "utterances" or "reasoning"), so it names speech and thought about the divine. Unlike religious studies, which examines religions from a neutral standpoint, theology is typically conducted from within, or in committed dialogue with, a particular religious tradition. Practitioners use philosophical, historical, ethnographic, and experiential argument to analyze, defend, critique, or compare doctrines and to draw conclusions about how to live.

## Classical roots

Plato used *theologia* around 380 BC in the *Republic* to mean "right speech about the gods," and argued in the *Laws* that heavenly bodies are moved by divine souls (*nous*) that care for humanity. Aristotle divided theoretical philosophy into mathematics, physics, and *theologike*, a branch equivalent to metaphysics that concerns the divine. The Roman writer Varro distinguished three kinds of such discourse: *mythical* (myths of the gods), *rational* (philosophical analysis of gods and cosmology), and *civil* (public rites and duties). Christian authors Tertullian and Augustine adopted this threefold pattern, while Boethius in the early sixth century defined *theologia* as the philosophical study of motionless, incorporeal reality, opposed to *physica*, which studies moving, corporeal things.

## Christian theology

In scholastic Latin the term came to mean the rational study of Christian doctrine, investigating the coherence of Scripture and tradition (often as compiled in Peter Lombard's *Sentences*). The English word entered use in this academic sense in the fourteenth century. Thomas Aquinas defined theology as having a triple character: taught by God, teaching about God, and leading to God (*Theologia a Deo docetur, Deum docet, et ad Deum ducit*). Christian theologians draw on biblical exegesis and rational argument, working within a confession or across traditions.

## Other Abrahamic traditions

In Judaism, most reflection since the destruction of the Second Temple has happened inside the community and synagogue, through rabbinic discussion of Jewish law and *Midrash* (rabbinic biblical commentary), with theology closely linked to ethics. Islam has no single dominant equivalent to Christian theology; the closest is *Kalam* (rational theological discussion), though elaboration of *Sharia* (Islamic law) and *Fiqh* (jurisprudence) play a comparable role. German universities have established departments of Islamic theology since the Deutsche Islamkonferenz.

## Eastern and non-theistic traditions

Buddhism lacks a creator God, so some scholars prefer "Buddhist philosophy" to "Buddhist theology." Others, such as Jose Ignacio Cabezon, accept theology as discourse on ultimate reality; the resulting field of *buddhology* studies Buddhahood, with the *Trikāya* (doctrine of the three "bodies" of a Buddha) central in all Mahayana traditions. Hinduism calls its schools of philosophy *darśana* ("viewpoint"); *Vedanta* is the most influential in modern Hinduism, with multiple sub-schools offering different theories of *Ishvara* (the Supreme Lord). Shaivism includes dualistic and non-dualistic strands, and Shakta traditions center on a feminine ultimate deity. In Shinto the term *shingaku* ("theology") was applied during the Edo period; modern scholarship more often uses *kyōgaku* (doctrinal studies) or *shūgaku* (denominational studies).

Modern Pagan communities, including Wicca, Heathenry, Druidry, and Kemetism, give precedence to orthopraxy (correct practice) over orthodoxy, so theological views vary widely; Graham Harvey observes that Pagans "rarely indulge in theology." Some scholars have proposed *hierology* as a more neutral term for traditions without a single deity.

## Theology in the university

Theological learning is as old as formal higher education. Taxila (possibly sixth century BC), the Platonic Academy (fourth century BC), the Confucian *Taixue* (second century BC), the School of Nisibis (fourth century AD), Nalanda (fifth or sixth century AD), and Al-Karaouine (tenth century) all incorporated religious instruction. The earliest European universities (Bologna 1088, Oxford c. 1096, Paris c. 1150) were founded largely to train clergy, with philosophy, law, and medicine developing later. In the High Middle Ages theology was called the "queen of the sciences" and philosophy its "handmaiden," serving as capstone to the Trivium and Quadrivium.

This preeminence began to erode during the Enlightenment, especially in Germany, as independent reason challenged theology's authority. Modern debates turn on whether theology is a genuinely theoretical discipline or requires prior faith commitment that conflicts with academic freedom. In Germany today, state-university theology faculties remain tied to specific denominations (Protestant or Roman Catholic); in the United States many major universities, including Harvard, Yale, Princeton, and Georgetown, were founded to train Christian ministers, a role now continued mainly by seminaries and divinity schools.

## Religious studies contrasted

Religious studies examines historical and contemporary religious practice using neutral, secular tools: anthropology, comparative religion, history of religions, philosophy of religion, psychology of religion, and sociology of religion. It brackets questions of truth or falsehood, which theology typically does not. The two fields are sometimes in tension, sometimes coexisting, and sometimes lacking a clear boundary.

## Criticism

Criticism of theology as a rational discipline is ancient. Protagoras, reportedly exiled from Athens in the fifth century BC, declared that "concerning the gods I cannot know either that they exist or that they do not exist." In 1772, Baron d'Holbach called theology "a continual insult to human reason." Ludwig Feuerbach argued in *The Essence of Christianity* (1841) that theology is a projection of human nature that must be dissolved into anthropology. A. J. Ayer's logical positivism held that statements about the divine are literally nonsensical. Richard Dawkins argued in 1993 that theology has "completely and repeatedly failed to answer any questions about the nature of reality or the human condition" and questioned whether it qualifies as a subject at all. Walter Kaufmann, while distinguishing theology from religion, acknowledged that "Christianity is inescapably a theological religion."
