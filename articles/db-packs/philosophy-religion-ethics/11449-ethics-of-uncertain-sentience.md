# Ethics of uncertain sentience

The ethics of uncertain sentience is the branch of applied ethics that asks how to act when it is genuinely unclear whether a being can subjectively experience the world. Sentience, meaning the capacity for feeling or perception, sits at the heart of moral status, so uncertainty about it produces practical dilemmas rather than abstract puzzles.

## Why the problem is hard

A being can be biologically complex without being sentient. Nociception is the detection of harmful stimuli by specialised receptors (nociceptors); it can trigger protective reflexes and learning without requiring conscious feeling. Pain, in the ethically relevant sense, is a subjective experience of harm. Lobsters, crabs, octopuses, insects and fish all show nociception, but each lacks clear evidence for the kind of subjective experience that would justify moral concern, and the neural architecture that produces it remains unknown. The problem is therefore not whether animals behave in sophisticated ways, but whether any of those behaviours are accompanied by felt experience.

The issue is consequential because the scale of animal use is enormous. Roughly 1–2.7 trillion wild fish are caught annually, with tens of billions more slaughtered in fish farms. About 1 trillion insects are raised and killed each year, with 79–94 billion alive at any moment. A small probability of sentience, multiplied by such numbers, yields large possible moral stakes.

## Responses to uncertainty

Philosophers have proposed several decision principles for cases where sentience cannot be confirmed.

The incautionary principle treats uncertainty as licence: when in doubt, treat the being as not sentient. The precautionary principle does the opposite, requiring that we act as if the being is sentient. The expected-value principle multiplies the probability of sentience by the moral value the being would have if it were sentient, and treats that product as its actual moral value. Jeff Sebo, who named this the "sentience problem", argues for the expected-value approach.

Jonathan Birch offers a practical framework grounded in the precautionary principle for animal welfare science. Simon Knutsson and Christian Munthe argue, from virtue ethics, that a morally decent person must at minimum pay attention to the possibility that such animals matter. Adam Shriver argues for precise, precautionary and probabilistic approaches, holding that neuroscience should guide, but not replace, basic protections.

Kai Chan, writing in environmental ethics, argues that a non-zero probability of sentience is enough to forbid excluding beings from moral consideration, which is ethical extensionism extending moral concern without specifying a threshold.

## Cases across the biological tree

In David Foster Wallace's 2004 essay *Consider the Lobster*, he describes the Maine Lobster Festival and notes evidence of nociceptors in lobsters alongside uncertainty about endogenous opioids, natural pain-relieving chemicals produced by the animal. Robert C. Jones responds that the evidence is sufficient for a precautionary stance. A 2021 UK government-commissioned review by the London School of Economics evaluated 300 studies and graded the evidence for sentience as very strong for octopuses, strong for most crabs, and substantial but not strong for squid, cuttlefish and lobsters. It recommended against boiling lobsters and crabs alive and shaped debate leading to the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act.

For insects, Shelley Adamo's 2016 review concluded the question remains open: nociception and complex learning exist, but argument by analogy to human pain is weak without knowing which neural structures support subjective experience. She notes that Morgan's canon, the principle of attributing the simplest psychological explanation consistent with the evidence, and the precautionary principle point in opposite policy directions.

For fish, Maximilian Padden Elder argues that contemporary evidence warrants treating them as potential sufferers. Objections based on the absence of a neocortex (the mammalian brain region once thought necessary for higher experience) or human-like pain displays are anthropocentric, since subcortical processing can support affective states.

## Beyond biological organisms

The same problem extends to artificial intelligence. Nick Bostrom and Eliezer Yudkowsky argue that if an AI is sentient, inflicting unnecessary pain on it is wrong unless overriding reasons exist. Their Principle of Substrate Non-Discrimination states that two beings with the same functionality and conscious experience have the same moral status even if one is built from silicon and the other from neurons.

Some people adopt AI veganism, avoiding AI systems over concerns about how they affect people, animals and the environment, drawing parallels to the harms of animal agriculture. Whether AI systems are sentient remains unknown, but the precautionary logic that applies to insects and fish applies in principle to computational systems.

## Tensions and limits

The frameworks disagree on what counts as adequate evidence. Precaution can be expensive and may block research that would resolve the underlying uncertainty. The expected-value approach requires assigning numerical probabilities to sentience, which is controversial. The incautionary approach risks ignoring suffering that turns out to be real. Across all of them, sentience is a private phenomenon, and current science can only infer it indirectly from behaviour, physiology and neural architecture.
