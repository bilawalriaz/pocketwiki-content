# French Wars of Religion

The French Wars of Religion were a series of eight or nine civil wars fought between 1562 and 1598 between French Catholics and Protestant Huguenots. They killed an estimated two to four million people, gutted the authority of the crown, and were only settled when the Huguenot leader Henry of Navarre converted to Catholicism, took the throne as Henry IV, and granted Huguenots substantial rights under the 1598 Edict of Nantes. That compromise ended the fighting but not the underlying tension, which resurfaced when Louis XIV revoked the edict in 1685.

A small vocabulary makes the period legible. Huguenots were French Protestants who followed Calvinism, a Reformed theological system emphasising predestination and a strict moral code, and it had been spreading among the nobility since the 1530s. The Catholic League was an ultra-Catholic faction led by the House of Guise that opposed both the monarchy and Protestantism. Salic Law was the French rule barring women from the throne, which later determined the succession. The Politiques were a moderate faction that valued national unity over religious uniformity, providing an early vocabulary for secular statecraft.

## Why the wars began

The accidental death of King Henry II in 1559 created a power vacuum. His widow, the regent Catherine de' Medici, tried to hold the kingdom together by balancing Catholic and Protestant factions, issuing the tolerant Edict of Saint-Germain in 1562. That attempt collapsed in March 1562 when partisans of the Catholic Guise family massacred Huguenots at Vassy, touching off the first war. The first three wars (1562–1570) ended in edicts of limited toleration, but each truce broke down.

## St. Bartholomew's Day and the radicalisation of both sides

On 23 August 1572, Catholic mobs in Paris killed thousands of Huguenots, and the killing spread across France. The slaughter began as a targeted assassination of Protestant leaders, then spiralled into mass violence, devastating the Huguenot movement and drawing international condemnation. A fragile truce followed, but in 1576 the Guise family organised the Catholic League, pushing France toward a more confessional politics. When the king's heir died in 1584, Salic Law made the Protestant Henry of Navarre the next in line. The League, refusing to accept a Protestant king, allied with Catholic Spain.

## The War of the Three Henrys

The kingdom then split into three rival camps named for their leaders: King Henry III, the Catholic Henry of Guise, and the Protestant Henry of Navarre. On 13 May 1588, the Day of the Barricades, Catholic crowds seized Paris from Henry III. The king retaliated by ordering the assassination of the Duke of Guise on 23 December 1588. A Catholic assassin murdered Henry III himself on 1 August 1589, leaving Navarre the rightful but still-ousted king. The conflict was now a war of succession with a foreign sponsor, Spain, backing the League.

## Henry IV and the Edict of Nantes

Henry IV could not conquer a Catholic capital as a Protestant king, so on 25 July 1593 he abjured Calvinism, reportedly remarking that "Paris is well worth a mass." He was crowned and entered Paris in 1594. To rebuild the kingdom, he issued the Edict of Nantes in April 1598, granting Huguenots freedom of conscience, limited rights of worship, and political safeguards. The arrangement effectively created a protected Huguenot "state within a state," and the simultaneous Peace of Vervins (2 May 1598) ended the war with Spain. Henry IV was assassinated in 1610, but the dynasty survived.

## Why the settlement did not hold

The edict bought a grudging peace, not reconciliation. Huguenot revolts in the 1620s were crushed and their political privileges eroded. In October 1685, Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes with the Edict of Fontainebleau, outlawed Protestant worship, and drove a large, often skilled, Huguenot minority into exile, weakening the French economy along with the Huguenot community. Historians still debate whether the wars were essentially religious, with faith driving the killing, or essentially a noble power struggle in which religion served as a rallying flag, and whether 1629's Peace of Alès, which stripped the last Huguenot political privileges, should mark the real end of the conflict rather than 1598.
