# Existentialism

Existentialism is a family of European philosophical views that focus on the individual's struggle to live authentically despite a world that offers no built-in meaning. It is better understood as a shared approach that rejects systematic philosophy's attempt to derive human meaning from reason, tradition, or pre-existing categories.

The core claim, most clearly stated by Jean-Paul Sartre, is that existence precedes essence. People first exist, then define themselves through their choices and actions; no human nature, social role, or divine plan fixes their purpose in advance. This reverses Aristotle and Aquinas, who held that a thing's essence (what it is) precedes and explains its existence. A cruel act, on this view, makes a person cruel, and that person is responsible for the identity they thereby create. The same logic allows change: a cruel person can choose to act otherwise and become something different.

Because no purpose is given, every person is radically free, and that freedom produces angst: the dizziness of standing on a cliff, aware that nothing, not even instinct or character, compels one to step back rather than jump. Angst differs from fear in having no object; it is the awareness of total responsibility. Closely linked is despair, which Kierkegaard treats as the universal condition of anyone whose identity rests on qualities (a career, a skill, a reputation) that could collapse. Both follow from freedom, not from external threat.

Related to freedom is facticity: the unchosen features of a life, including birthplace, body, past actions, and absorbed values. Facticity limits freedom without determining it; a person can assign new meaning to past events but cannot erase them. Simone de Beauvoir emphasised sedimentation, the slow accumulation of past choices that resist new directions yet remain changeable over time.

The opposite of authentic living is bad faith (mauvaise foi). Sartre's example is a waiter who performs "waiter" so convincingly that he mistakes the social image for his real self. Bad faith includes pretending choices are random, denying freedom altogether, or hiding behind "one's genes" or "one's role." Authenticity, the central virtue, means acting in accordance with one's own freedom and taking responsibility for what one does.

The absurd arises from the gap between humans' demand for meaning and the universe's silence. Albert Camus argued that absurdity is not a property of the world or of people but emerges from their juxtaposition. His response, in The Myth of Sisyphus, was to imagine Sisyphus happy: one must persevere without appeal to any higher justification. Kierkegaard located absurdity instead in individual actions, whose basis is freedom rather than reason. Both views reject quietism, accepting meaninglessness as an excuse not to act.

Self-understanding is also shaped by the Other, the experience of another free subject inhabiting the same world, and by the Look, Sartre's term for being seen by such a subject. The Look objectifies what it sees and produces shame, showing that facticity is partly constituted by how others perceive one. The Other grounds intersubjectivity, the shared character of experience between minds.

The 19th-century figures now grouped under the banner, Søren Kierkegaard, Friedrich Nietzsche, and the novelist Fyodor Dostoevsky, never used the word. Kierkegaard, who held that each individual must give life meaning authentically, is usually treated as the movement's originator. Nietzsche argued that no one, not God, society, nor the self, gives humans their qualities, anticipating "existence precedes essence." Dostoevsky's Notes from Underground and Crime and Punishment dramatise the existential crisis, an acute confrontation with the apparent meaninglessness of one's situation.

The 20th-century core includes Martin Heidegger (Being and Time, 1927), Sartre (Being and Nothingness, 1943), Beauvoir, Karl Jaspers, Gabriel Marcel, and Paul Tillich. Albert Camus is often grouped with them, though he rejected the label. Heidegger later criticised Sartre in Letter on Humanism, arguing that reversing "essence precedes existence" still leaves one trapped in metaphysics, because the reversal stays within the framework it claims to overturn. Jaspers called his own closely related thought Existenzphilosophie. The term "existentialism" was coined by Marcel in the mid-1940s and first applied to Sartre, who at first rejected it before publicly embracing it in October 1945 in the lecture Existentialism Is a Humanism.

Existentialism is often confused with nihilism because both confront a world without given meaning. The distinction is that existentialists insist on acting and creating meaning in response to that absence (Camus's "one must imagine Sisyphus happy") rather than abandoning the search. Standard critiques, from logical positivists and Walter Kaufmann, charge that existentialism is hostile to reason and method; Herbert Marcuse argued that it projects the alienation of a particular historical moment onto existence itself. The tradition's reach has extended into literature (Kafka, Beckett, Camus, Sartre), theatre (the Theatre of the Absurd, including Beckett's Waiting for Godot), psychology (Rollo May, Irvin Yalom, Viktor Frankl's logotherapy), theology (Tillich, Bultmann), and Black existentialism (Frantz Fanon, Lewis Gordon), which extends the framework to questions of race and oppression.
