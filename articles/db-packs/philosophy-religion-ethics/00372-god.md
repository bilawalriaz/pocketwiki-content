# God

In theistic belief, God is the creator, sustainer, and ruler of the universe. Conceptions vary: one personal God in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam; an impersonal ultimate reality called *Brahman* in Hinduism; a non-intervening designer in deism; and, in atheism and agnosticism, a claim that is false or unknowable.

The English "God" comes from the Proto-Germanic *ǥuđan*, probably from a root meaning "to call" or "invoke." The earliest written form appears in the 6th-century Gothic Bible, the Codex Argenteus. The word was originally neuter and shifted to masculine during the Christianization of Germanic peoples. Capitalization marks the monotheistic God off from generic deities. Other languages carry their own names: *El* and *Yahweh* in Hebrew, *Allah* in Arabic, *Waheguru* in Sikhism.

How many gods there are defines whole traditions. Monotheism holds to a single deity and is central to Judaism and to Islam, where the doctrine is *tawhid*, the oneness of God. Christianity is monotheistic but describes God as a Trinity of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Hinduism often treats *Brahman* as a single supreme reality that shows itself through many deities. Non-theistic religions such as Jainism and some Buddhist traditions reject a creator God altogether.

How God relates to the world is a second axis. *Transcendence* means God exists independently of the material universe. *Pantheism*, defended by Baruch Spinoza in the 17th century, identifies God with the universe itself. *Panentheism* holds that God contains the universe while going beyond it. *Deism*, prominent in the 18th century, teaches that God created the world and does not intervene. Theism, the default of the major monotheisms, holds that God is both transcendent and personally active.

What God is like generates most philosophical friction. Theists commonly ascribe *omnipotence* (all-powerful), *omniscience* (all-knowing), and *omnibenevolence* (all-loving), together with *aseity*, self-existence, the idea that God depends on nothing for being. Holding the three "omni-" attributes together produces the problem of evil: if God is all-good, all-powerful, and all-knowing, why is there suffering? Omnipotence also produces a paradox: can God make a stone so heavy that God cannot lift it? Foreknowledge of every event, if God has it, seems to squeeze out human free will, a tension debated for centuries.

The existence of God is argued through a small set of recurring argument types, each with a standard counter. The *ontological argument*, formulated by Anselm in the Middle Ages and by René Descartes in the 17th century, tries to derive God's existence from the concept of a maximally great being. The *cosmological argument* reasons from the universe's beginning to a first cause. The *teleological argument*, or argument from design, infers a designer from the order of nature; it is weakened by the anthropic principle (we only observe a universe that lets observers exist) and by the charge of "god of the gaps" reasoning, where God fills whatever science has not yet reached. Additional lines of appeal run from beauty, from moral experience, and from conscience. Against these, atheism denies any gods exist, agnosticism holds the question is unknown or unknowable, and Stephen Jay Gould's "non-overlapping magisteria" (NOMA) proposes that science and theology address separate domains.

Anthropological and psychological theories from the 19th and 20th centuries treat gods as projections: stories a species tells to explain weather, death, and moral rules, useful for binding large groups. Neuroscience offers a narrower version: temporal lobe seizures and the molecule DMT can produce vivid experiences of a divine presence, suggesting the brain is built in a way that makes such experiences easy to have.

Knowledge of God is sought through *fideism* (faith as the route), through revelation, messages delivered by prophets or scripture, and through reason and natural theology. In Islam, divine attributes are invoked by name, such as *Al-Rahman*, "the Most Merciful." Worship, prayer, sacrifice, and remembrance are common responses in theistic traditions and are usually framed as benefiting the worshipper, not changing God. Religions divide over who is saved and how: *exclusivism* holds that only one tradition is true, *pluralism* that all or many lead to the same goal, and *inclusivism* that one tradition is central but others contain partial truth. The Baháʼí Faith teaches the unity of all religions and accepts progressive revelation through history.

Depiction varies by tradition. Islam and Judaism practice *aniconism*, avoiding visual images of the divine. Christian art moved from a symbolic hand reaching from a cloud to fully human figures, making God directly anthropomorphic. Most traditions default to male pronouns for God, though many theologians stress that God is ultimately genderless.

The recurring open questions are why a loving God hides, whether human ideas of God are more a product of psychology than of any reality, and whether the claim "God exists" can be tested at all or sits outside science by its nature.
