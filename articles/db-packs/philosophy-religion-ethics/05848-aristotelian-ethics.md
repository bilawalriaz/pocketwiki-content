# Aristotelian ethics

Aristotle defined ethics as a rational inquiry into how humans should live, a field pioneered by Socrates and Plato before him. He separated ethics, which studies the good of the individual, from politics, which studies the good of the city-state, though he held the individual's good to be subordinate to the community's. His central claim is that a person becomes good by building an excellent character through repeated practice, so that doing the right thing becomes natural, and that the ultimate aim of life is eudaimonia, a Greek term usually translated as well-being, happiness, or human flourishing.

## Character, virtue, and the mean

Virtue (Greek ēthikē aretē) is a stable disposition to act well, developed like a skill through habituation. A person who possesses it tends to do the right thing, at the right time, and in the right way. Moral virtues are character excellences such as bravery and the proper regulation of bodily appetites, and acting bravely or temperately are the excellent activities (Greek praxis) the virtues make possible. Most moral virtues sit at a mean between two extremes: cowardice and recklessness around courage, excess and deficiency around temperance. Virtue is practical: ethics aims to make people good, not just to describe goodness. Because situations differ, the right course requires prudence, or practical wisdom (Greek phronēsis), distinct from the theoretical wisdom (Greek sophia) of the philosopher.

## Reason and the highest good

Eudaimonia is the highest human good, and Aristotle connects it to his metaphysical theory of potentiality and actuality: eudaimonia is an actuality (energeia), and the virtues are stable dispositions (hexeis) that make such activity possible. To identify what is distinctively human, he uses the function argument: since nutrition, growth, and perception are shared with other living things, what sets humans apart is reason, so the human function is excellent exercise of the intellect, in practice through phronesis and the moral virtues, and in theory through contemplation. For this reason the most virtuous life, and the most like the divine, is the life of philosophy, a Socratic conclusion Aristotle preserves. He places phronesis among the intellectual virtues, while insisting that the highest moral virtues require one another and require intellectual virtue, so the truly virtuous person possesses the whole set.

## The three treatises

Three ethical works survive from Aristotle or his school: the Nicomachean Ethics (NE, 10 books, the most widely read), the Eudemian Ethics (EE), and the Magna Moralia (MM), whose authenticity has been doubted. Books IV–VI of the Eudemian Ethics also appear as Books V–VII of the Nicomachean Ethics, suggesting editorial rearrangement after Aristotle's time. The Politics is often treated as a sequel, since the NE closes by saying the ethical inquiry has prepared the way for political questions.

## Method

Aristotle begins ethics with what is roughly familiar to well-brought-up readers, the fact (Greek to hoti), relying on everyday experience of virtuous action and on reputable opinion (the "endoxic method" from Greek endoxa), rather than a grand theory of the good. He classifies popular accounts of the good life into three, pleasure, honor, and contemplation, and uses the function argument to argue that contemplation best fulfills the human function. A common objection is that he appears to derive what is good from what humans are, an instance of the is-ought problem.

## Moral virtue in practice

Character is partly formed by upbringing and partly by repeated action, which is why Aristotle likens ethical character to a skill learned by practice, such as playing an instrument. He distinguishes the disposition to act well from the disposition to feel appropriately: emotional responses such as modesty and righteous indignation (nemesis) can themselves lie at a mean shaped by habit. When a person chooses rightly but acts against the choice, as in giving in to chocolate cake against one's better judgment, the failure is akrasia, translated as weakness of will, incontinence, or lack of self-mastery.

## Justice

In Book V of the Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle distinguishes two senses. General justice is virtue expressed toward others, dealing fairly, honestly, and without cheating or theft. Particular justice is the proportional distribution of what people deserve according to merit, which is why Aristotle treats a good judge as necessary, since equitable decisions require deliberation about the details of each case.

## Intellectual virtues

Aristotle separates moral virtues from intellectual virtues, which are excellences of thought. In the Posterior Analytics and Nicomachean Ethics he identifies five that arrive at truth by affirmation or denial: sophia (wisdom uniting scientific knowledge and intuition of first principles), episteme (scientific knowledge of necessary, unchanging objects), and nous (intuition of first principles) in the theoretical class; phronesis (practical wisdom) in the practical class; and techne (craft knowledge) in the productive class. He adds subordinate excellences such as euboulia (good deliberation), sunesis (understanding why a situation is as it is), gnōmē (equitable judgment), and deinotes (cleverness in achieving a goal).

## Influence

Aristotle's ethical works were read continuously from antiquity onward and remain influential. In the 12th century Latin translations enabled Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas to synthesize Aristotle with Christian theology; Aquinas added the theological virtues of faith, hope, and charity, and treated perfect happiness as attainable only in the next life, but he preserved much of Aristotle's framework and wrote a commentary on the Nicomachean Ethics. It remained the dominant ethics text at Protestant universities until the late 17th century, with over fifty Protestant commentaries published before 1682. Modern virtue ethics, associated with thinkers such as Bernard Williams, takes its inspiration from Aristotle's emphasis on character, while the function argument has drawn persistent criticism for its move from what humans are to what is good for them.
