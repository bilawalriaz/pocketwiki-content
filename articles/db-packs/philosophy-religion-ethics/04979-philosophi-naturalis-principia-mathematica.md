# Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica

*Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica* (1687) is Isaac Newton's three-book Latin treatise that established the mathematical laws of motion and universal gravitation. It provided a single framework covering falling apples, ocean tides, and planetary orbits, and it became the foundation of classical mechanics.

## What the book contains

Newton begins with definitions of mass, momentum, and inertial mass, followed by the three axioms now called Newton's laws of motion. The first law (inertia) says a body remains at rest or in uniform straight motion unless acted on by a force. The second law defines force as the rate of change of momentum. The third law states that every action has an equal and opposite reaction.

**Book 1**, *De motu corporum*, treats motion in a vacuum. It derives the relationship between centripetal force (the net inward force on a body following a curved path) and the geometry of the orbit, recovering Kepler's laws of planetary motion. It also states the inverse-square law: gravitational force between two bodies falls off as the square of the distance separating them. A central result is the **Shell Theorem**, which proves that a spherically symmetric body attracts external objects exactly as if all its mass were concentrated at its center. This result lets a planet be treated as a point mass.

**Book 2** analyzes motion through resisting mediums such as fluids. It critiques René Descartes' vortex theory, which attributed planetary motion to swirling eddies in a space-filling aether. Newton's calculations showed vortices cannot produce stable planetary orbits. Book 2 also helped found fluid mechanics.

**Book 3**, *De mundi systemate*, applies Books 1 and 2 to the solar system. It accounts for Kepler's laws, the Moon's orbital irregularities, ocean tides, the precession of the equinoxes (the slow westward drift of the equinoxes along the ecliptic), and the paths of comets.

## How Newton argued

Newton used geometric proofs resembling calculus. In the General Scholium he declared *Hypotheses non fingo* — "I frame no hypotheses" — meaning he would derive laws from observed phenomena without speculating on hidden causes. He refused to explain why gravity attracts, only how it behaves. The "Rules of Reasoning in Philosophy," added in later editions, codified parsimony (admit no more causes than are necessary to explain the phenomena) and inductive generalization from experiment, shaping modern scientific method.

## Publication and reception

Edmond Halley paid to print the book after the Royal Society's funds ran out. The first edition appeared in 1687 under a license (*imprimatur*) from Samuel Pepys, then President of the Royal Society, with a print run of roughly 500 to 750 copies. Roger Cotes edited the 1713 second edition, and Newton himself prepared the 1726 third. Andrew Motte's 1729 translation became the standard English version; Émilie du Châtelet produced the standard French one. Modern English translations by I. Bernard Cohen, Anne Whitman, and Julia Budenz (1999) and by Charles Leedham-Green (2021) remain in print.

A priority dispute with Robert Hooke followed publication. Hooke claimed Newton took the inverse-square idea from him; Newton denied it, citing his own earlier work. The historical consensus is that Hooke suggested the law qualitatively while Newton supplied the mathematical proof.

## Legacy

The *Principia* replaced Cartesian physics with a quantitative mechanics that held for two centuries until Einstein's relativity extended it. Laplace and Lagrange treated it as the model of a scientific achievement. A page from the book was placed on the Voyager Golden Record, sent beyond the solar system in 1977.
