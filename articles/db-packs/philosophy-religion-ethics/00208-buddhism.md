# Buddhism

Buddhism is an Indian religion and philosophy that diagnoses the human condition and prescribes a path out of it. The founder, Siddhartha Gautama of the Shakya clan, was a śramaṇa (wandering ascetic teacher) who lived c. 5th century BCE in the eastern Gangetic plain. After rejecting both sensual indulgence and extreme asceticism, he sat beneath a Bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya and attained *bodhi* (awakening), spending 45 years teaching before dying at 80 in Kushinagar. The tradition, Buddhadharma, has roughly 324 million followers (4.1% of the world population, 2020) and spans most of Asia.

## The Core Diagnosis

Ordinary existence is marked by *dukkha*, pervasive unsatisfactoriness caused by the fact that nothing we grasp is permanent. The cause of dukkha is *taṇhā* (craving), rooted in *avidyā* (ignorance of the true nature of things). This traps beings in *saṃsāra*, the cyclic process of rebirth through six realms (heavenly, demi-god, human, animal, hungry ghost, hell). What continues after death is not a soul but a continuum of consciousness (*vijñāna*) shaped by *karma*, the intentional actions of body, speech, and mind that ripen as future experience.

Liberation is *nirvāṇa*, the "blowing out" of greed, hatred, and delusion. It is reached through the Middle Way by training three integrated modes:

- **Ethics (śīla):** non-harming. Laypeople keep five precepts (no killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech, intoxicants); monastics follow hundreds of Vinaya rules.
- **Meditation (samādhi):** concentration leading to deep absorption (*jhāna*).
- **Wisdom (prajñā):** insight into impermanence, non-self, and dependent origination.

These three together form the Noble Eightfold Path: Right View, Intention, Speech, Action, Livelihood, Effort, Mindfulness, and Concentration.

Three marks of existence (*ti-lakṣaṇa*) define what wisdom sees. All conditioned phenomena are *anicca* (impermanent), *dukkha* (unsatisfactory for that reason), and *anattā* (without a permanent self). *Dependent origination* (*pratītyasamutpāda*) states that everything arises in dependence on conditions. The Twelve Nidānas trace the causal chain: ignorance → karmic formations → consciousness → name-and-form → six senses → contact → feeling → craving → grasping → becoming → birth → aging and death. Breaking the chain at any link liberates.

## Practice

Two meditative approaches shape training. *Samatha* (calm) develops one-pointed concentration, the gateway to the jhānas. *Vipassanā* (insight) uses that stability to perceive impermanence, non-self, and conditional arising directly. Schools differ on whether insight follows calm, precedes it, or arises alongside it. Four boundless qualities, *mettā* (loving-kindness), *karuṇā* (compassion), *muditā* (empathetic joy), *upekkhā* (equanimity), counter ill will. Mindfulness (*sati*) is non-forgetting attention to the present, trained through breath meditation (*ānāpānasati*) or the four establishments of mindfulness (*satipaṭṭhāna*).

Devotional practices (offerings, chanting of sūtras and mantras, pilgrimage, stupa veneration) build *śraddhā* (faith) and accumulate merit to support better rebirth.

## The Branches

A practitioner in any branch takes refuge in the *Three Jewels* (Triratna): the Buddha, the Dharma (teaching), and the Sangha (monastic community, or the noble ones who have attained awakening).

**Theravāda** ("School of the Elders") preserves the Pali Canon, written in Sri Lanka from c. 1st century BCE. It emphasises the *arhat*, one who has personally attained nirvāṇa, and presents the Buddha as a human teacher. The Canon, organised as the *Tripiṭaka* ("Three Baskets"), contains the Vinaya (monastic code), Suttas (discourses), and Abhidhamma (scholastic analysis).

**Mahāyāna** ("Great Vehicle") emerged c. 1st century BCE – 2nd century CE through new sūtras (Prajñāpāramitā, Lotus, Avataṃsaka). Its ideal is the *bodhisattva*, who vows to attain Buddhahood for all beings. It teaches *tathāgatagarbha* (Buddha-nature), the potential for awakening in all beings, sometimes read as a tension with non-self. Its two main philosophical schools are *Madhyamaka* (Nāgārjuna), which analyses all phenomena as empty of intrinsic nature (*svabhāva*), and *Yogācāra*, which analyses experience as "consciousness-only" (*vijñaptimātratā*). Devotion to celestial Buddhas such as Amitābha and his western Pure Land Sukhāvatī is characteristic.

**Vajrayāna** ("Indestructible Vehicle"), usually classified within Mahāyāna, uses tantric methods (mantra, mudrā, maṇḍala, deity yoga, subtle-body practice) to accelerate attainment. It developed in India c. 4th–8th century CE, shows mutual influence with Śaiva Hinduism, and became the dominant form in Tibet from the 8th century, producing the Nyingma, Kagyu, Sakya, and Gelug schools.

## History

| Period | Development |
|--------|-------------|
| c. 5th c. BCE | Life of the Buddha; founding of the Sangha. |
| c. 3rd c. BCE | Emperor Ashoka patronises Buddhism; missions to Sri Lanka and Central Asia. |
| 1st c. BCE – 2nd c. CE | Earliest Mahāyāna sūtras; Buddhism reaches China via the Silk Road. |
| 4th–6th c. CE | Gupta period: Madhyamaka, Yogācāra, early Tantra. |
| 7th–8th c. CE | Vajrayāna established in Tibet; Chan (Zen) and Pure Land in Tang China. |
| 8th–12th c. CE | Pāla-era Buddhism in India; Islamic invasions destroy monastic institutions. |
| c. 1200 | Buddhism effectively disappears from the Indian subcontinent. |
| 13th–14th c. | Theravāda becomes dominant in Burma, Thailand, Cambodia, Laos. |
| 19th–20th c. | Buddhism reaches the West; modernist and reform movements emerge. |

Theravāda also spread to Sri Lanka (3rd c. BCE) and East Asia: Korea (4th c.), Japan (6th–7th c., Tendai, Shingon, later Zen, Pure Land, Nichiren), Vietnam (1st–2nd c.). Mahāyāna entered China 1st–2nd century CE through translators like An Shigao and Lokakṣema. Buddhist modernism (Olcott, Dharmapala) reframed the tradition in rationalist and psychological terms in response to colonialism. Navayana (Ambedkar, 1956) is a Dalit movement that converts to Buddhism while rejecting karma, rebirth, and monasticism in favour of social equality.

## Living Debates

The dates and social background of the historical Buddha remain uncertain (5th vs. 6th century BCE). Scholars disagree on whether a coherent "earliest Buddhism" can be recovered, on Mahāyāna's origins (textual, monastic, or lay), and on whether tathāgatagarbha contradicts non-self. Vajrayāna's debt to Hindu Tantra is read as direct borrowing, mutual influence, or undetermined. Contemporary Buddhists also debate whether meat-eating is permitted and whether Buddhism is best classed as religion, philosophy, or psychology.
