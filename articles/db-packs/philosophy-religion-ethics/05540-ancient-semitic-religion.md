# Ancient Semitic religion

Ancient Semitic religion refers to the polytheistic religions of Semitic-speaking peoples of the ancient Near East and Northeast Africa. Because "Semitic" is a linguistic category, the religious grouping is approximate; Egyptian, Persian, Greek, Hittite, Hurrian, Luwian, Lydian, Phrygian, Urartian, Minoan, Mitannian, and Parthian religions are excluded even where their speakers lived alongside Semitic ones.

The traditions fall into regional branches: Canaanite religions of the Levant (including the henotheistic worship of the Israelites, Judeans, and Samaritans, alongside the Amorites, Phoenicians, Moabites, Edomites, Ammonites, and Suteans), Sumerian-influenced Mesopotamian religion, Phoenician religion of Carthage, Nabataean religion, Eblaite, Ugaritic, Dilmunite, and Aramean religions, and Arabian polytheism.

Semitic polytheism fed into Abrahamic monotheism through Yahwism, a form of Canaanite religion focused on Yahweh, the national god of the Iron Age kingdoms of Israel and Judah. In that process, Yahweh was identified with El, the head of the Canaanite pantheon. The name "El" (Hebrew "Eloah") and the Arabic "ʾilāh" (god) share a common root, with the definite form "Allāh."

## The Proto-Semitic pantheon

The oldest reconstructable layer of Semitic religion survives because gods carry recognizable names across Akkadian, Ugaritic, Phoenician, Hebrew, Arabic, Old South Arabian, and Ethiopic. The shared core is a sky god, a mother goddess, male and female fertility deities, a storm god, a sun, and a moon.

## Mesopotamia

In Babylonian religion, the Sun, Moon, and the five naked-eye planets were linked to the chief gods. A cuneiform list now in the British Museum orders them: Sin (Moon), Shamash (Sun), Marduk (Jupiter), Ishtar (Venus), Ninurta (Saturn), Nabu (Mercury), and Nergal (Mars).

Assyrian state religion (Ashurism) centered on Ashur, patron of Assur, and Ishtar, patroness of Nineveh. The last clear evidence of worship of Ashur and other Mesopotamian gods dates to the 3rd century AD, after Christianity spread from the 1st century AD onward, though isolated Assyrian communities kept the practice until the 17th century. Ashur, patron of Assur from c. 22nd century BC, long competed with Marduk, patron of Babylon from c. 19th century BC, and in Assyria eventually replaced Marduk, even becoming Ishtar's husband. Major figures of the tradition include the gods Anu, Enlil, Ea, Sin, Shamash, Ishtar, and Ninurta, and the heroes and demons Adapa, Gilgamesh, Lugalbanda, Lilitu, and Pazuzu.

## Canaan

Canaanite religion was practiced across the Levant through the Bronze and Iron Ages. Knowledge of it expanded after 1928, when excavations at Ras Shamra (ancient Ugarit) in northern Syria uncovered Bronze Age alphabetic cuneiform tablets. Levantine scribes preferred papyrus, and the humid Mediterranean climate destroyed most of it, so before Ugarit scholars relied on the Hebrew Bible, with secondary help from Greek writers including Lucian of Samosata, Philo of Byblos (preserving Sanchuniathon), and Damascius; the Tel Mardikh archive (excavated from the 1960s) has added more.

Canaanites were polytheists. Families venerated ancestral household gods while recognizing a wider pantheon that included Baal, Anath, and El. Kings played a religious role and may have been venerated as divine, especially in rites such as the sacred marriage of the New Year Festival. The Ugaritic term for the pantheon, *ʾilhm* (Elohim), means "children of El," with the creator El as father of the other deities. In the Greek tradition El was married to Beruth (Beirut), and similar divine-city pairings appear in the Bible, where Melkart is linked to Tyre, Yahweh to Jerusalem, and Tanit and Baal Hammon to Carthage. El Elyon ("God Most High") appears in Genesis 14:18–19 as the deity whose priest was Melchizedek of Salem. The Phoenician-derived account of El Elyon's union producing Uranus and Ge (Heaven and Earth) parallels Genesis 1:1 and the Babylonian Anunnaki genealogy.

## Roots of Abrahamic monotheism

Mesopotamian and Canaanite traditions left clear marks on the Hebrew Bible. Many scholars hold that the Babylonian *Enuma Elish* shaped the Genesis creation account, the Epic of Gilgamesh the Genesis flood narrative, and the Sumerian myth of Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta the Tower of Babel; the story of Esther has also been traced to Assyro-Babylonian roots.

Deuteronomy 32:8 in the Masoretic Text has the Most High dividing the nations according to "the number of the sons of Israel." The Septuagint instead reads "angels of God" (with some manuscripts giving "sons of God"), and the Dead Sea Scrolls version specifies seventy sons of the Most High ruling seventy nations, an idea paralleled in Ugaritic texts. The Arslan Tash inscription suggests each of these seventy sons of El Elyon was bound to a people by covenant, so the Israelite picture of a supreme god who apportions nations to his subordinates grew directly out of older Canaanite polytheism.
