# Virtue ethics

Virtue ethics is a normative ethical theory that makes a person's character, rather than the outcomes of their actions (consequentialism) or adherence to rules and duties (deontology), the central subject of moral evaluation. Where consequentialism asks what produces the best result and deontology asks what is one's duty, virtue ethics asks what kind of person one should be and how one should habitually act.

## Core concepts

A **virtue** is a stable, deeply ingrained character trait that disposes a person to think, feel, and act well in some domain of life. A **vice** is the corresponding disposition to think, feel, and act poorly. Unlike habits, virtues sit at the core of personality.

Virtues are not merely behavioural. They are complex dispositions that unite thought and feeling. A generous person not only reasons correctly about when to help but also takes pleasure in helping, without inner conflict. This separates the virtuous from three other types: the *vicious* (who reason badly and feel wrongly), the *incontinent* (who know the right thing but act wrongly under feeling's pull), and the merely *continent* (who feel wrongly but act rightly by willpower). Feeling the right emotion at the right time, toward the right person, for the right reason, is part of possessing a virtue.

## Phronesis and eudaimonia

Two concepts carry most of the explanatory work.

**Phronesis** (practical wisdom) is an acquired intellectual virtue that lets its possessor perceive what a situation calls for and identify the right thing to do. Unlike theoretical wisdom, it terminates in action. John McDowell calls it a "perceptual sensitivity" to reasons for acting.

**Eudaimonia** is most often translated as flourishing. In this tradition it is an objective state: the well-lived life, achieved by exercising reason in accordance with virtue. For Aristotle, eudaimonia is the goal of human life, properly realised in the *polis* (city-state). A virtue is not a skill that produces eudaimonia; it is an expression of eudaimonia itself, a constitutive element of the human good rather than a mere instrument.

Not all modern virtue ethics is eudaimonic. Some versions reject the teleological structure and define virtues without reference to a human end, or identify them with traits tending toward an independently specified good, edging toward consequentialism.

## Historical outline

The tradition begins with Socrates, who held that virtue is knowledge. Plato set out the four **cardinal virtues** in the *Republic*: wisdom, justice, courage, and temperance. Aristotle, whose *Nicomachean Ethics* is the central text, distinguished **moral virtues** (character traits about emotion and action) from **intellectual virtues** (skills of the mind such as *episteme*, *sophia*, and *phronesis*). He argued that each moral virtue is a **golden mean** between a vice of excess and a vice of deficiency: courage lies between rashness and cowardice.

The Greek framework passed through Cicero and Ambrose into medieval Christian ethics. Eastern Orthodoxy linked virtue to *theosis* (deification), framing human flourishing as progressive participation in the divine life, cultivated through liturgy and ascetic practice. Aquinas gave the most thorough scholastic treatment in the *Summa Theologiae*. Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* remained the standard ethics text at Protestant universities into the late seventeenth century, with over fifty Protestant commentaries published before 1682.

After the Enlightenment, virtue ethics receded under utilitarianism and Kantian deontology. Its revival, the **aretaic turn**, is usually dated to G. E. M. Anscombe's 1958 essay "Modern Moral Philosophy." Key landmarks include Philippa Foot's *Virtues and Vices* (1978), Alasdair MacIntyre's *After Virtue* (1981), Julia Annas's *The Morality of Happiness* (1993), and Rosalind Hursthouse's *On Virtue Ethics* (1999). The framework has been applied in psychology (Seligman, Goleman), development (Nussbaum, Sen), and political philosophy (Sandel), and has generated **virtue epistemology**, virtue politics, and virtue jurisprudence.

Lists have varied widely. MacIntyre notes that Homer, the New Testament authors, Aquinas, and Benjamin Franklin each proposed different catalogues.

## Criticisms

Three objections recur. Nussbaum argues virtue ethics is less a rival to Kantian deontology and utilitarianism than an internal strand of them, since Kant's *Doctrine of Virtue* and utilitarians from Sidgwick to Mill all theorise virtues. Neo-Kantians, summed up in Trianosky's nine points, hold that morality must be grounded in rules, duties, and the question of what is right to do, a structure virtue ethics rejects. Louden argues virtue ethics promotes unsustainable utopianism: pluralist societies contain many groups with conflicting virtues, so ethics cannot dispense with rules about acts.

## Applications

The framework now extends beyond moral philosophy. McCloskey has used it to defend capitalism as compatible with bourgeois virtues. Page has grounded peace education in virtue ethics. Faunce argues healthcare whistleblowing needs a virtue-ethics foundation, reflected in the UNESCO Declaration on Bioethics. Schwartz treats practical wisdom as an antidote to bureaucratic medicine. Vallor's *Technology and the Virtues* proposes technomoral virtues, honesty, humility, justice, courage, empathy, care, civility, flexibility, and magnanimity, needed for flourishing in a socio-technical world.
