# Shia Islam

Shia Islam is the second-largest branch of Islam, comprising roughly 10–13% of the global Muslim population, or about 200–260 million people as of 2026. It began as the *shīʿat*, the "partisans" of Ali ibn Abi Talib, Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law, and developed into a distinct religious tradition with its own theology, law, and institutions. The founding dispute with Sunni Islam is the question of who should have led the Muslim community after Muhammad died in 632 CE.

The decisive Shia claim is that Muhammad explicitly designated Ali as his successor at Ghadir Khumm, declaring "Anyone who has me as his mawla, has Ali as his mawla." Shia Muslims read this as a divine appointment, a *nass*. When Muhammad died, Ali and the Prophet's family were occupied with burial preparations while Abu Bakr, Umar, and Abu Ubaidah met at Saqifa, the covered porch of a Medinan clan, and chose Abu Bakr as caliph. Shias see this as a violation of Muhammad's command; Sunnis regard it as a legitimate political decision. The two communities have defined themselves against this event ever since.

The split hardened at Karbala in 680 CE. After Muawiyah's death, his son Yazid demanded allegiance from Husayn ibn Ali, Ali's son, who refused on the grounds that pledging to Yazid would betray a treaty Hasan, Husayn's brother, had made with Muawiyah. Intercepted en route to Kufa, Husayn and around 72 followers were killed; his head was sent to Damascus. The annual commemoration, Ashura on the 10th of Muharram, remains the central event of the Shia religious calendar and the origin of a durable narrative of resistance to unjust rule.

Shia theology grew from this political wound. Leadership is a matter of divine guidance, not human selection, and the imamate must continue through infallible descendants of Ali and Fatima, Muhammad's daughter. These imams possess *ismah*, protection from error and sin, and interpret both the Quran and the Prophet's teachings. The major branches are Twelverism, which follows twelve imams ending with Muhammad al-Mahdi; Ismailism, which follows a different line; and Zaydism, which recognises Zayd ibn Ali as the fifth imam. Zaydis do not attribute *ismah* to the imams, an important exception to the broader Shia doctrine.

Two further doctrines separate Shia from Sunni theology. First, *ʿadl* (divine justice) is one of the five *uṣūl al-dīn*, the roots of religion, in Twelver thought, alongside monotheism, prophethood, imamate, and resurrection. *ʿAdl* gives moral categories a reality in God's nature, in contrast to Sunni Ashari theology, which subordinates them to divine will. Second, *amr bayn al-amrayn* ("a matter between two matters") resolves the tension between divine decree and human freedom by affirming that humans act with genuine agency within God's sustaining will.

Twelvers believe the twelfth imam entered *ghaybah* (occultation) in 874 CE and will return alongside Jesus (*ʿIsa*) to establish justice before the Day of Judgment. Because the hidden imam is no longer accessible, qualified jurists (*mujtahids*) interpret religious law through *ijtihad* (independent reasoning), and ordinary believers follow a *marjaʿ al-taqlid* (source of emulation). The most consequential political extension is *wilayat al-faqih* (guardianship of the Islamic jurist), the doctrine that, in the absence of the Mahdi, a qualified jurist should govern. It underpins the Islamic Republic of Iran and remains contested among Shia scholars themselves.

Twelver practice follows the Jaʽfari school of law, named after the sixth imam, Jaʿfar al-Sadiq. It draws on four sources: the Quran, the Sunnah (including imamic traditions), consensus (*ijmaʿ*), and the intellect (*ʿaql*). Observable differences from Sunni practice include combining some daily prayers, adding a phrase affirming Ali to the *shahada* (the Islamic declaration of faith), and the public mourning rituals of Muharram. The canonical hadith collections, the *Kutub al-Arbaʿa* (Four Books), were compiled between the 9th and 11th centuries AH, with al-Kulayni's *al-Kāfī*, al-Saduq's *Man La Yahduruhu al-Faqih*, and al-Tusi's *Tahdhib al-Ahkam* and *al-Istibsar* as the principal works.

Shia Muslims form majorities in Iran (about 90%), Iraq (about 65%), and Azerbaijan (about 55%), with substantial communities in Lebanon, Bahrain, Yemen, Turkey, Pakistan, and Afghanistan. Iran is the only state where Shia Islam is both the legal framework and the basis of government. The Shia centre of gravity shifted from Arab to Persian lands during the Safavid dynasty (1501–1736), which made Twelverism the state religion of Persia. The Fatimid Caliphate, founded in 909 in North Africa, was an Ismaili state.

Historical persecution of Shias has recurred across centuries, including the 1514 Ottoman massacre, the 1802 Wahhabi sack of Karbala, Saddam Hussein's campaigns against Iraqi Shia communities, ISIS persecution, and restrictions such as Malaysia's 2011 ban on Shia teachings. Western historians still disagree about whether early Shia identity was primarily a political faction or a religious movement, and whether doctrines such as *ismah* and the imamate developed gradually or were retrofitted to legitimise a lost political cause.
