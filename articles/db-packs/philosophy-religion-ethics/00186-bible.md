# Bible

The Bible is an anthology, not a single book: a Jewish and Christian collection of religious texts written over roughly 1,200 years by mostly unknown authors, then hand-copied for centuries before printing. Its name comes from Koine Greek *ta biblia* ("the books"). Christianity and Judaism share its oldest layer, the Hebrew scriptures, but organize, translate, and add to them differently, so the word "Bible" points to two related but distinct canons.

The Jewish canon is the **Tanakh**, an acronym for its three divisions: **Torah** (Teaching, the five books of Moses), **Nevi'im** (Prophets, narrative history and oracles), and **Ketuvim** (Writings, including Psalms, Proverbs, Job, the Five Scrolls, and Daniel). Most is in Biblical Hebrew, with Aramaic passages in Daniel and Ezra. The authoritative medieval edition is the **Masoretic Text**, standardized by 9th-century Masoretes, scribes who added vowels and accents to a consonantal script; the oldest complete copy is the Leningrad Codex (1008 CE). The Samaritan community uses only the Samaritan Pentateuch, a different recension of the Torah.

The Christian Bible keeps those Jewish scriptures as the Old Testament and adds the New Testament: 27 books in Koine Greek, written in the 1st century CE. The New Testament has four kinds of writing: the four Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John) narrating Jesus's life, death, and resurrection; Acts, on the early church; Epistles (letters, mostly by Paul); and Revelation (apocalyptic prophecy). Old Testament size varies by tradition: Protestants count 39, Roman Catholics 46 (adding deuterocanonical books from the Septuagint such as Tobit and Maccabees), Eastern Orthodox accept a few more, and the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo canon reaches 81 books, including 1 Enoch and Jubilees.

Composition began in oral songs and stories; the oldest Hebrew parts (such as the Song of Deborah) date to c. 1200 BCE. The Torah reached near-final form centuries later, probably under Persian (Achaemenid) or early Hellenistic rule, roughly 450–350 BCE. The New Testament took shape across the 1st–4th centuries CE, with formal canon lists at the Councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397). One event shadows the whole process: the Roman destruction of the Jerusalem Temple in 70 CE, which ended the priestly sacrificial system and reshaped both rabbinic Judaism and Christianity.

No original manuscripts survive. Texts were copied on papyrus and parchment scrolls, then in bound codices, folded sheets forming pages, the format popularized by Christians by the 3rd century. The Dead Sea Scrolls (250 BCE–100 CE, discovered 1947 at Qumran) are the oldest substantial Hebrew Bible manuscripts, more than 1,000 years older than the Leningrad Codex. They broadly confirm the Masoretic Text while showing variants closer to the Septuagint or Samaritan Pentateuch. New Testament manuscripts are more numerous than for any other ancient work; scribes produced local "text-types" (Alexandrian, Western, Byzantine), and critical editions note meaningful variants in roughly 7% of Greek New Testament words and 10% of Hebrew ones. Most variants are accidental (spelling, word order); some are intentional theological harmonizations.

The Septuagint (LXX) is the key bridge between the canons. Begun in Alexandria late in the 3rd century BCE and completed by 132 BCE, it is a Koine Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures for Greek-speaking diaspora Jews and later expanded to include deuterocanonical works. Because the Early Church used the Septuagint as its Old Testament, modern canon debates track back to which tradition a community inherited: the Hebrew one (Protestants), the Greek-expanded one (Catholics, Orthodox), or something broader (Ethiopian Orthodox).

Themes are concrete as well as theological: creation, covenant, justice, suffering, the problem of evil, family, authority, and the tension between divine sovereignty and human freedom. Hebrew Bible wisdom literature treats human reason as a condition for right living; the prophets frame criminal justice as rooted in God as ultimate judge, with impartial courts and protection of the vulnerable (Deut. 16:19, Lev. 19:15). The New Testament presents Jesus as the fulfillment of Hebrew prophecy and inaugurates a "new covenant" (Jeremiah 31), linking discipleship to healing and social responsibility (Matthew 10:8).

Interpretation traditions differ sharply. Judaism closes the written canon and develops external commentary, centered on the Talmud (c. 550 CE), studied in the Yeshiva. Christianity ranges from literalist inerrancy (the 1978 Chicago Statement applies to the unavailable autographs) to historical-critical readings; Catholics and Orthodox add Sacred Tradition, while many Protestants hold *sola scriptura* (scripture alone) or *prima scriptura* (scripture primary). Islam regards the Bible as originally revealed but corrupted (*tahrif*), with the Quran as correction. Rastafarianism treats it as essential; Unitarian Universalists, as one important text among several.

A working mental model:

- Two canons, one shared core. Judaism's Tanakh equals the Protestant Old Testament by content; Catholicism and Orthodoxy add Septuagint books; Ethiopian Orthodoxy adds more still.
- "Originals" are gone. Every Bible text is a copy of a copy, and translation history (Septuagint, Vulgate, Peshitta, King James, modern critical editions) is part of what the text is.
- The text is a library, not a single voice. A reader is meeting books that disagree, written across vastly different conditions, read as law, history, poetry, prophecy, and fiction.
- Archaeology confirms parts and is silent on others. The Tel Dan Stele mentions the "House of David"; Babylonian destruction layers match 2 Kings 18:13. There is no Egyptian evidence for Israel in Egypt, and no 10th-century remains for David, Solomon, or the First Temple.
- The legacy is double-edged. The same text has inspired abolitionism, civil rights, hospitals, and public education, and has been used to justify slavery, patriarchy, colonialism, and violence, a tension Phyllis Trible's *Texts of Terror* and John Riches's framing of the Bible as a source of both truth and oppression both address.
