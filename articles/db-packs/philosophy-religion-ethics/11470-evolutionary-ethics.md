# Evolutionary ethics

Evolutionary ethics is the study of how evolutionary theory might bear on our understanding of morality. Biology can describe, explain, or seem to justify moral behaviour, and the field splits into three branches that ask different questions.

## The three branches

- **Descriptive** asks why humans have the moral psychology we do, treating morality as a natural phenomenon like language or vision.
- **Normative** asks what we ought to do and tries to ground moral obligation in evolutionary facts.
- **Metaethical** asks what evolution reveals about moral language and the status of objective moral truth.

Descriptive ethics is the least controversial branch. The near-universal human disgust at incest, for example, is read as an evolved adaptation that historically increased survival, not as direct evidence that incest is wrong. The branch covers altruism, fairness, kindness, in-group loyalty, monogamy, parental care, retribution, moral cheating, and hypocrisy.

A central puzzle is altruism. Natural selection favours genes that propagate themselves, so why do organisms help others at a cost? The standard answers are kin selection (helping relatives who share your genes), reciprocal altruism (helping those who return the favour directly or through reputation), and group selection (cooperative groups outcompeting defectors). A separate, unresolved question is whether specific moral feelings are adaptations in their own right or by-products of other adaptations.

Normative evolutionary ethics is the most controversial branch because it tries to derive what we ought to do from what evolutionarily is. Its most famous failure is Social Darwinism, championed by Herbert Spencer and William Graham Sumner in the late nineteenth century, who argued that societies should reject welfare laws and let the "unfit" lose. Critics including Thomas Henry Huxley, G. E. Moore, William James, Charles Sanders Peirce, and John Dewey discredited it on scientific and logical grounds by the early twentieth century. G. E. Moore named the underlying error the *naturalistic fallacy*: defining "good" in terms of a non-normative property such as "survives well." Modern normative ethics need not repeat that mistake, but it still faces the deeper problem of explaining how any descriptive fact about our evolved nature can have normative authority for a rational agent. Just because a trait exists does not mean it ought to be exercised.

Evolutionary metaethics asks what evolution reveals about moral language and the status of moral truth. A prominent argument, defended by Michael Ruse, E. O. Wilson, Richard Joyce, and Sharon Street, treats evolutionary theory as a weapon against moral realism, the view that objective moral facts exist. Street's version runs as follows. Natural selection rewards dispositions that increased reproductive fitness rather than dispositions that track moral truth. If our moral beliefs are shaped by survival pressure rather than contact with moral reality, it would be a remarkable coincidence if "morally blind" traits aligned with independent moral truths. If realism requires that they do, realism forces moral scepticism, which is implausible; therefore realism should be rejected in favour of some antirealist alternative.

Realists have offered two main replies. David Copp argues the dilemma is false because evolution would favour traits promoting social peace and cooperation, which are also what any plausible moral realism says is good. William Fitzpatrick argues that heavy evolutionary influence on moral beliefs does not prevent autonomous moral reasoning, just as mathematical and scientific beliefs can be partly autonomous despite being formed by evolved minds. The wide cross-cultural variability of moral codes is also hard to square with the claim that morality is pervasively shaped by genes.

## HistoryCharles Darwin's *The Descent of Man* (1871) gave the first major treatment, arguing in Chapters IV and V that human conscience evolved from social instincts and that no absolute gap separates us from other animals. The field then split between the Social Darwinist misuse and a longer tradition of biological explanation. E. O. Wilson's *Sociobiology* (1975) launched the modern descriptive revival by arguing for a genetic basis of social behaviour. Richard Alexander, Robert Trivers, and George Williams reframed the question. Alexander argued that ethics arises because conflicts of interest are inevitable among genetically distinct individuals, and that moral systems are societal, not evolved, responses to those conflicts. His account of parent–offspring conflict, in which parents are selected to spread investment equally while each child is selected to demand more, shows why natural selection cannot generate a universal sense of fairness among siblings.

Street's evolutionary argument against moral realism, developed from the late 2000s, is the central live problem in metaethics, because the realists' two defences (convergence on moral truth and autonomy of moral reasoning) remain contested.
