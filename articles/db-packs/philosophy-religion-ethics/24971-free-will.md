# Free will

Free will is the capacity to choose between possible actions in a way that grounds moral responsibility. Philosophers split sharply on whether this capacity survives the truth of determinism, the doctrine that prior events and natural laws fix exactly one future. The dispute, often called the dilemma of determinism, has been argued since ancient Greece and still has no consensus.

Two main camps answer the question. Compatibilists hold that free will is consistent with determinism: as long as you act on your own desires without external coercion, you are free, even if those desires were themselves caused. Incompatibilists deny this. Within incompatibilism, hard determinists say determinism is true, so free will does not exist; metaphysical libertarians say determinism is false, leaving room for genuine alternative possibilities and an agent who is the ultimate originator of actions. A third variant, hard incompatibilism (defended by Derk Pereboom) and the related "pessimism" of Galen Strawson, holds that free will is impossible whether the world is deterministic or not, since any chain of choices requires an uncaused first act of self-creation, which Strawson calls absurd.

The classic incompatibilist case is the consequence argument, formulated by Carl Ginet in the 1960s (with roots in C. D. Broad). If determinism holds, you have no control over the past or the laws of nature; therefore you have no control over their consequences, which include your present choices. Compatibilists such as David Lewis reply that freedom only requires the power to act differently had past circumstances been different, not the power to alter the past itself.

Libertarian accounts divide into non-causal theories (free acts arise as spontaneous volitions rather than from prior events), event-causal theories (indeterminism, for example from quantum mechanics, supplies elbow room in the decision process while selection remains responsive to reasons), and agent-causal theories (a non-physical substance such as a soul intervenes). Robert Kane's efforts-of-will model is a leading event-causal version, treating indeterminacy as an internal obstacle the agent must overcome by effort, a condition Kane calls "ultimate responsibility." A common objection is that randomness cannot create authorship: if a choice is left to chance, no one owns it.

Compatibilists offer several positive accounts. Classical compatibilists such as Thomas Hobbes define free will as the absence of external impediment: you are free if, had you wanted to do otherwise, you could have. Modern compatibilists such as Harry Frankfurt add a psychological layer: freedom requires that your will aligns with your second-order desires about which desires should win, the hierarchical mesh. Daniel Dennett argues in *Elbow Room* and *Freedom Evolves* that, given chaos and limits on knowledge, the future is ill-defined for finite beings, so the ability to act differently from expectation is real and sufficient. Christian List treats free will as a higher-level phenomenon of agency, not a physical one.

The problem has three main deterministic variants, each raising a distinct worry. Causal (nomological) determinism, the scientific picture of unbroken cause-and-effect illustrated by Laplace's demon, says every event is necessitated by prior conditions and laws. Theological determinism holds that a God's infallible foreknowledge or predestination fixes all outcomes; weak theological determinism is compatible with free will only under a "soft" reading, while strong theological determinism is not. Logical determinism holds that propositions about the future already have truth values, making future contingents a puzzle for choice. Biological, cultural, and psychological determinism motivate compatibilist models because they describe internal, not external, causes.

Brain and behaviour research complicates the picture. In Benjamin Libet's 1980s experiments, unconscious brain activity (the readiness potential) preceded the reported moment of conscious intention by about half a second, and the brain registered movement-related activity around 0.2 s before motion, findings often taken to suggest that conscious will is an illusion. Libet himself held that a "conscious veto" remains. Later work by Aaron Schurger and others questions whether the readiness potential is itself a causal build-up. Alien hand syndrome, Tourette tics, and delusions of control in schizophrenia show that the sense of agency can detach from the sense of ownership of an action, while studies of patients lacking the supplementary motor area suggest that voluntary action and the subjective feeling of willing can dissociate. Psychologists such as Daniel Wegner argue that the perception of conscious will is inferred after the fact from cues of priority and consistency, and Emily Pronin documents an introspection illusion whereby people grant themselves more free will than they grant others.

A natural counter to determinism is physical indeterminism from quantum mechanics, but Niels Bohr argued there is no connection between subatomic indeterminacy and freedom of will, and most libertarians grant that quantum randomness cannot by itself confer authorship. The Buddhist doctrine of dependent origination rejects both absolute free will and strict determinism, treating volitions as conditioned rather than originating from a self; the Hindu schools diverge, with Samkhya denying it, some Vedantists such as Madhvacharya denying it on theistic grounds, and others locating freedom beyond ordinary willing. Steven Pinker argues that explaining behaviour through genetics or evolution is explanation, not exculpation, since responsibility only requires that behaviour respond to praise and blame.

Moral responsibility, praise, and blame track whether an action is freely willed, and that link is what keeps the debate live across philosophy, theology, neuroscience, and law.
