# Ethics of care

Ethics of care is a normative ethical theory, developed by feminists and environmentalists from the 1980s onward, that grounds moral action in interpersonal relationships and the virtue of care, rather than in generalizable rules.

## From "what is just?" to "how to respond?"

Two families dominate mainstream Western ethics. **Consequentialism** (exemplified by **utilitarianism**) judges acts by their outcomes. **Deontology** (exemplified by **Kantian ethics**) judges acts by duties such as fairness and rights. Both treat morality as impartial, general principles.

Care ethics rejects that starting point. Its central question is not "what is just?" but "how to respond to this particular person in this particular situation?" Carol Gilligan, the American ethicist and psychologist usually credited as the theory's originator, criticised applying generalised rules as "morally problematic, since it breeds moral blindness or indifference". Three assumptions define the framework: people live in varying degrees of dependence and interdependence; those affected by a choice deserve consideration in proportion to their vulnerability; and situational details determine how to safeguard and promote their interests.

## Origin in a critique of Kohlberg

Gilligan built the theory as a critique of her mentor Lawrence Kohlberg's stages of moral development. On Kohlberg's scale, boys scored as more morally mature than girls, and the gap persisted into adulthood, though when education is controlled for, the gender differences vanish. Gilligan argued the scale was not objective but encoded a masculine perspective built on justice and rights. In her 1982 book *In a Different Voice*, she proposed that women tend to view morality through empathy and compassion, while men tend to view it through abstract duties.

The claim remains contested. Dana Ward judged Kohlberg's scale psychometrically sound, and follow-up studies suggest any care-versus-justice split may reflect gender, life situation, or both, rather than a hardwired sex difference.

## Relation to other traditions

Care ethics aims to recover values excluded from traditional moral philosophy, placing caring and relationship above logic and reason, in deliberate contrast to Kant, for whom actions done out of inclination have no moral worth. Virginia Held has drawn parallels with **virtue ethics** but separated care ethics from the British virtue tradition of Hume by insisting that people are fundamentally relational rather than independent. Jason Josephson Storm has argued for close parallels with traditional Buddhist virtue ethics, especially Śāntideva's prioritisation of compassion. The relationship between care ethics and virtue ethics remains debated.

## Care-focused feminism

Some feminists have accused care ethics of reinforcing traditional gender stereotypes by tying women to nurturing. Others, working under the label care-focused feminism, developed the theory further, most notably Nel Noddings, along with Sara Ruddick, Virginia Held, and Eva Feder Kittay. They treat women's capacity for care as a human strength that should be taught to and expected of men as well as women, and they criticise society for assigning caring labour to women and devaluing it. Ruddick, Held, and Kittay argue that caring should be performed and caregivers valued in both public and private spheres.

## Joan Tronto's ethical qualities

Joan Tronto, who has argued that "ethic of care" is ambiguous because care has not been allowed a central role in moral theory, proposed four qualities of care and added a fifth in 2013.

**Attentiveness** requires recognising others' needs, including the hard distinction between ignorance and inattentiveness. **Responsibility** means taking responsibility oneself, distinguished from obligation, which is tied to pre-set roles and contracts. **Competence** requires following through adequately so that care needs are actually met. **Responsiveness** means attending to the care receiver's response, not one's own imagined reciprocity; responsiveness reveals vulnerability and inequality rather than resolving them. **Plurality, communication, trust, respect, and solidarity** (the fifth quality) are the qualities needed for collective responsibility and for treating citizenship as always embedded in relations of care.

## Beyond the family

Care ethics is often accused of applying only within families and friend groups, but Ruddick, Held, Tronto, and others have argued it extends to politics and citizenship. Theorists contrast it with theories of the "liberal individual" and social contract traced to Locke and Hobbes. Because childhood and many other real situations involve large power imbalances, care, not contract, is the better basis for understanding such relationships. The framework has also been taken up in contemporary art through Mierle Laderman Ukeles's 1969 *Manifesto for Maintenance Art*, which elevated everyday acts of cleaning, repairing, and sustaining to the status of artistic practice, and has shaped socially engaged art and feminist institutional critique.

## Criticisms

Within nursing, Peter Allmark has objected that subjective caring does not guarantee that care is harmless, and that the theory can conflict with treating everyone with unbiased consideration. Care ethics has also been faulted for offering weak protection against paternalism, the risk that caregivers mistake their own needs for those of the people they care for. Ruddick's response is that caregivers should respect the "embodied willfulness" of those receiving care.
