# History of Buddhism

Buddhism is a major world religion founded in the 5th century BCE in northeastern India by Siddhārtha Gautama, the Buddha, whose teachings offer a path to enlightenment and the cessation of suffering. The religion spread over roughly 2,500 years from a small oral movement in the Gangetic Plains to a transcontinental tradition, shaped by royal patronage, doctrinal innovation, and political upheaval.

## The Buddha and the First Community

Siddhārtha Gautama taught in the Gangetic Plains in the 5th century BCE. After his death, his monastic community, the *saṅgha*, preserved his teachings through oral recitation. The community soon split into the Sthavira and Mahāsāṃghika schools, a schism driven mainly by disagreements over monastic discipline (*vinaya*). This first split eventually produced numerous Early Buddhist Schools, each developing its own *Abhidharma* literature, systematic analyses of doctrine.

## Imperial Patronage and Geographic Spread

Royal support carried Buddhism beyond India. The Mauryan Emperor Ashoka (268–232 BCE) promoted Buddhism through edicts (royal proclamations), *stupas* (relic mounds), and missions reaching Sri Lanka and the Hellenistic world. The first anthropomorphic Buddha images appeared in this period in the Greco-Buddhist art of Gandhāra, where Hellenistic and Indian visual traditions met. Later, the Kushan Empire (30–375 CE) helped the Sarvāstivāda school spread Buddhism along the Silk Road into Central Asia and China.

## Doctrinal Evolution: Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna

Two later movements reshaped Buddhist thought. Mahāyāna, the "Great Vehicle," emerged between roughly 200 BCE and 100 CE, emphasizing the *bodhisattva* path, the vow to attain Buddhahood for the benefit of all beings. Vajrayāna, the "Diamond Vehicle," developed under the Gupta (4th–6th centuries CE) and Pāla (8th–12th centuries CE) empires, incorporating tantric practices and rituals as accelerated paths to enlightenment.

## Asian Transmission

Buddhism reached China by the 1st century CE, where it evolved into distinct schools such as Chan (later known as Zen in Japan) and Pure Land. It arrived in Korea in the 4th century and Japan in the 6th century, shaping both cultures. In the 7th century, the Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang traveled to India to study at Buddhist centers including Nālandā, and Buddhism took root in Tibet, where Vajrayāna would later dominate under Pāla influence. In Southeast Asia, both Mahāyāna and Theravāda, the conservative "School of the Elders" aiming for individual liberation as an *arhat*, were present, but Theravāda later became dominant in mainland countries like Thailand and Myanmar through missionary activity from Sri Lanka. By the 13th–14th centuries the Mongol Yuan dynasty adopted Tibetan Buddhism as a state religion.

## Decline in India and the Modern Revival

Buddhism's presence in India waned after the 12th century. In 1193, Turkic raiders burned the university of Nālandā, a major Buddhist intellectual center, marking a broader decline of North Indian Buddhism under Islamic invasions and Hindu revival. The 20th century brought further destruction under communist regimes in China, Tibet, and Mongolia, but also a global revival. Buddhist modernism, emphasizing rationality and meditation, and diaspora communities helped carry the tradition into Europe, the Americas, and beyond.

## Open Historical Questions

Several foundational episodes remain uncertain. Scholars debate the historicity of the first and second Buddhist councils, the precise causes of the initial Sthavira–Mahāsāṃghika schism, the actual extent of Buddhist communities in the Hellenistic world, and the scale of persecution under rulers like Pushyamitra Shunga, whose actions are sometimes described in Buddhist sources as far more violent than the evidence supports.
