# The Buddha

Siddhartha Gautama (c. 6th–5th century BCE), later known as the Buddha ("Awakened One"), was a wandering ascetic and teacher whose insights into suffering and liberation founded Buddhism. Born a prince of the Shakya clan in Lumbini (modern Nepal), he abandoned palace life after encountering the "four sights" of an old man, a sick person, a corpse, and an ascetic. He renounced his family, practiced severe austeries, then rejected them in favor of a Middle Way between indulgence and self-mortification. Seated under the Bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya, he attained **bodhi** (enlightenment), then spent roughly 45 years teaching across the Ganges basin before dying at Kushinagar in an event called **parinirvana**, the final release from conditioned existence.

## Historical Context and Sources

The Buddha lived during the Mahajanapada period, a contemporary of Mahavira, likely under the kings Bimbisara and Ajatashatru of Magadha. He taught amid the rise of **śramaṇa** movements (non-Brahmin ascetic groups that challenged Vedic ritualism) and urbanisation in the Ganges basin, which created audiences for alternatives to Brahmanical religion. No contemporary written records survive. The earliest mentions appear in Ashoka's edicts (c. 268–232 BCE); the earliest surviving manuscripts are the Gandhāran texts (c. 1st century BCE–3rd century CE). Discourses are preserved in the Pali Canon and the Āgamas, while later biographies such as the *Buddhacarita* (1st century CE) and *Lalitavistara Sūtra* (3rd century CE) add mythological layers. Scholars debate the chronology, with "long" dates of 624–544 BCE and "short" dates of 448–368 BCE; most favour c. 563–483 BCE.

## Core Teachings

The central claim is that **dukkha** (suffering, frustration, unsatisfactoriness) is a defining feature of ordinary existence, and that it has a diagnosable cause and a cure.

| Doctrine | Content |
|---|---|
| Four Noble Truths | Dukkha exists; its cause is craving (taṇhā); cessation is possible; the path is the Noble Eightfold Path |
| Noble Eightfold Path | Right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, concentration |
| Dependent origination | Phenomena arise interdependently through a chain of conditions |
| Anatta (non-self) | There is no permanent, unchanging self |
| Five aggregates | Form, sensation, perception, mental formations, consciousness, the components that, in combination, seem to be a self but are impermanent |
| Nirvana | Liberation from rebirth by extinguishing the fires of desire, hatred, and delusion |

The framework is therapeutic: suffering is identified, its origin traced, the goal of freedom declared attainable, and a practical method prescribed. **Dharma** names the Buddha's teachings and, in older usage, the cosmic law they describe. The Middle Way is the operating stance: ethical conduct, mental discipline, and wisdom are cultivated together.

## Biographical Sequence

1. Birth in Lumbini to Suddhodana and Maya of the Shakya clan.
2. Renunciation after the "four sights" exposed the pervasiveness of suffering.
3. Study of meditation under Alara Kalama and Uddaka Ramaputta.
4. Practice of extreme asceticism, abandoned as counterproductive.
5. Enlightenment under the Bodhi tree, where he defeated Mara, a personification of obstacles to liberation.
6. First sermon at Sarnath (the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta), establishing the **saṅgha**, the monastic order of monks and, later, the bhikkhunī order of nuns.
7. Forty-five years of teaching across the Ganges basin.
8. Death and parinirvana at Kushinagar.

## Development of Buddhist Traditions

After his death, Buddhism branched into three major streams. **Theravāda** ("Way of the Elders") is the older tradition, dominant today in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. **Mahāyāna** ("Great Vehicle") emerged centuries later and reinterpreted the Buddha as a cosmic figure rather than a historical teacher. **Vajrayāna** ("Diamond Vehicle") is the tantric tradition of Tibet and Mongolia, building on Mahāyāna.

A key Mahāyāna concept is the **trikāya** doctrine, which describes three "bodies" of the Buddha: the nirmāṇakāya (the physical, historical body), the saṃbhogakāya (an enjoyment body accessible to advanced bodhisattvas), and the dharmakāya (the formless body that is ultimate reality itself). Early texts do not attribute omniscience to the Buddha; later traditions progressively deified him. His figure was absorbed into Hinduism as the ninth avatar of Vishnu, into Christianity as St. Josaphat, and into Islam as Budd or Shakyamuni.

**Karma** (intentional action) shapes future rebirths across these traditions, and the Buddha himself is recorded using the title **Tathāgata** ("one who has thus gone"). The tradition that grew from his teachings diversified to suit very different cultural and philosophical contexts.
