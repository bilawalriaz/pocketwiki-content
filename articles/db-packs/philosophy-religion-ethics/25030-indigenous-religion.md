# Indigenous religion

In the academic study of religion, "indigenous religion" (also "native religion") is a category for the belief systems of communities described as indigenous. It is set against two other categories, "world religions" and "new religious movements", and covers belief systems across the Americas, Australasia, Asia, Africa, and Europe. Most of the world's religions could in fact be classed this way, although indigenous religionists are far outnumbered globally by adherents of world religions.

## How the category is defined

Indigenous religions are usually the localised belief systems of small-scale societies. They do not proselytise, which separates them from Christianity, Islam, and Buddhism, all of which seek converts and are classed as world religions. They are typically oral, tied to traditional ways of life, and pluralist, absorbing multiple influences rather than insisting on one exclusive truth. Some indigenous religions, including those of the Maori, Indigenous Australian, and Canadian First Nations peoples, have nevertheless reached a global audience through music and other cultural exports.

The scholar Carole M. Cusack summarised the exclusion: indigenous religions "are typically this-wordly, orally transmitted, non-proselytizing, folk-oriented, expressed in myths and traditional law, and pluralist."

## Earlier labels and why they were rejected

In the nineteenth century these religions were usually called "primitive religion" or "non-literate religion", on the assumption that they showed how religion was practised by the earliest humans. In the 1970s Andrew Walls of the University of Aberdeen coined "primal religion" to focus on non-Western forms in Africa, Asia, and Oceania. Scholars such as Graham Harvey and James Cox argue that all of these older labels, primitive, primal, and tribal, are problematic. In Cox's view they frame non-Western religions as undeveloped, as a preparation for conversion to Christianity; Harvey adds that such frameworks quietly centre Western, industrialised, Protestant-Enlightenment culture as the standard. The newer term "indigenous religion" grew out of this discomfort with the older vocabulary, but it too remains debated.

## Contested cases

Shinto is often called an indigenous religion, yet the Japan specialist John K. Nelson notes the label is "unclear" in this case. The Japanese were not colonised and instead colonised neighbouring peoples such as the Ainu, so they sit awkwardly inside a category built around colonial experience. Shinto is therefore usually treated as a native religion, the religion of the ethnic group that settled the islands, rather than strictly an indigenous one.

Heathenry, a modern Pagan movement classed by scholars as a new religious movement, has also attracted followers who want to call it indigenous. Some Heathens, especially in the United States, frame themselves as heirs to the pre-Christian belief systems of Germanic communities in Northern Europe and as victims of medieval Christian colonialism and imperialism. The scholars Jennifer Snook, Thad Horrell, and Kirsten Horton have pushed back, noting that most US Heathens are considered white and have therefore both benefited from and helped carry out colonial policies directed at indigenous communities in the Americas and elsewhere. Treating Heathenry as indigenous therefore stretches the category beyond its usual colonial and small-scale-society meanings.

The boundaries of the category keep shifting because the underlying idea of "indigenous" is itself contested and what counts as indigenous changes with history, geography, and politics.
