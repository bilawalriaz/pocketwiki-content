# Justification (epistemology)

Justification, also called *epistemic justification*, is the property a belief has when it meets the norms about what a person ought to believe given their evidence. A justified belief is one the holder is entitled to hold blamelessly. Epistemology treats justification as the standard way to distinguish true belief from knowledge: knowledge is commonly held to be *justified* true belief.

## The JTB tradition and Gettier

Plato's dialogues *Meno* and *Theaetetus* are the historical origin of the "justified true belief" (JTB) account of knowledge. Although Plato himself appears to reject JTB at the end of *Theaetetus*, later tradition treated JTB as the standard answer until Edmund Gettier's counterexamples (the **Gettier problem**) showed that a belief can be true, justified, and still fail to be knowledge. The third condition, justification, became contested from that point on.

## Two conceptions of justification

William P. Alston distinguishes two competing conceptions:

- **Deontological** justification evaluates whether the person fulfilled their epistemic duty, their obligation to believe responsibly. Someone who does their best with bad evidence can still be justified. This conception aligns with **epistemic internalism**, the view that the believer must have internal access to the justifying reasons.
- **Truth-conducive** justification is grounded in having evidence or reasons that make the belief likely true. This conception aligns with **epistemic externalism**, the view that reliable external factors suffice, even without the believer's reflective access to them.

## Sources and structure

Two structural questions drive most debate. The *source* question asks what kinds of input can justify a belief: perceptual experience (sensory evidence), reason, and authoritative testimony are the leading candidates. The *structure* question asks how justified beliefs hang together: must some beliefs be basic and justify others (foundationalism), or does mutual coherence among a web of beliefs suffice (coherentism)?

## Major theories

- **Foundationalism**: basic beliefs justify non-basic ones; the chain stops at foundations.
- **Epistemic coherentism**: a belief is justified by cohering with the overall system of beliefs.
- **Infinitism**: justification requires an infinite chain of reasons, each supported by another.
- **Foundherentism** (Susan Haack): combines fallible foundations with coherence.
- **Internalism**: the believer must have internal access to justifying reasons.
- **Externalism**: outside, reliable sources suffice without internal access.
- **Reformed epistemology** (Alvin Plantinga): beliefs are warranted by proper cognitive function.
- **Evidentialism**: justification depends solely on the evidence for the belief.
- **Reliabilism**: a belief is justified if produced by a reliable process.
- **Infallibilism**: justification requires certainty; being wrong is incompatible with knowledge.
- **Fallibilism**: claims can be justified even though not conclusively provable.
- **Non-justificationism**: knowledge is produced by attacking and refuting claims rather than positively justifying them.
- **Skepticism**: justification, and therefore knowledge, is impossible or undecidable.

## Criticisms

**Robert Fogelin** argues that modern theories of justification resemble Agrippa's five modes (the classical Pyrrhonian argument that every justification regresses, circles, or rests on an unproven assumption) and that contemporary epistemologists have made no real progress against ancient skepticism.

**William P. Alston** denies that "justified" picks out any single epistemically crucial property. On his view, different epistemologists have been emphasising different epistemic desiderata (desirable features of belief, such as consistency, reliability, or explanatory power) under one label, making a unified theory of justification a will-o'-the-wisp.

**Seyyed Jaaber Mousavirad** argues that justification is not necessary for knowledge itself, only for its verification. People routinely hold true scientific, religious, or social beliefs by imitation, without supporting reasons, and those beliefs still count as knowledge. Justification demonstrates or confirms a belief rather than constituting it.

**Jonathan Kvanvig** treats justification as inessential to the value of knowledge, claiming knowledge is no more valuable than true belief once justification is set aside.
