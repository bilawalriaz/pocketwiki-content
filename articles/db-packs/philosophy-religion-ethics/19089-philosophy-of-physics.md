# Philosophy of physics

Philosophy of physics examines the conceptual foundations of physical theories, drawing on metaphysics (what exists), epistemology (how knowledge is grounded and limited), and philosophy of science to clarify what physics says about reality. Contemporary work clusters around three pillars: quantum mechanics, relativity, and statistical mechanics.

## The three pillars

Quantum mechanics raises questions about quantum states, the measurement problem (how observation yields definite outcomes from indefinite superpositions), and whether observers play a special role. Philosophers also analyse entanglement (correlations between separated particles) and nonlocality (correlations that seem to exceed light-speed limits), the quantum-classical boundary, and how to interpret the theory's formalism.

Relativity, both special and general, raises questions about spacetime, simultaneity, causality, and determinism, and whether quantum mechanics and general relativity can be unified. Cosmological consequences of general relativity and the redshift of light from receding galaxies together indicate the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago in the Big Bang.

Statistical mechanics asks how time-symmetric microscopic laws produce irreversible macroscopic behaviour such as heat flowing from hot to cold. This arrow-of-time problem, plus the interpretation of probability in physics, are its central philosophical issues. Probabilities may be epistemic (reflecting ignorance of microstates) or ontic (objective features of the world).

Philosophers also examine physical laws, symmetries (invariances such as under rotation), conservation principles, the role of mathematics, and emerging areas like quantum gravity and quantum information.

## Space and time

In classical mechanics, time is fundamental. Some theories, including loop quantum gravity, treat spacetime as emergent; Carlo Rovelli, a founder of loop quantum gravity, writes, "No more fields on spacetime: just fields on fields." Operationally, time is defined by measurement: the standard second is 9,192,631,770 oscillations of a hyperfine transition in the caesium-133 atom. Space is defined similarly: the standard metre is the distance light travels in vacuum during 1/299,792,458 of a second.

Newton and Galileo held that time is the same for everyone. Einstein's relativity, in Hermann Minkowski's spacetime, showed that rates of time depend on the inertial frame (reference frame moving at constant velocity), eliminating a universally privileged present, and that space and time merge into a four-dimensional spacetime.

## Quantum mechanics: uncertainty, Bell's theorem, and interpretations

The uncertainty principle sets a lower bound on the product of uncertainties for conjugate variables, paired quantities such as position and momentum. Formally, the bound is given by the commutator (a measure of how two operators fail to commute) of the corresponding operators.

Bell's theorem shows that quantum mechanics is incompatible with local hidden-variable theories, theories where particles carry unmeasured "hidden" properties and no influence propagates faster than light. Bell's 1964 paper responded to the 1935 EPR paradox by Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen, which argued quantum theory is incomplete because measuring one particle of an entangled pair appears to collapse its distant partner's state. Bell derived the Bell inequality, a mathematical constraint on the correlations, and showed quantum mechanics predicts violations of it: "If [a hidden-variable theory] is local it will not agree with quantum mechanics, and if it agrees with quantum mechanics it will not be local." Bell tests from 1972 onward have consistently confirmed quantum predictions and ruled out local hidden-variable theories.

The Copenhagen interpretation, principally associated with Bohr and Heisenberg, holds that quantum mechanics is intrinsically indeterministic, with probabilities given by the Born rule (probability of an outcome equals the squared magnitude of the corresponding wavefunction amplitude), that measurement is irreversible, and that no truth attaches to an object except through measurement results. Heisenberg formulated the uncertainty principle at Bohr's institute in March 1927.

The many-worlds interpretation, proposed by Hugh Everett III, rejects wavefunction collapse and treats superpositions as describing many coexisting realities. It is deterministic but faces the unsolved problem of recovering the probabilistic Born rule. Roland Omnès noted that many-worlds cannot be experimentally distinguished from collapse-based accounts; the dispute concerns "the uniqueness of facts."

## Historical roots

Aristotelian physics imagined a finite spherical universe with Earth at its centre; matter composed of earth, water, air, and fire moved naturally toward or away from that centre, and celestial bodies circled in the aether. Movement was defined as change in place. Newtonian physics overturned this: Newton's first law states that every body perseveres in rest or uniform straight-line motion unless compelled by impressed forces, with no natural or inherent motion, and absolute space was a three-dimensional Euclidean space, infinite and without centre. Leibniz, Newton's contemporary, argued from the 1690s onward that space, time, and motion are relative rather than absolute, anticipating Einstein.
