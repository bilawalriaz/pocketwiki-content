# Nicomachean Ethics

The *Nicomachean Ethics* is Aristotle's principal work on ethics, probably composed during his final years in Athens (c. 335–322 BCE). It may be lecture notes rather than a finished treatise, and parts of it overlap with the *Eudemian Ethics* (Books V–VII of the *Nicomachean* match Books IV–VI of the *Eudemian*), which is why scholars still debate whether the two share a common source. What matters is the argument: ethics is a practical science whose subject is *eudaimonia*, the highest human good, usually translated as "flourishing."

## The Highest Good

Aristotle starts from an ordinary observation. Every deliberate action aims at some good: medicine at health, strategy at victory. These goods are nested, so there must be a highest, most complete good pursued for its own sake. That good, he argues, is *eudaimonia*. It is not a feeling of pleasure or a stock of possessions. It is an *energeia*, an ongoing activity, of the soul in accordance with virtue (*aretē*). He defends this with a function argument. Every thing has an *ergon*, a characteristic activity that defines what it is at its best. The human *ergon* is life guided by reason (*logos*), the capacity that separates us from other animals. So *eudaimonia* is the active exercise of the rational soul in conformity with excellence, sustained over a complete lifetime and supported by a moderate supply of external goods such as friends, family, and health.

## Moral Virtue and the Mean

Moral virtues are character traits, not innate ideas and not teachable by lecture. They are acquired the way we acquire skills: by repeated practice, until the right response becomes second nature. Aristotle calls these stable states *hexeis*. Each one is a mean (*mesótēs*) between two vices, one of excess and one of deficiency. Courage sits between rashness and cowardice; temperance (*sōphrosunē*) sits between overindulgence and insensibility to bodily pleasure. The virtuous person does not stumble into the mean by accident. They recognise the right action, choose it for its own sake, and act from a settled disposition.

A few named virtues illustrate the range. Generosity concerns the right use of money; magnificence, the right use of large sums on a fitting scale; magnanimity, sometimes translated "greatness of soul," demands excellence across the virtues rather than superiority in one. Justice (*dikaiosunē*) is unusual. Aristotle treats it as a complete virtue in its relation to others: it splits into distributive justice, the fair allocation of honours and goods according to merit, and rectificatory justice, the correction of unfair gains and losses in transactions. He also introduces *epieikeia*, equity, as a corrective when a strict rule produces an unjust result in a particular case.

## Practical Wisdom

Character without intelligence cannot hit the mean, because the mean is not a fixed midpoint. It shifts with circumstances. Aristotle calls the intellectual virtue that guides action *phronesis*, usually rendered "practical wisdom." It grasps general principles, but it also perceives the salient features of a particular situation, so that the brave person, for example, knows which dangers are worth facing. *Phronesis* is therefore the bridge between knowing what virtue requires and actually doing it. Aristotle's method reflects this. He begins from the considered views of "people of good upbringing" and refines them inductively, because ethics deals with "things that are so for the most part" and cannot be settled with mathematical precision.

## Incontinence and Pleasure

If virtue requires knowledge plus choice, how can someone knowingly do wrong? Aristotle calls this *akrasia*, incontinence or weakness of will. He reconciles it with Socrates' claim that wrongdoing is always involuntary by arguing that the incontinent person's practical knowledge is temporarily overridden by appetite; the proposition "this is wrong" is still held, but the force of desire silences it in the moment. This is not vice, where the wrong action is chosen because it is wrong. Pleasure itself is not the problem. Aristotle treats it as something that accompanies, and is inseparable from, unimpeded activity. Excessive bodily pleasure is the threat, because it warps judgement and pulls action away from the mean.

## Friendship

Books VIII and IX argue that friendship (*philia*) is not a luxury but a necessary condition of *eudaimonia*. Aristotle sorts friendships into three types. Friendships of utility bind people who benefit each other through some exchange; they are common and fragile because the bond dissolves when the benefit ends. Friendships of pleasure rest on mutual enjoyment, such as shared humour, and fade with it. The third type, friendship of virtue, is rare and durable: each friend recognises and loves the other's good character for its own sake, and the relationship lasts because the characters are stable. A virtuous friend is also a kind of mirror, since being active in the presence of friends makes virtuous activity more sustained.

## Contemplation and the Practical Life

In Book X, Aristotle offers what looks like a surprising conclusion. The highest form of *eudaimonia* is *theōria*, contemplative activity, because it most fully exercises *nous*, the intellect, which is the most divine element in us. This life is the most self-sufficient and the most pleasant in itself. But Aristotle immediately qualifies it: humans are political animals, not gods, so the life of practical virtue remains a real, if secondary, form of happiness, and ethical theory is incomplete until it is translated into practice through education and good laws. The *Nicomachean Ethics* therefore ends pointing forward to the *Politics*, where the institutions that shape character are examined. Whether this two-tiered ending is a tension or a deliberate ranking is one of the open questions that has driven the modern revival of virtue ethics, sparked by G.E.M. Anscombe's 1958 essay "Modern Moral Philosophy."
