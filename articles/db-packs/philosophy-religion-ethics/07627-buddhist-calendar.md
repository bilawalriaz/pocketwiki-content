# Buddhist calendar

The Buddhist calendar is a set of lunisolar calendars used across Theravada Southeast Asia and Sri Lanka, mainly to mark religious festivals. It borrows sidereal years from the older Hindu calendar but reconciles them with the Moon using a version of the Metonic cycle, a 19-year cycle that inserts seven extra months. The combination is unusual because the Metonic cycle was designed for tropical years, so the calendar drifts away from the real seasons at roughly one day every 100 years. No coordinated reform has been undertaken.

## Era and epoch

All Theravada systems count years from the Buddha's *parinibbāna* (final death), but traditions disagree on the date. Burmese tradition places it at 13 May 544 BCE, Thai tradition at 11 March 544 BCE, and Sri Lankan tradition at 16 April 545 BCE. The offset between the Buddhist Era (BE) and the Common Era is usually 543 years but can be 542, depending on the year. The Thai solar calendar fixes the difference at exactly 543, so 2569 BE corresponds to 2026 CE.

The Tibetan calendar uses a different epoch, the arrival of the Kālacakra Tantra in 1027 CE, giving 2153 in 2026 CE.

## Months

The year normally contains twelve lunar months alternating between 29 and 30 days, giving 354 days. Because 12 synodic months fall about 11 days short of a solar year, the calendar adds an intercalary month in leap years, always placed after the summer solstice in the Burmese system, and after the vernal equinox in the Arakanese system. Hindu calendars instead insert the extra month whenever the accumulated fraction reaches one. The Burmese calendar also adds a day to the month of Nayon a little more than seven times every 39 years. The basis is a 57-year cycle containing 21 months and 11 extra days, equal to 20,819 days for both lunar and solar counts.

Each month is split into a waxing half (days 1–15, ending at full moon) and a waning half (days 1–14 or 15, ending at civil new moon). The 12 months share Sanskrit names (Caitra, Vaiśākha, Jyaiṣṭha) but local calendars use Burmese, Khmer, Lao, Sinhala, and Thai names such as Tagu, Kason, and Nayon.

A regular Burmese year has 354 days, a small leap year 384 (extra month), and a great leap year 385 (extra month plus an extra day in Nayon). Cambodian, Lao, and Thai systems instead place the intercalary day in a separate year, giving small leap years of 355 days and great leap years of 384.

## New Year and animal cycle

New Year falls when the Sun enters Aries, which in 2013 was 17 April, drifting from about 22 March in 638 CE and April 9–10 in the 17th century. Thailand and Cambodia no longer use the lunisolar date, fixing it to the solar calendar; Myanmar still follows the traditional date.

Cambodian, Lao, and Thai calendars name years in a 12-animal cycle: Rat, Ox, Tiger, Rabbit, Naga, Snake, Horse, Goat, Monkey, Rooster, Dog, Pig. A 10-year numbered cycle runs alongside it; Cambodians commonly identify a year using both, for example 2017 CE as 2561 BE, Year of the Rooster, Nuppasak (Year 9).

## Festivals

The lunisolar calendar is retained mainly for Buddhist holidays, with full-moon days of specific months marking the major events. Vesak, on the full moon of Vesākha in May, celebrates the Buddha's birth, enlightenment, and death, and is a public holiday across Cambodia, Laos, Thailand, Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Malaysia, and Singapore. Vassa, the rains retreat, begins on the full moon of Āsāḷha in July and ends on the full moon of Assayuja in October. Songkran, the Southeast Asian New Year in April, also marks the start of the animal zodiac year in some countries.

## Drift and reform

Because the sidereal year is about 24 minutes longer than the tropical year, the calendar accumulates a one-day error roughly every 60 years and 4 months, which explains the New Year drift from late March to mid-April over 14 centuries. Myanmar's 19th-century Konbaung court tried to fix the problem by adjusting the intercalation schedule, but as a result Burmese calendars can only be published a year or two in advance. Thailand abandoned the lunisolar version for civil use in 1941, realigning its Buddhist Era to the Gregorian calendar; the Thai-style Buddhist calendar (Gregorian plus 543 years) is now the official civil calendar in Thailand and is supported as a locale option in Java 8, iOS, and macOS.
