# Research ethics

Research ethics is the branch of applied ethics that governs how scientific investigation is conceived, carried out, and reported. It spans the integrity of the scientific record and the protection of people, animals, and societies touched by research. The discipline is most fully developed in medicine, where the cost of error is measured in human lives.

Three problem areas define the field. *Scientific misconduct* covers fabrication (inventing data), falsification (manipulating data or methods), and plagiarism (taking others' work or ideas). *Research design choices* such as cherry-picking which experiments to report introduce bias. *Treatment of human and animal subjects* is the historical reason the field exists.

**Why the codes exist.** Twentieth-century abuses built it. Nazi human experimentation produced the 1947 Nuremberg Code, which first required voluntary consent in human-subjects research. The Tuskegee syphilis experiment, in which US public-health researchers withheld effective treatment from Black men with syphilis to observe disease progression, ran from 1932 to 1972 and became a defining scandal on exposure. It directly motivated the 1978 Belmont Report, which crystallised three principles: respect for persons, beneficence, and justice. The 1964 Declaration of Helsinki, issued by the World Medical Association, extended these ideas into a global statement for physicians conducting research and is regularly revised. No single code is universally accepted; Nuremberg, Helsinki, and Belmont form the working canon.

**Scientific integrity as a modern movement.** The idea of professional standards reaches back to Charles Babbage in the nineteenth century, but formal research-integrity policy dates only from the 1990s, after US scandals in the late 1970s exposed the limits of scientific self-regulation. International codes now exist at every level: the European Charter for Researchers (2005), the Singapore Statement (2010), the European Code of Conduct for Research Integrity (2011, revised 2017 and 2023), and the Hong Kong Principles for assessing researchers (2020).

Beyond fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism, researchers have catalogued a wider set of *questionable research practices*: selective reporting, p-hacking (testing many analyses and reporting only the ones that yield significant results), undisclosed conflicts of interest, and other behaviours that may not be deliberate but distort the record. The link to the reproducibility crisis is direct: if a finding cannot be re-derived from its data, it cannot be trusted. From around 2010, integrity policy and *open science* (open publication, data, and code) have fused, because the only way to verify a result is to have the materials to reproduce it. The 2023 European Code formalises this with the rule that data should be "as open as possible, as closed as necessary", guided by the FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). Whether these codes change day-to-day laboratory behaviour remains disputed; written principles often sit uneasily beside the competitive pressures that shape real research.

**Medical ethics and bioethics.** Medicine rests on four unranked values: respect for autonomy (the patient's right to decide), non-maleficence (do no harm), beneficence (actively promote well-being), and justice (fair distribution of benefits and burdens). Conflicts are resolved case by case, sometimes by giving one principle priority, as in involuntary treatment. The narrower medical ethics of earlier centuries is now embedded in *bioethics*, which addresses moral questions raised by biology, medicine, biotechnology, and the life sciences more broadly, including environmental and public-health concerns.

**Clinical research and vulnerable participants.** Clinical-trial participants hold a recognised bundle of rights: informed consent, shared decision-making, privacy, the return of results, and the right to withdraw. *Informed consent* is the core safeguard, requiring adequate understanding and voluntary agreement. Some populations cannot meaningfully give such consent: children, prisoners, soldiers, detained migrants, people with conditions that preclude autonomous decision-making, and anyone for whom the study's inducements could be unfairly persuasive. Children in trials raise especially difficult problems because their assent cannot substitute for fully informed consent.

**Governance.** Day-to-day oversight falls to research ethics committees. In the US these are Institutional Review Boards, established in law by the 1974 National Research Act. The UK uses Research Ethics Committees under the National Research Ethics Service. Canada operates Research Ethics Boards and additionally requires mandatory research-ethics training for students, faculty, and staff. The European Union sets guidelines that member states implement; large bodies like the WHO have their own committees. The persistent criticism, voiced in *Social Sciences & Medicine* in 2009, is that medical research ethics is dominated by *principlism*, the four-principle framework, and that this rule-based approach can crowd out the broader social, cultural, and environmental consequences that newer fields, including information technology, biotechnology, and engineering, increasingly need to address.
