# Phenomenology (philosophy)

Phenomenology is a philosophical method and movement, largely of the early 20th century, that investigates the structure of subjective conscious experience as it is lived, independent of theories about the external world. It turns away from speculation about what lies behind appearances and toward describing how things actually show themselves to a conscious subject. Because the method takes first-person experience as its data, it is not psychology (which studies mental states empirically) and not phenomenalism (which reduces objects to bundles of sensations). Husserl called his version *transcendental phenomenology*, an attempt to find universal logical structures in experience. Other branches, including Heidegger's hermeneutic phenomenology, Merleau-Ponty's embodied phenomenology, genetic phenomenology, and existential phenomenology, share the basic commitment to studying phenomena as they appear but draw different conclusions about what experience reveals.

## Core ideas

**Intentionality.** Borrowed by Husserl from his teacher Franz Brentano (who developed it from Aristotle's *On the Soul*), this is the doctrine that every conscious act is *of* or *about* something. Seeing is seeing *of* a tree, fearing is fearing *of* a loss, imagining is imagining *of* a centaur. There is no bare consciousness; consciousness occurs as the simultaneity of an act and its object, and the object need not exist physically: a memory or fantasy is an intentional object just as much as a perceived thing. Husserl also held that every intentional act carries a *pre-reflective self-consciousness*, an implicit awareness that the experience is mine, prior to any reflective thought about it.

**The epoché and phenomenological reduction.** Husserl's procedure for getting at experience as it is given. The *epoché* is the suspension of the "natural attitude," the everyday confidence that the world exists and that our usual categories fit it. Reality itself is not doubted; the point is to set aside inherited assumptions so that what is directly present in experience can be examined on its own terms. The closely linked *phenomenological reduction* then traces what is given back to the structures of subjectivity that make it given, leading "back" (Latin *re-ducere*) to how the world shows up for a subject at all.

**Eidetic variation.** A method of imaginatively altering a thing's properties to find its *essence* (eidos, Plato's term): those features without which it would not be the thing it is. Husserl acknowledged that essences uncovered this way are sometimes vague and always revisable, but defended the method as genuinely informative.

**Noesis and noema.** From his 1913 *Ideas*, Husserl distinguished the *noesis*, the real intentional act itself (the judging, fearing, loving), from the *noema*, the ideal content or sense the act carries (what is judged, feared, loved). The full noema includes both a meaning and a referent, the object *as meant* in the act, which may or may not coincide with an external thing.

**Evidence and the "Principle of All Principles."** In phenomenology, evidence is not a fact pointing to a proposition but the lived experience of something being present as intelligible. Husserl's Principle of All Principles states that every originary intuition is a legitimizing source of cognition: what is offered in direct experience is to be accepted as it presents itself, within the limits in which it presents itself.

**Empathy and intersubjectivity.** The experience of another person is not inferred from their body; it is constituted by applying the awareness of one's own lived body to another's body as another subjectivity. Intersubjective agreement about a shared world is what allows objectivity to appear at all, and the *lifeworld* (Husserl's *Lebenswelt*) is the intersubjective, taken-for-granted background in which all particular objects and events stand out as meaningful.

## Major figures

**Edmund Husserl** founded the tradition. His *Logical Investigations* (1900/01) introduced the descriptive study of intentional acts; his *Ideas* (1913) developed transcendental phenomenology, including the noesis/noema distinction and the phenomenological reduction.

**Martin Heidegger** redirected the method in *Being and Time* (1927). He rejected what he saw as Husserl's residual subjectivism and placed *Dasein*, human existence, ahead of consciousness. For Heidegger, philosophy must ask about being itself; experience is always shaped by historicality, temporality, and social context, which is why his approach joined phenomenology to hermeneutics, the study of interpretation. Husserl objected that Heidegger raised the question of being but answered it by switching topics to Dasein, amounting to abstract anthropology rather than ontology. Heidegger's later work largely diverges from classical phenomenology.

**Maurice Merleau-Ponty** (*The Phenomenology of Perception*, 1945) drew on Gestalt psychology and Heidegger to develop an embodied phenomenology. His central claim is that the body, not a detached mind, is the primary site of engagement with the world, and that perception discloses a meaningful world that is never completely determinable but still aims at truth.

## Method in practice

Researchers in the social sciences, psychology, health sciences, architecture, and human-computer interaction use phenomenological analysis to recover the structure of lived experience rather than to explain behavior causally. The four-step method summarized by Shaun Gallagher and Dan Zahavi, epoché, phenomenological reduction, eidetic variation, and intersubjective corroboration, is widely cited as the working sequence, though later thinkers modify it. The ongoing tension with the empirical sciences, exemplified by Daniel Dennett's rejection of qualia as scientifically tractable and Liliana Albertazzi's counter that color perception can be empirically universalized, has produced hybrid frameworks such as neurophenomenology and embodied constructivism, whose status as genuinely phenomenological remains contested.
