# Sunni Islam

Sunni Islam is the largest branch of Islam, representing 85–90% of the world's approximately 1.62 billion Muslims (as of 2010). It is defined by the belief that Muhammad did not designate a successor, and that the early community chose his replacement at the Meeting of Saqifa after his death in 632 CE. There, Abu Bakr was confirmed as the first caliph, establishing collective leadership over hereditary rule. This decision is the central historical fact that distinguishes Sunnism from Shia Islam, which holds that Ali, Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law, was the divinely appointed successor.

## The Rashidun and the Roots of Identity

The first four caliphs, Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, and Ali, are collectively revered as the *Rashidun* ("Rightly-Guided Ones"). Their rule spanned 632 to 661 CE, a period marked by rapid expansion and internal fracture. Uthman was assassinated in 656, triggering the First Fitna (civil war), and Ali's son Husayn was killed at the Battle of Karbala in 680. Despite these conflicts, Sunnis venerate all four as exemplars of legitimate leadership.

The name *Sunni* derives from *sunnah*, the recorded practices and teachings of Muhammad. The phrase *ahl as-sunna* ("people of the Sunna") emerged as a marker of orthodoxy during the Battle of Siffin, when Ali's supporter Malik al-Ashtar invoked prophetic tradition against Muawiya. Early scholars like Abu Hanifa used the related phrase *ahl al-'adl wa-ahl as-sunna* ("people of righteousness and the Sunna"), framing sunnah adherence as a mark of correctness.

## Sources of Authority

Sunni Islam derives its framework from three foundational sources: the Quran, the *hadith* (recorded sayings and actions of Muhammad, especially the canonical Six Books), and *ijma* (scholarly consensus). These form the basis of *sharia* (Islamic law). A fourth source, *qiyas* (analogical reasoning), is accepted by the four legal schools and extends rulings to new cases.

## Jurisprudence: The Four Schools

By the 9th century, Sunni jurisprudence (*fiqh*) had crystallized into four *madhabs* (legal schools), each named after its founding scholar:

| School | Founder (d.) | Methodological Emphasis |
|--------|-------------|------------------------|
| Hanafi | Abu Hanifa (767) | Reason (*ra'y*) and preference |
| Maliki | Malik ibn Anas (795) | Medinian custom and public interest |
| Shafi'i | al-Shafi'i (820) | Strict textual evidence |
| Hanbali | Ahmad ibn Hanbal (855) | Hadith and literal text |

These schools historically competed but eventually recognized one another's validity. The Ottoman Empire later formalized the four-school system as a counterweight to Shia Safavid influence, a structure largely maintained today. In 19th-century South Asia, the Barelvi movement synthesized Hanafi law with Maturidi theology and Sufi devotional practice, presenting itself as a continuation of pre-Salafi tradition.

## Theology: Three Approaches

Sunni theology (*kalam*) developed three approaches, all affirming *tawhid* (God's absolute oneness) but diverging on reason. The Ash'ari school, founded by Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (d. 935), rejected Mu'tazilite rationalism while affirming that God's attributes "best befit His Majesty." The Maturidi school, established by Abu Mansur al-Maturidi (d. 944), allowed reason to evaluate hadith but treated revelation as decisive beyond human intellect. The Athari (textualist) school, rooted in hadith scholarship and exemplified by Ibn Hanbal, rejected speculative theology in favor of literal acceptance of scripture (*tafwid*).

Whether all three qualify as fully Sunni remains contested. Modern Salafis, reviving Athari textualism, are sometimes excluded by Ash'ari-Maturidi institutions, and Salafis claim the same exclusive authenticity for themselves. Al-Azhar University has upheld the Ash'ari-Maturidi synthesis as normative, and the Turkish Diyanet promotes a similar state-sanctioned orthodoxy. These tensions surfaced at the 2016 Grozny and Kuwait conferences, which debated whether Salafis fall within *Ahl al-Sunna wa-l-Jama'ah* (the mainstream Sunni community).

## Six Pillars of Faith and the Salaf

Sunni creed rests on six pillars of faith: belief in God, angels, revealed scriptures, the prophets, the Day of Judgment, and divine decree. The *salaf*, the pious predecessors of the first three generations, serve as normative models, a reverence that directly inspires Salafi movements today.

## Modern Transformations

The abolition of the Ottoman Caliphate in 1924 by Mustafa Kemal Atatürk removed the last institution claiming universal caliphal authority. Responses ranged from India's Khilafat Movement to the rise of Saudi Arabia under a doctrine rooted in Ibn Taymiyyah's textualism, later labeled Wahhabism, and to 20th-century political Islamism exemplified by the Taliban and ISIL. These movements have often clashed with traditional institutions, highlighting an enduring tension over who defines legitimate Sunni identity.

The phrase "Sunni" itself was popularized in its modern sense by Muhammad Rashid Rida's 1928–29 treatise, reflecting how the tradition's self-understanding continues to be actively negotiated.
