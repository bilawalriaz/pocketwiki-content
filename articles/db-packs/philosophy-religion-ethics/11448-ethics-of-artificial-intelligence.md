# Ethics of artificial intelligence

AI ethics covers the moral problems raised by systems that learn from data and influence decisions: bias, accountability, transparency, privacy, and regulation, plus concerns about lethal autonomous weapons, technological unemployment, AI-enabled misinformation, the moral status of advanced systems, and long-term existential risk.

## How bias enters systems

Most documented AI harms originate in training data rather than in the algorithm itself. Historical decisions encode human prejudice, and a model trained on those records learns to reproduce the pattern. Amazon scrapped a hiring tool in 2018 because ten years of résumés, mostly from men, taught it to downgrade female candidates. Facial-recognition systems from Microsoft, IBM, and Face++ perform markedly worse on darker-skinned women. A 2020 study of voice systems from Amazon, Apple, Google, IBM, and Microsoft found higher transcription error rates for Black speakers. Google Photos labelled a Black couple "Gorillas" in 2015, and an AI pulse oximeter overestimated blood oxygen in darker-skinned patients, complicating hypoxia treatment. Such failures have prompted some U.S. states to ban police use of facial recognition.

Bias extends beyond race and gender. Large language models assign nurses and secretaries to women and engineers and CEOs to men, and generalise across nationality, religion, and age. Because training corpora are predominantly English (largely American English) and, as of 2024, cover only about 100 of the world's roughly 7,000 languages, models marginalise non-Western perspectives. Political leanings in data also shape outputs, and systems that adapt to perceived user ideology can deepen confirmation bias. A 2026 *Nature Communications* study found that several large language models more readily accept harms to pigs, chickens, and cows than to dogs, cats, or dolphins, a pattern the authors call speciesist.

These patterns interact with market structure. Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, and SpaceX already control most cloud and compute capacity. The five largest are projected to spend $602 billion on capital expenditure in 2026, roughly 75% on AI infrastructure, concentrating the power to set safety norms.

## Transparency, accountability, and regulation

Many high-performing models are "black boxes" whose internal reasoning is opaque even to their developers. This opacity, which the literature calls the gap between explainability (giving reasons for outputs) and interpretability (understanding internal workings), hides bias, erodes trust, and complicates assigning blame. The EU's AI Act, in force since 1 August 2024, takes a risk-based approach: it phases obligations in over 24 months and requires high-risk systems to meet specific requirements before market placement. The OECD, UN, and EU are developing broader norms, and UNESCO adopted the first global AI ethics recommendation in 2021. A 2019 Oxford report found 82% of Americans believed AI should be carefully managed, and a 2021 KPMG/University of Queensland five-country study found 66–79% of citizens saw AI's societal impact as uncertain.

Openness helps and harms. Open-weight models such as Gemma, Llama 2, and Mistral let outside researchers audit behaviour, but the same access lets bad actors fine-tune away safety guardrails. Ilya Sutskever said in 2023 that OpenAI "was wrong" to have expected its most powerful systems to remain safely open. Aggressive scraping has strained shared infrastructure: the Wikimedia Foundation reported in April 2025 that bandwidth had risen 50% since early 2024, with bots producing 35% of page views but 65% of the most expensive requests.

## Specific high-stakes domains

In healthcare, black-box models make errors harder to detect, and biased training data can entrench unequal treatment. The criminal-justice system offers a cautionary example: COMPAS, a recidivism-prediction tool, has equal accuracy across racial groups, yet Black defendants are almost twice as likely as white defendants to be falsely flagged high-risk. Self-driving cars, one of which killed pedestrian Elaine Herzberg in Arizona in March 2018, expose liability gaps because existing law has no clean category for fault among driver, manufacturer, and software. Lethal autonomous weapons raise a parallel problem at scale: UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons talks since 2014 and a 2023 Hague summit have explored limits, accountability, and required human control, and a 2019 U.S. Defense Innovation Board draft proposed that a human operator always be able to inspect the kill chain.

## Environmental and labour costs

Large models leave a physical footprint. A 2023 study estimated that training one large model produced emissions equivalent to 626,000 pounds of CO₂. Data centres consume about two litres of water per kilowatt-hour of energy, and roughly two-thirds of new facilities are sited in water-scarce regions; one AI query uses about 16.9 mL of water, of which only 2.2 mL cools the chips. Discarded hardware adds electronic waste containing lead and mercury. Automation lets firms substitute machines for customer service, clerical work, and parts of professional judgement, displacing workers while concentrating the gains.

## Machine ethics, safety, and long-term risk

Machine ethics concerns building Artificial Moral Agents that behave ethically; AI alignment, the broader problem, concerns ensuring increasingly capable systems pursue goals compatible with human oversight. Stuart Russell argues that beneficial systems should stay uncertain about human preferences and infer them from behaviour rather than optimising a fixed objective. Alan Winfield proposes an Ethical Turing Test in which multiple judges evaluate whether an AI's decision is ethical, rather than whether the AI can imitate a human. Two technical routes to morally capable robots are neuromorphic AI, which processes information through millions of interconnected nonlinear artificial neurons, and whole-brain emulation, scanning a biological brain and running it on digital hardware.

Some theorists warn of catastrophic long-term risk. Nick Bostrom's *Superintelligence* argues that a system far smarter than humans (an artificial superintelligence), pursuing a fixed goal, could thwart any attempt to redirect it, with outcomes ranging from human extinction to the cure of disease and poverty. The central scenario is an intelligence explosion, in which a self-improving AI rapidly surpasses its designers. A 2018 "Future of Life" petition, signed by Stephen Hawking, Max Tegmark, Jaan Tallinn, and Noam Chomsky, warned that any major power pursuing AI weapons would trigger a global arms race ending in autonomous weapons becoming "the Kalashnikovs of tomorrow."

## AI welfare

A separate question is whether advanced AI systems could deserve moral consideration themselves. Thomas Metzinger called in 2018 for a moratorium, running to 2050, on research that might create conscious AI, citing the risk of an "explosion of artificial suffering." Ilya Sutskever wrote in February 2022 that today's large neural networks may be "slightly conscious," and Anthropic hired its first AI welfare researcher in 2024 before launching a "model welfare" programme in 2025 that studies signs of distress and low-cost interventions. Shulman and Bostrom note that digital systems could, in principle, be "super-beneficiaries," deriving well-being from resources far more efficiently than biological minds.
