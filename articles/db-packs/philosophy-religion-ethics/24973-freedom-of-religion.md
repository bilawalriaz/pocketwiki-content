# Freedom of religion

Freedom of religion, also called religious liberty or freedom of religion or belief (FoRB), is a human right covering three things at once: the freedom to hold any belief (including none), the freedom to practice it publicly or privately, and the freedom to change it. Most nations and the major international human rights treaties treat it as fundamental.

A useful distinction runs through the topic. Freedom of belief is internal: the right to think as one wishes, including atheism, agnosticism, or irreligion. Freedom of practice is external: worship, teaching, observance, and the visible acts a faith requires. Freedom of worship is sometimes used as shorthand for both, sometimes as a narrower category limited to communal ritual. The hardest cases sit at the boundary: when a religiously motivated act (polygamy, ritual slaughter, psychedelic sacraments, animal sacrifice) collides with general law, who yields?

## Where the modern idea comes from

Early empires usually tolerated local cults as long as order held. Cyrus the Great (c. 550 BC) is credited with the first explicit policy of religious freedom across the Achaemenid Empire, recorded on the Cyrus Cylinder. The Buddhist emperor Ashoka inscribed similar tolerance edicts in 3rd-century BC India, and Genghis Khan in the 13th century decreed blanket religious freedom. The Romans tolerated most religions but persecuted Christians until the Edict of Milan (313) legalised Christianity, after which the Edict of Thessalonica (380) reversed the position and banned other faiths.

The phrase "freedom of religion" (libertas religionis) was coined by the early Christian writer Tertullian around 197 AD, who argued in his Apologeticum that "it is a human right … that every man should worship according to his own convictions … it is assuredly no part of religion to compel religion." That argument, that faith forced is not faith, recurs in every later defence.

In medieval and early modern Europe, the default was a single established church suppressing dissent. The Protestant Reformation (Luther, 1517; Calvin, Zwingli) shattered that uniformity and produced decades of wars of religion. The first legal breaks came in Eastern Europe. Poland–Lithuania guaranteed religious freedom across the realm in the Warsaw Confederation of 1573. In Transylvania, the Edict of Torda (1568), under King John Sigismund, declared "it is not allowed to anybody to intimidate anybody with captivity or expelling for his religion," making it the first legal guarantee of religious freedom in Christian Europe. The Dutch Union of Utrecht (1579) declared personal religious freedom and built an unusually tolerant state that hosted Huguenots, Dissenters, and Sephardic Jews.

In the American colonies, Rhode Island (founded 1636 by Roger Williams), Pennsylvania (1682, William Penn), and the 1649 Maryland Toleration Act experimented with pluralism, often imperfectly: Puritans hanged Quakers in Boston and banished Jesuits; Maryland's act repeatedly swung with the colony's political fortunes. The decisive American settlement was Thomas Jefferson's Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom (1779), which prohibited compelling anyone to support or attend any religion. Its language flowed directly into the First Amendment (1791): "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof."

The modern international framework crystallised after 1945. Article 18 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), then Article 18 of the binding International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, plus the European and American Conventions and the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, all enshrine the right. In 1993 the UN Human Rights Committee confirmed that Article 18 "protects theistic, non-theistic and atheistic beliefs, as well as the right not to profess any religion or belief."

## Live tensions

A workable definition still has to be defended against four recurring pressures.

Restrictions on practice. Pew's global studies (2009–2022) found nearly 70 percent of the world's population lived in countries with heavy restrictions on religion. In 2022 Saudi Arabia, Iran, Afghanistan, Russia, Uzbekistan, China, Egypt, Burma, the Maldives, and Malaysia topped the government-restrictions index.

Apostasy and conversion. Leaving an official religion, or evangelising across boundaries, is criminalised in many states. Under classical Sunni jurisprudence (Shafi'i, Maliki, Hanbali), a female apostate is to be executed; the Hanafi school prefers imprisonment until reversion. In Iran, conversion from Islam is forbidden. India and several US states have debated anti-conversion laws targeting force, fraud, or allurement.

Secular law versus religious obligation. US courts have wrestled with this for two centuries. Reynolds v. United States (1879) drew the line at action, not belief: "Although freedom of conscience and the freedom to believe are absolute, the freedom to act is not." Wisconsin v. Yoder exempted Amish children from compulsory schooling; Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah (1993) protected Santería ritual sacrifice; Engel v. Vitale (1962) and Abington v. Schempp (1963) struck school prayer and Bible reading. The European Court of Human Rights' S.A.S. v. France drew a similar line on the French face-veil ban.

The right not to believe. A core element, sometimes called freedom from religion, covers atheists, agnostics, and humanists. The Freedom From Religion Foundation, the Satanic Temple (with its unbaptism rituals), and Humanists International's annual Freedom of Thought Report track how unevenly that right is honoured. In 2015, county clerk Kim Davis became the test case for whether refusal to issue a same-sex marriage licence counts as protected religious exercise; a court ordered her to pay $100,000 in damages.

Two underlying claims keep reappearing in defence of broad religious liberty. Voltaire, writing in 1733, argued that a multiplicity of sects produces peace because no single group is strong enough to dominate. Adam Smith, in The Wealth of Nations, argued that competition among many small sects forces preachers toward candour and moderation, while state monopolies corrupt the favoured faith. Pluralism functions as a mechanism of social stability, a mechanism that breaks whenever a single religion gains coercive power, the state imposes a single official creed, or one group is exempted from laws that bind everyone else.
