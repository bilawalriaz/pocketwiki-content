# Gutenberg Bible

The Gutenberg Bible, printed by Johannes Gutenberg in Mainz during the 1450s, was the first major book in Europe produced with mass-produced metal movable type. Its appearance marks the start of the "Gutenberg Revolution," the shift from hand-copied manuscripts to printed books in the West.

## A book, not a translation

The Gutenberg Bible is a printed edition of the Latin Vulgate, St. Jerome's Latin translation of the Bible, which was the standard biblical text in Western Christianity. Gutenberg's contribution was the means of producing it, not the text. His workshop process, which he called *Das Werk der Bücher* ("the work of the books"), worked as a loop: dampen paper so it absorbs ink, press it onto inked metal type, lift it off, re-ink the type, and repeat. This is relief printing, in which only the raised surface of the type contacts the paper, and it made identical copies cheap once the type was set.

Two technical problems had to be solved for this loop to work. Water-based manuscript ink would not stick to metal, so Gutenberg developed a durable, oil-based, varnish-like ink with high metallic content (copper, lead, titanium) that adhered to the type. And because paper was expensive, the type had to be designed so whole pages could be locked together, inked, and printed in one pass, requiring 290 master characters, including ligatures (two-letter combinations cast as a single piece, such as "fi"), to fill a line evenly.

## How the book was laid out

The Bible was printed on "Royal" paper, 42 cm × 60 cm at full sheet, with a width-to-height ratio of about 1:1.4. A complete copy has 1,288 pages and is usually bound in two volumes. Early pages carried 40 lines per column; later pages carried 42, to save paper, so some folios exist in two distinct settings. The type uses blackletter (Textura and Schwabacher), the dense, angular script familiar from medieval manuscripts. To make both edges of each column line up, a process called justification, Gutenberg varied the width of individual letters and the spacing between them.

Decoration was split between press and hand. Some red headings and initials, called rubrics, were initially printed, but this was soon abandoned; blank spaces were left for a scribe to fill in by hand. The wide margins made room for buyers to commission illuminated decoration, hand-painted ornament in gold and colour, if they wished. Copies printed on vellum (high-quality animal skin, costlier than paper) were more expensive and usually more heavily decorated. Nine surviving copies still carry their original 15th-century bindings, mostly from Mainz or Erfurt.

## Timeline and aftermath

- c. 1450: Preparation likely begins.
- 1454: First finished copies available; by October 22 Gutenberg has also printed a 31-line Indulgence, a cheaper test of his press.
- March 1455: Future Pope Pius II reports seeing pages displayed in Frankfurt.
- 1456: The earliest visible print date on a surviving copy is 15 August.

The print run sold out quickly. Copies reached as far as England, and at about 30 florins, roughly 100 g of gold, they were cheaper than a manuscript Bible but still far beyond ordinary readers. Buyers were monasteries, universities, and wealthy individuals, often donating the book to a church or library.

## Influence, survival, and unknowns

The Bible set the pattern for later printed Bibles such as the 36-line Bible and the first Eggestein Bible, and shaped the official Clementine Vulgate commissioned by the Papacy in the late 16th century. As of 2009, 49 copies are known, 25 substantially complete, with fragments representing about 16 more; most now sit in university libraries. A two-volume copy stolen from Moscow State University in 2009 was recovered in 2013, and high-quality digital reproductions, including projects at Keio University, have made the book accessible without handling the originals.

The exact number of copies printed is uncertain: historical sources give 158 or 180, while modern scholars estimate 160–185. The precise duration of the printing is also unknown.
