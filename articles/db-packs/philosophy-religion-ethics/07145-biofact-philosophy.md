# Biofact (philosophy)

In philosophy and sociology, a **biofact** is a being that is simultaneously an artifact and a living being, or both natural and artificial. The term is a neologism that fuses the Greek *bios* (life) with *artifact*. A biofact comes into existence through purposive human action, such as breeding or genetic engineering, but continues to exist through biological growth. Some sources cite products of genetic engineering as examples.

## Origin of the term

The earliest known use of the word appears in a 1943 German article, "Biofakt und Artefakt," by the Austrian protozoologist Bruno M. Klein. Writing in *Mikrokosmos* and addressing both microscopy and philosophy, Klein used *Biofakt* for a visible dead product that an organism produces while still alive, such as a shell. His classification ran along the axes of biotic versus abiotic and dead versus alive, not nature versus technology or growth versus manufacture.

The contemporary philosophical sense is associated with the German philosopher Nicole C. Karafyllis, who introduced it from 2001 and consolidated it in her 2003 edited volume *Biofakte: Versuch über den Menschen zwischen Artefakt und Lebewesen*. For Karafyllis, biofact is a hermeneutic concept, an interpretive frame for comparing nature and technology within the domain of the living.

## Why the concept matters

Karafyllis introduces the term to resist the habit of subsuming all living beings under "nature." A plant shaped over centuries by breeding, or an organism created by genetic engineering or cloning, shows that growth alone no longer separates the natural from the technical. The biofact carries signatures of both culture and technique and sits at an ontological intersection.

The concept has consequences across several branches of philosophy:

- Philosophy of technology. Biofacts raise the question of whether biotechnology and agriculture belong inside a general theory of technique, beyond the usual focus on machines and artifacts, and whether concepts of design and construction must be reformulated for living things. Karafyllis argues that biofacts depend on a method of provocation, in which an organism is deliberately pushed into a new developmental trajectory.
- Philosophy of nature and biophilosophy. If naturalness is not self-explanatory, the categories of organism and living being must be re-examined.
- Philosophy and sociology of science. The concept calls for historicizing the technicality, mediality, and materiality through which a living object comes into being, and frames questions about scientific expertise, since biofacts are made as scientific objects yet released into the lifeworld and public sphere.
- Phenomenology, anthropology, and ontology. Because biofacts involve growth and the establishment of a self, they bear on how living beings appear in experience. Jürgen Habermas, in *Die Zukunft der menschlichen Natur* (2001), stressed the anthropological stakes of blurring the distinction between "coming into being" and "making."
- Technoscience critique. The term equips philosophers to question programmes that frame the union of scientific knowledge and the technical creation of "technonature" as political progress.

## Biofacts versus ordinary artifacts

Artifacts are artificial objects not found in nature. Biofacts are partially man-made yet still grow, produced for a defined utility. They are biotic artifacts whose hybrid character can be read from biological, technical, cultural, and political perspectives.

## A typology of biofacts

Karafyllis complements the growth and reproduction of organisms with their technical shaping and proposes these categories:

| Category | Relation to human technique |
|---|---|
| Natural life form | No significant human intervention |
| Unaltered biofact | Cultivated or kept, genetically unchanged |
| Reshaped biofact | Form altered by selection, breeding, or training |
| Genetically reproduced biofact | Produced through cloning or similar reproduction |
| Genetically modified biofact | Genome directly engineered |

The "unaltered biofact" row captures a subtlety: a wildflower transplanted into a garden is still the same organism genetically, but its move into a technical context is enough to make it a biofact in Karafyllis's sense.

## Reception and limits

The term has been taken up in BioArt, though not always with the critical edge Karafyllis intended. Its main influence remains in German-language philosophy of technology and nature, with English-language uptake concentrated in work on biotechnology, synthetic biology, and the politics of scientific knowledge.
