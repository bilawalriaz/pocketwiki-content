# Consciousness

Consciousness is the state of being aware of one's internal thoughts and external surroundings. It is studied across philosophy, science, and medicine, yet it has no single agreed definition, no consensus on how to investigate it scientifically, and no settled account of how it relates to the brain.

## Two Meanings, One Word

The English words "conscious" and "consciousness" entered the language in the 17th century, derived from the Latin *conscius* ("knowing with") and *conscientia*, shared knowledge with moral weight. In 1690, John Locke defined consciousness in his *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* as "the perception of what passes in a man's own mind," anchoring the modern sense of inner awareness. William James, in his 1890 *Principles of Psychology*, popularised the "stream of consciousness" metaphor to capture its continuous, shifting quality.

Today philosophers usually split the term into two components. **Phenomenal consciousness** is the raw feel of experience, the "what it is like" to taste wine, see red, or feel pain. These particular subjective instances are called **qualia**. **Access consciousness** is information globally available in the mind for reasoning, reporting, and controlling behaviour. Most debate centres on the phenomenal side, because it is hard to see how physical brain activity could produce felt experience in the first place.

## The Mind-Body Problem

The deepest question in the philosophy of mind is how mental phenomena relate to physical brain events. In the 17th century, René Descartes proposed **mind-body dualism**: consciousness belongs to an immaterial substance (*res cogitans*) distinct from the physical body (*res extensa*). Most contemporary philosophers reject dualism and favour **physicalism**, the view that the mind is the brain, made entirely of matter. Two related puzzles remain. The **problem of other minds** asks how anyone can know that other people, let alone animals or machines, are conscious at all, since each of us only directly experiences our own awareness. The **hard problem** of consciousness asks why and how physical processes give rise to subjective experience at all, rather than running in the dark.

## Studying Consciousness Scientifically

Mainstream science avoided consciousness for most of the 20th century because of its subjective character. Since the 1980s, a field of **Consciousness Studies** has grown, using tools like fMRI and EEG to find **neural correlates of consciousness (NCC)**, the brain-activity patterns that track conscious states. Because researchers cannot directly see another mind, they rely heavily on **verbal reports**: a subject's spoken or written description of what they experience. A key finding is that higher brain regions, especially the prefrontal cortex, and "top-down" neural activity, where the brain interprets incoming signals, predict conscious experience more reliably than raw sensory areas alone.

## Four Leading Theories

Several competing models try to explain how brains produce consciousness. **Global Workspace Theory (GWT)**, proposed by Bernard Baars in 1988, holds that information becomes conscious when it is broadcast across a shared "global workspace" and made available to many specialised, otherwise unconscious brain processes. **Integrated Information Theory (IIT)**, developed by Giulio Tononi from 2004, postulates that consciousness is identical to a system's capacity to integrate information, measured by a quantity called **Phi (Φ)**; once integration crosses a threshold, experience appears. **Orchestrated Objective Reduction (Orch-OR)** is a quantum-mechanical theory that locates consciousness in quantum events inside neural **microtubules**. **Attention Schema Theory** argues that the brain builds a simplified internal model of its own attention, and that this model is what we call awareness. None of these theories has won general acceptance.

## Consciousness in Medicine

In clinical practice, consciousness is treated as a level of arousal and responsiveness, running from coma to full alertness. The **Glasgow Coma Scale** scores patients from 3 to 15, where 3 to 8 indicates coma and 15 means fully conscious. Recognised **disorders of consciousness** include the minimally conscious state, the persistent vegetative state, and brain death. A striking condition called **anosognosia** leaves stroke patients unaware of their own disabilities, showing that belief in one's own state and the state itself can come apart in the brain.

## Beyond Humans and Toward Machines

In 2012, the **Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness** stated that convergent evidence shows non-human animals, including all mammals and birds, and many other creatures, possess the neural substrates for consciousness and the capacity for intentional behaviour. In children, **autobiographical consciousness**, the ability to reflect on one's own past and future, is thought to emerge around ages 5 to 7, alongside language and a **theory of mind**, the understanding that others have their own beliefs and perspectives.

Whether machines could be conscious is unsettled. Alan Turing's 1950 **Turing test** asks whether a machine can imitate human conversation convincingly enough to be indistinguishable from a person. John Searle's **Chinese Room argument** counters that convincingly simulating understanding is not the same as genuinely having it, because symbols can be manipulated without any comprehension behind them.

## The Mental Model

Consciousness sits at the intersection of three questions with no shared answer: what it is, how the brain produces it, and who else has it. Definitions split into raw feel (phenomenal) and reportable, usable information (access). The hard problem is why physical processes yield subjective experience at all, a gap that physicalism, the leading philosophical view, has yet to close. Neuroscience maps the brain activity that tracks consciousness, especially in higher, integrative regions, while a handful of theories (GWT, IIT, Orch-OR, Attention Schema) offer competing accounts. Medicine treats consciousness as a graded clinical state, animals share the underlying neural machinery, and the prospect of conscious machines remains an open philosophical and empirical frontier.
