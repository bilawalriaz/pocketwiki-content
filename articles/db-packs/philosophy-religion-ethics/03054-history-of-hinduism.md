# History of Hinduism

Hinduism is not a single, unified religion with a single founder. It is a diverse family of traditions native to the Indian subcontinent, a synthesis of older cultures that took shape roughly around the beginning of the Common Era. Its history is a long process of assimilation, philosophical development, and adaptation, evolving from ancient Vedic ritual into the spectrum of beliefs and practices followed by over a billion people today.

## Origins: The Vedic Foundation

The earliest identifiable layer is the Vedic religion of the Indo-Aryans (c. 1750–500 BCE), a sacrificial system built on hymns to nature deities (devas) and the cosmic order known as ṛta. These hymns were collected in the Rigveda and related texts, which orthodox tradition later classified as Śruti, literally "heard" revelation and the highest scriptural authority.

By the 9th–8th centuries BCE, the later Upanishads shifted the focus from ritual to philosophy, introducing two ideas that would shape all later Hindu thought: Brahman, the ultimate unchanging reality, and ātman, the individual self. Together they form the basis of Vedanta, the tradition that asks how the self relates to ultimate reality. Alongside this Vedic stream ran indigenous traditions, including those of the Indus Valley Civilization (c. 3300–1700 BCE), whose seals hint at proto-Hindu iconography, and Śramaṇa, or "renouncer," movements that rejected Vedic authority altogether.

## The Hindu Synthesis

Between roughly 200 BCE and 300 CE, Brahmanical Hinduism faced a challenge from two Śramaṇa rivals, Buddhism and Jainism, which attracted followers by rejecting caste and ritual. The response was the "Hindu synthesis": Brahmins broadened their services, absorbing local traditions and Śramaṇa ideas into a new Brahmanical framework expressed in smriti literature. Smriti, meaning "remembered," refers to secondary texts such as the great epics (the Ramayana and Mahabharata, which contains the Bhagavad Gita) and law codes, authoritative but inferior to the revealed Śruti.

This synthesis also coincided with the "Second Urbanisation" (c. 600–200 BCE), a wave of city growth in the Ganges plain that provided the social base for new religious movements.

## The Classical and Puranic Periods

The Gupta Empire (c. 320–650 CE) is often called a "Golden Age." Sanskrit literature flourished, and the six orthodox schools of Hindu philosophy were codified. In South India, the Bhakti movement, emphasizing personal devotion to a god rather than ritual, began to take root.

After roughly 650 CE, Puranic Hinduism became the dominant popular form. The Puranas, texts containing myths, legends, and genealogies of gods, heroes, and kings, disseminated a mainstream ideology across diverse populations and supported the rise of major devotional sects devoted to Vishnu and Shiva. The late-classical period also saw Buddhism decline in its Indian homeland.

The most influential philosopher of this era was Shankara (8th century CE), who systematized Advaita Vedanta. His core claim is that ātman and Brahman are identical, and that the everyday world of separation and change is māyā, illusion. Later thinkers such as Ramanuja reacted against this abstract monism with devotional theism, in which the soul remains distinct from a personal God.

The framework behind rebirth, shared across Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain thought, rests on three linked ideas: saṃsāra, the cycle of birth, death, and rebirth; karma, the principle that actions in this life shape future existences; and dharma, the cosmic law or duty that guides righteous living. The goal is moksha, liberation from rebirth.

## Islamic Rule and the Bhakti Response

From the 13th century onward, Muslim conquests introduced new political and religious pressures. The Mughal Empire (1526–1857) alternated between tolerance and intolerance; rulers such as Aurangzeb are associated with temple destruction, while other periods saw coexistence and cultural exchange. Hindu political powers including the Vijayanagara Empire and, later, the Maratha Empire (1674–1818) emerged as defenders of Hindu culture and temples.

The Bhakti movement intensified throughout the medieval period, producing poet-saints across India whose devotional works often appealed across religious boundaries, including to Muslim audiences.

## Modern Hinduism and Global Spread

British colonial rule triggered a Hindu Renaissance and reform movements such as the Brahmo Samaj and Arya Samaj, which sought to purify Hinduism and engage with modernity. Western Indology played a major role in framing Hinduism as a single, unified religion, a category sharper than what existed on the ground. In the 20th century, teachers like Vivekananda and Prabhupada carried Vedanta and Yoga to a global audience, and the Indian diaspora established Hindu communities worldwide. Politically, Hindu nationalism (Hindutva) has become a significant force in contemporary India.

## Open Questions

Four debates remain unresolved. The first concerns origins: how much of Hinduism derives from the Indus Valley Civilization versus the Indo-Aryan Vedic tradition is still contested. The second concerns intellectual influence: the direction of borrowing between early Upanishadic thought and Buddhism is unclear. The third concerns Shankara, whose prominence some scholars now attribute partly to later amplification. The fourth concerns the Muslim period, where narratives range from widespread destruction to coexistence and voluntary conversion, and the truth is probably a regional and chronological mixture of both.
