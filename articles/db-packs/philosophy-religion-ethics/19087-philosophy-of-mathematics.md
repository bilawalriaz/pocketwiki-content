# Philosophy of mathematics

Philosophy of mathematics asks two intertwined questions: what are mathematical objects (numbers, sets, functions), and how do we come to know truths about them? The first is a metaphysical question about being; the second is an epistemological question about knowledge.

## Three classical positions

At the start of the twentieth century, three schools crystallised in response to worries about the certainty of analysis (the calculus of continuous quantities) and the new paradoxes of set theory.

**Logicism** holds that mathematics is just logic. Gottlob Frege tried to build arithmetic from a system of logic including Basic Law V (a principle letting you form the extension of any concept). Bertrand Russell showed that Law V is self-contradictory: the set of all sets that do not contain themselves seems to both contain and not contain itself. Russell and Alfred North Whitehead salvaged the project with ramified type theory, a hierarchy that prevents such self-reference, at the cost of heavy complications. Modern logicists restore the program using Hume's principle: the number of *F*s equals the number of *G*s exactly when the *F*s can be put into one-to-one correspondence with the *G*s.

**Intuitionism**, founded by L. E. J. Brouwer, holds that mathematics is a free construction of the human mind, so there are no truths independent of being mentally constructed. To avoid paradoxes, intuitionistic logic drops the law of the excluded middle (every statement is true or false) and rejects proof by contradiction. It also rejects the axiom of choice in most formulations.

**Formalism**, associated with David Hilbert, treats mathematics as the manipulation of symbol strings according to fixed rules, with no claim about what the symbols "mean". Hilbert's program aimed to prove all mathematics both complete (every true statement provable) and consistent (never proving a contradiction), using only a small, uncontroversial finitary arithmetic as a foundation. Kurt Gödel's second incompleteness theorem destroyed this hope: any sufficiently strong consistent system cannot prove its own consistency.

## Platonism and its rivals

**Mathematical Platonism** is the default working view of most mathematicians: numbers, sets, and functions are abstract, eternal, non-physical objects whose properties mathematicians discover rather than invent. Tegmark's **mathematicism** pushes this further, claiming that reality itself is a mathematical structure.

The chief objection, sharpened by Paul Benacerraf, is epistemological: abstract objects cannot causally interact with physical brains, so how do mathematicians come to know about them? Benacerraf's identification problem asks how we link our symbols to the right objects, since no physical contact distinguishes the number 4 from any other abstract entity. Hartry Field's **fictionalism** responds that mathematics is dispensable, because physics can be rewritten without numbers, and should be viewed as a useful fiction.

**Structuralism** sidesteps the problem by denying that mathematical objects have intrinsic properties; the number 1 is nothing more than the first whole number after 0, defined by its place in a structure. **Finitism** (Leopold Kronecker: "God created the natural numbers, all else is the work of man") restricts mathematics to objects buildable from natural numbers in finitely many steps.

Quine and Putnam's **indispensability argument** says we ought to believe in mathematical objects because physics needs them to explain observations: since physics needs numbers, numbers must exist. **Social constructivism** sees mathematics as a product of human culture, its direction shaped by community fashions and funding.

## Logic, rigor, and the foundational crisis

Around 1900, paradoxes in set theory, alongside non-Euclidean geometries, the nowhere-differentiable Weierstrass function, and Cantor's several sizes of infinity, triggered the foundational crisis. The crisis was stabilised by Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory with the axiom of choice (ZFC), a higher-order logic strong enough to host all standard mathematics. Within ZFC, "rigor" loses its old meaning: a proof is simply correct or incorrect.

In intuitionism and constructivism (Errett Bishop's 1967 *Foundations of Constructive Analysis*), every existence theorem must supply an explicit example. Intuitionistic logic drops the law of the excluded middle and proof by contradiction, and rejects the axiom of choice in most forms.

## Mathematics and the sciences

Mathematics resembles the empirical sciences in important ways: it is falsifiable (a wrong theorem is killed by a single counterexample), and many discoveries come from systematic experimentation with examples or diagrams, as Carl Friedrich Gauss said of his method: "durch planmässiges Tattonieren" (through systematic experimentation). Yet mathematical truths, once proved, do not depend on experiment.

Eugene Wigner's 1960 paper named "the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics": even the purest theories turn out to describe physical phenomena nobody had in mind when the theory was built. Prime factorisation, studied for two millennia, became the basis of the RSA cryptosystem. Ellipses were pure conic sections for the Greeks before Kepler showed in 1609 that planets move along them. Non-Euclidean geometry became the framework of Einstein's general relativity, where spacetime is a curved four-dimensional manifold. Conversely, mathematics drives physics: Dirac's equations predicted the positron, and the eightfold way predicted the Ω⁻ baryon, both later observed.

**Quasi-empiricism** (Putnam, Lakatos) holds that mathematicians test hypotheses as well as prove theorems, and an argument can transmit falsity backward from conclusion to premises. **Aesthetic judgment** plays a real role in proof selection: G. H. Hardy's *A Mathematician's Apology* argues that beauty is a legitimate guide, and Paul Erdős imagined a "Book" of the most elegant proofs.
