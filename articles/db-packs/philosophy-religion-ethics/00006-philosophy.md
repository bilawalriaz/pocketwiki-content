# Philosophy

Philosophy (from Greek *philosophía*, "love of wisdom," combining *phílos* "loving" with *sophía* "wisdom") is a systematic, rational, and self-critical study of general and fundamental questions about existence, knowledge, mind, reason, language, and value. The Pythagorean origin of the word is uncertain. It entered English via Old French around 1175 CE. Historically, "philosophy" encompassed nearly all rational inquiry, including what are now the empirical sciences; Newton's 1687 *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica* is classified as physics today. The modern narrow sense (metaphysics, epistemology, ethics) dates only from the end of the modern period.

## Defining philosophy

Definitions are themselves controversial, a debate called metaphilosophy. Method-based definitions risk being too wide or too narrow; Kant organized philosophy around four questions beginning "What can I know?" Quine treated philosophy as an empirical yet abstract science, which struggles to explain why it has not progressed like the natural sciences; one response calls it "the midwife of the sciences." Wittgenstein framed philosophy as linguistic therapy, Husserl as a "rigorous science" of essences through epoché (suspending theoretical assumptions), and the ancient Stoics as spiritual practice aimed at *eudaimonia* (flourishing through reflective life).

## Major traditions

**Western** philosophy began in 6th-century BCE Greece with the pre-Socratics, including Thales (c. 626–c. 545 BCE). Socrates (469–399 BCE) was followed by Plato (427–347) and Aristotle (384–322), who broadened its topics. Later antiquity produced Epicureanism, Stoicism, Skepticism, and Neoplatonism. The medieval period engaged religious questions; the Renaissance revived ancient schools from the 14th century; the 17th-century modern period focused on how knowledge is created; the 20th century added formal logic, the linguistic turn, pragmatism, phenomenology, existentialism, and post-structuralism, alongside growing but still underrepresented female participation.

**Arabic–Persian** philosophy arose in the early 9th century from Islamic theology, using Greek thought to interpret the Quran. Al-Kindi (801–873) sought harmony of reason and faith; Avicenna built a comprehensive system; al-Ghazali critiqued reason's reach, after which influence waned until Mulla Sadra (1571–1636). Islamic modernism arose in the 19th–20th centuries to relate tradition and modernity.

**Indian** philosophy, rooted in the Vedas and Upanishads, integrates reality, knowledge, and enlightenment. Buddhism (Gautama Siddhartha, 563–483 BCE) and Jainism (Mahavira, 599–527 BCE) emerged alongside Vedic thought. From about 200 BCE, six orthodox Hindu schools developed. Advaita Vedanta (Adi Shankara, c. 700–750) taught that all is one; Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita saw entities as real aspects of unity. The modern period from around 1800, including Vivekananda, harmonized traditions with Western thought.

**Chinese** philosophy focuses on social conduct, government, and self-cultivation. Confucianism and Daoism arose in the 6th century BCE amid political turbulence, alongside Mohism and Legalism. Buddhism arrived in the 1st century CE; Neo-Confucianism (11th c.) grounded ethics metaphysically. The modern era brought Chinese Marxism and New Confucianism.

Other traditions include Japanese philosophy, the Kyoto School (20th c.) blending Eastern spirituality with Western concepts; Latin American philosophy, moving from indigenous thought through colonial scholasticism (c. 1550) to positivism and liberation philosophy; and African philosophy, long oral and centered on community, morality, and Ubuntu, with systematic work emerging around 1900.

## Core branches

**Epistemology** studies knowledge, its sources (perception, memory, testimony), limits, and value. The classic analysis of knowledge as justified true belief faces the Gettier problem. The regress problem (justifications requiring further justification) yields foundationalist and coherentist responses; skepticism doubts whether certainty is attainable.

**Ethics** studies right conduct through meta-ethics, normative ethics (consequentialism/utilitarianism by outcomes, deontology by duties, virtue ethics by character), and applied ethics.

**Logic** studies correct reasoning. Deductive validity means premises guarantee the conclusion (as in modus ponens); induction generalizes from cases, abduction infers the best explanation.

**Metaphysics** studies reality's most general features. Ontology asks what fundamentally exists; key debates include physicalism versus mental or abstract entities, identity over change, particulars versus universals, and determinism versus free will.

Other branches include aesthetics (objective or subjective beauty), philosophy of language (meaning, reference; prominent since Frege and Russell), philosophy of mind (the mind–body problem, materialism, idealism, dualism, functionalism, and the hard problem of consciousness), philosophy of religion, philosophy of science (demarcation from pseudoscience), and political philosophy (legitimacy, justice, liberty).

## Methods

Philosophers use conceptual analysis, ordinary language analysis, thought experiments, reflective equilibrium, Peirce's pragmatic maxim (meaning equals practical consequences), phenomenological reduction, and methodological naturalism. Method choice often drives disagreement.

## Relation to other fields

Philosophy is a meta-discipline clarifying other fields' concepts and assumptions. It informs bioethics, business ethics, legal and journalistic standards, and theology; logic shaped mathematics and computer science. Enlightenment ideals underpinned constitutional democracy and the American and French Revolutions; Marxism influenced the Russian and Chinese Communist Revolutions; Gandhi's non-violence shaped Indian independence. Feminist philosophy, from Wollstonecraft through de Beauvoir to Butler, reshaped concepts of gender. Some scholars reject philosophy's practical utility, holding it valuable for its own sake.

## Key terms

- **Epistemology**: study of knowledge.
- **Metaphysics**: study of reality's most general features.
- **Ontology**: what exists at the most fundamental level.
- **Gettier problem**: counterexamples to defining knowledge as justified true belief.
- **Regress problem**: justifications seemingly requiring endless further justification.
- **Deductive validity**: premises guarantee the conclusion's truth.
- **Abduction**: inference to the best explanation.
- **Mind–body problem**: how mental phenomena relate to the physical.
- **Epoché**: phenomenological suspension of theoretical assumptions.
- **Eudaimonia**: flourishing achieved through a reflective life.
