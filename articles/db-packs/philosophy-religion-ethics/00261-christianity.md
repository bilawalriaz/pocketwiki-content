# Christianity

Christianity is an Abrahamic monotheistic religion built on the life, teachings, death, and resurrection of Jesus Christ, whom followers call the Son of God and the long-expected Messiah. With over 2.3 billion adherents, it is the world's largest religion.

## Origins as a Jewish movement

Christianity began in the 1st century AD, in the Roman province of Judaea, as a sect within Judaism. Its earliest followers were Jews who recognised Jesus as the fulfilment of Old Testament prophecy about a coming Messiah. Two changes turned the sect into a separate religion. First, the conviction that Jesus had risen from the dead within a generation of his crucifixion became the central claim of the movement. Second, missionary work to non-Jews (Gentiles), led especially by the Apostle Paul, pulled communities away from Jewish ritual law. The label "Christian" was first recorded in Antioch around 100 AD. Despite Roman persecution, the faith spread across the Eastern Mediterranean in its first three centuries.

## From sect to state church

Christianity's public status changed three times in the 4th century. Emperor Constantine I issued the Edict of Milan in 313, decriminalising the religion. The Council of Nicaea was convened in 325 to settle disputes about Jesus's nature, and produced the Nicene Creed, a statement of core belief still recited in most churches. The Nicene Creed was finalised at the Council of Constantinople in 381. In 380, the Edict of Thessalonica made Nicene Christianity the official religion of the Roman Empire.

Once tied to imperial power, doctrinal disputes became political ones, and church councils drew the line between orthodoxy (accepted teaching) and heresy (rejected teaching). Two great separations followed. In the 5th century, the Church of the East and Oriental Orthodoxy broke away over Christology, the theological question of how Jesus can be both fully God and fully human. In 1054, the East–West Schism formally divided the Eastern Orthodox Church from the Catholic Church over papal authority and a cluster of theological and cultural differences. A third rupture, the Protestant Reformation, began in 1517 when Martin Luther posted the Ninety-five Theses, and 16th-century Europe split into Catholic, Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, and many later Protestant bodies.

## Core beliefs

Christianity's defining doctrine is the Trinity: one God existing in three co-equal persons, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, a distinctive claim, since Judaism and Islam are strictly unitarian. Closely linked is Christology, the teaching that Jesus is both divine and human. The second pillar is soteriology, the doctrine of salvation: Christians hold that Jesus's death and resurrection atone for human sin and reconcile people with God. The Bible, comprising the Old and New Testaments, is the sacred scripture, and the Apostles' Creed and Nicene Creed summarise the faith that most denominations share. How salvation actually works, whether through faith alone, faith combined with works, or predestination, is the main point on which denominations differ.

## Worship and practice

Two rites, called sacraments, are observed across nearly all of Christianity. Baptism, performed by immersion or sprinkling, initiates a person into the community. The Eucharist (also called Holy Communion or the Mass) recalls Jesus's Last Supper with bread and wine. Worship services (liturgy) typically combine scripture readings, a sermon, and communal prayer. The liturgical calendar organises the year around Christmas, celebrating Jesus's birth, and Easter, celebrating his resurrection. The cross and the fish (Ichthys) are the most recognisable symbols.

## Branches and demographics

Christianity divides into three main branches:

| Branch | Approximate adherents |
| :--- | :--- |
| Catholicism | 1.3 billion |
| Protestantism | 800 million |
| Eastern Orthodoxy | 300 million |

Smaller but historically important groups include Oriental Orthodoxy and Restorationist movements such as the Latter-day Saints and Jehovah's Witnesses. The Catholic Church, led by the pope in Rome, is the largest single body. Protestantism is a family of traditions, including Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, Baptist, Methodist, and Pentecostal communities. Eastern Orthodoxy is a communion of self-governing national churches led by patriarchs.

The faith is now growing fastest in sub-Saharan Africa and Asia, while attendance is declining in much of Europe, North America, and Australia. Global missionary work and, historically, European colonisation carried it to every continent between the 15th and 17th centuries.
