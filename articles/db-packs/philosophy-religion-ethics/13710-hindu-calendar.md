# Hindu calendar

The Hindu calendar, also called Panchanga or Panjika, is a family of lunisolar calendars used across the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia for Hindu religious and social purposes. A lunisolar calendar combines lunar months for dates and festivals with the solar year for seasons, inserting an extra month roughly every 32.5 months to keep lunar months aligned with the solar year and the agricultural seasons. The calendars share this structure but differ in whether they emphasize the lunar or solar cycle, in their month names, and in when the new year begins.

## Why the system exists

Twelve lunar months cover about 354 days, while a solar year covers about 365. Unlike the Gregorian calendar, which pads months with extra days, the Hindu calendar preserves the integrity of each lunar month and instead repeats one month every two to three years. Twelve lunar months are grouped into six lunar seasons timed to farming, flowering, and weather. The repeated month, called Adhika Masa ("extra month"), is labelled impure or inauspicious so that festivals, weddings, and rituals scheduled in it are not confused with the original. A complementary rare correction, the kshaya month, drops a month altogether when the sun moves unusually fast through the zodiac; one example is 1 BCE, when the month of Pausha did not occur. Intercalation over long periods follows a 19-year cycle similar to the Metonic cycle used in the Hebrew calendar, with extra months typically falling in the 3rd, 5th, 8th, 11th, 14th, 16th, and 19th years.

## Two month traditions

A lunar month contains two fortnights, or paksha. Shukla Paksha is the bright, waxing half ending in full moon; Krishna Paksha is the darkening half ending in new moon. Two regional traditions differ in how they number these fortnights. The amanta tradition ends each lunar month on the new moon, so Shukla Paksha precedes Krishna Paksha, and is followed by most peninsular states (Gujarat, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Goa). The purnimanta tradition ends each month on the full moon, reversing the order, and is followed north of the Vindhya mountains and in Nepal. The same physical day can fall in different named months depending on tradition. Shukla Paksha, by contrast, always belongs to the same month in both systems.

## Solar months and the zodiac

The sky is divided into twelve zodiac signs of 30° each, called rashi, named after constellations (Meṣa/Aries, Vṛṣabha/Taurus, through Mīna/Pisces). The time the sun takes to cross each sign is a solar month, and the solar months share the rashi names. Solar-based calendars such as the Tamil and Malayalam calendars rename these months (Chithirai, Vaigasi, etc.) but track the same solar cycle. The year is grouped into six seasons (ṛtu): Vasanta (spring), Grīṣma (summer), Varṣā (monsoon), Śarad (autumn), Hemanta (pre-winter), and Śiśira (winter), each spanning two solar months.

## Eras

Most Hindu years are counted from one of two era systems. The Vikram Samvat, used in northern, western, central, and eastern India and in Nepal, begins in 57 BCE and is apocryphally linked to king Vikramaditya. The Shalivahana Shaka, used across the Deccan and as the basis of the Indian national calendar, begins in 78 CE. A Shaka era was also carried into Southeast Asia; the Kedukan Bukit inscription in Sumatra is dated to Shaka 604 (682 CE), among the earliest such dated inscriptions in the Indonesian islands.

## Days and weekdays

A solar day, divasa, runs from one sunrise to the next and is used for everyday purposes. A lunar day, tithi, is the time the moon's angular distance from the sun changes by 12°, so its length varies between about 21.5 and 26 hours, and a tithi can begin and end in the middle of an afternoon. A lunar month contains 30 tithis, and tithis are the basis for timing rituals and festivals. The seven weekdays take their names from the sun, moon, and the five visible planets (Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn) in the same order as Greek and Roman calendars, an alignment probably established by the 3rd century CE.

## The five limbs

A complete almanac, or Panchangam, gives five interlocking pieces of information for each day: tithi (lunar day), divasa (solar day), nakshatra (the lunar mansion, one of 27 divisions of 13°20' each), yoga (the sum of the sun's and moon's longitudes, divided into 27 equal parts of 800 arcminutes), and karana (half a tithi, with 11 named karanas repeating to fill 60 half-tithis per lunar month). Festivals keyed to the moon move across the Gregorian calendar; festivals keyed to the sun, such as those tied to a sankranti (the sun's entry into a new rashi), fall on nearly the same Gregorian date every year.

## History

Astronomical timekeeping is one of the six Vedangas, the ancillary sciences attached to the Vedas, and the Rigveda Jyotisha-vedanga already contains the formula for counting half-months from the sun's position. Hindu astronomers from the 5th to 12th centuries, including Aryabhata, Brahmagupta, Varahamihira, and Bhaskara II, refined the system; their texts recorded the sidereal year at values clustering around 365 days, 6 hours, 12 minutes, with small variations across treatises (Surya Siddhanta: 365 d 6 h 12 m 36.56 s; Siddhanta Shiromani: 365 d 6 h 12 m 9 s).

## Reach

The Hindu calendar's framework spread with religion and trade. Early Buddhist communities in India adopted the ancient Vedic calendar and later local variants. The Buddhist calendar and the traditional lunisolar calendars of Cambodia, Laos, Myanmar, Sri Lanka, and Thailand are based on an older version of the Hindu calendar. Jain festival timing follows the same lunisolar structure. In Bali, a 210-day Pawukon calendar coexists with a Hindu lunisolar saka calendar. The Hindu Calendar Reform Committee of 1952 identified more than thirty well-developed regional variants still in use across India.
