# Truth

Truth is the property of accurately representing reality. It is attributed to *truthbearers* (sentences, propositions, and beliefs, the things that can be true or false) and is generally thought to be grounded in the world through *truthmakers* (the facts or states of affairs that make those truthbearers true). Sentences are concrete linguistic expressions whose truth can shift with context; propositions are their abstract meanings; beliefs are mental states, private and harder to analyse. *Truth conditions* specify the circumstances under which a statement would be true, anchoring truth in reality rather than convention. Because the word "true" appears across every domain, philosophers have long asked what makes a statement true, and the answer remains contested.

## The main theories

Six families of answers dominate.

**Correspondence** says a statement is true when it matches a fact. It is the most intuitive account, anchoring truth in the world, but it is hard to apply where "facts" are not obvious, such as ethics or mathematics.

**Coherence** says a statement is true when it fits consistently within a system of beliefs. This works for rational inquiry, where claims are tested against a wider web of reasoning, but a critic can point to a coherent fiction and ask what stops it from counting as "true" here.

**Pragmatic** theories, associated with William James and C. S. Peirce, define truth as the belief that works in practice or that would survive ideal inquiry. The risk is that "what works" depends on the agent, opening the door to subjectivity.

**Semantic** theory, developed by Alfred Tarski, sidesteps metaphysical questions by defining truth in a higher-level language (a *metalanguage*) using T-sentences: "'Snow is white' is true if and only if snow is white." This avoids paradoxes such as the liar ("This statement is false"), but says little about what truth *is* at heart.

**Deflationary** theories go further, claiming that "true" adds no deep property at all. To say "'Snow is white' is true" is just to endorse the original claim, a useful linguistic device rather than a substantive feature of reality.

**Pluralists** reject a single definition, holding that truth works differently in different domains.

## How truths are classified

Philosophers sort truths by how they are known and how they depend on the world. *A priori* truths (such as those in logic and mathematics) are knowable without experience; *a posteriori* truths require observation. *Necessary* truths hold in every possible world; *contingent* truths hold only because the world happens to be a certain way. *Analytic* truths follow from meaning alone, *synthetic* truths from observation combined with meaning. The principle of *bivalence* says every proposition is either true or false, with no middle; rejecting it allows *truth-value gaps* (statements that are neither) and *fuzzy* or *truthlike* degrees of truth, as used in fuzzy logic and probability.

## Historical development

Ancient thinkers linked truth to reality: Parmenides and Plato treated it as the disclosure of being, and Aristotle developed early correspondence ideas. Medieval philosophy integrated truth with theology; Augustine identified truth with God, and Aquinas defined it as conformity of a thing to a divine idea. In the early modern period, Descartes treated clear and distinct ideas as true, and Kant redefined truth as agreement between judgment and object. Nietzsche later critiqued truth as a moral phenomenon tied to the will to power. The twentieth century brought Tarski's formal semantics, Armstrong's truthmaker theory, and a rise of deflationary views, alongside postmodern critiques of objectivity.

## Truth in culture and disciplines

Christian theology identifies truth with God ("I am the truth"); Islam names God *Al-Haqq*, the Real; Hindu thought centres on *satya*; Buddhism distinguishes conventional from ultimate truth. In art, truth is conveyed through aesthetic and symbolic meaning; in law, it must be established by evidence and standards of proof; in journalism, it is pursued despite misinformation and bias. Computer science encodes truth as binary values in Boolean logic, while probability theory represents it in degrees, capturing uncertainty.

## Living debates

The central open question is whether a single account covers every domain or whether truth is irreducibly plural. Related disputes ask whether moral, aesthetic, and religious claims can be objectively true, whether paradoxes like the liar can be dissolved, whether truth requires justification, and whether it must be grounded in a mind-independent reality (*realism*) or only in successful epistemic practice (*anti-realism*).
