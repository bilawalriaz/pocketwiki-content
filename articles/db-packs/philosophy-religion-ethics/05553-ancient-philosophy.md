# Ancient philosophy

Ancient philosophy refers to the philosophical traditions that emerged independently in several cultures between roughly the 7th century BCE and the early post-classical period (around 600 CE). Karl Jaspers called the concentrated burst of original thought from the 7th to the 3rd century BCE the "Axial Age," because philosophy based on individual insight arose in Greece, India, China, Iran, and Israel at roughly the same time.

The classical traditions usually ended when a new religion became politically dominant. In the West, the spread of Christianity through the Roman Empire closed Hellenistic philosophy and opened the medieval period. In the Middle East, the rise of Islam ended Old Iranian philosophy and began Islamic philosophy. In China, the Han dynasty replaced Qin Legalism with Confucianism and Daoism, and Buddhism arrived around the 1st century CE, eventually becoming a third major current.

## Greek and Roman philosophy

Greek philosophy is conventionally divided into three stages.

**Pre-Socratic philosophers (c. 600–400 BCE)** asked what the world is fundamentally made of. The Milesian school (Thales, Anaximander, Anaximenes) sought a single underlying substance. The Pythagoreans added number and harmony. Heraclitus stressed perpetual change; Parmenides and the Eleatics argued that true reality is unchanging. The pluralists (Empedocles, Anaxagoras) and atomists (Leucippus, Democritus) explained nature through combinations of basic elements or indivisible particles. The Sophists (Protagoras, Gorgias, Thrasymachus) shifted attention from cosmology to language, argument, and ethics.

**Classical Greek philosophy (5th–4th centuries BCE)** centers on three figures. Socrates (469–399 BCE) held that ethics could be examined by rational definition, leaving no writings. His student Plato (428–347 BCE) founded the Academy and argued for eternal Forms accessible to reason. Plato's student Aristotle (384–322 BCE) built a comprehensive system covering logic, physics, biology, politics, and ethics, and trained Alexander the Great.

**Hellenistic philosophy (after 323 BCE)** responded to the loss of the city-state by offering practical ways of life. Stoicism (Zeno of Citium; later Epictetus, Seneca, Marcus Aurelius) taught that virtue and acceptance of fate bring tranquility. Epicureanism (Epicurus; Lucretius) sought freedom from fear through modest pleasure and materialist atomism. Skepticism, including Pyrrhonism and Academic Skepticism, suspended judgment to achieve peace of mind. Cynicism (Diogenes of Sinope) rejected social conventions. Neoplatonism, founded by Plotinus in the 3rd century CE, reworked Plato into a system of emanation from a single source. Boethius (472–524 CE) transmitted Aristotle's logic into the Latin Middle Ages.

## Ancient Iranian philosophy

Iranian thought is rooted in Zoroastrianism, traditionally attributed to Zarathustra, whose Gathas within the Avesta are regarded as his direct teachings. The Cyrus Cylinder, an early charter issued by Cyrus the Great, is often read as a political expression of Zarathustrian ideas. Later movements included Manichaeism (Mani, c. 216–276 CE), which synthesized Iranian, Christian, and Buddhist elements, and Mazdakism, a late egalitarian and materialist sect. These traditions shared an Indo-Iranian background with Vedic India but differed in their view of humanity's social and cosmic role.

## Ancient Indian philosophy

Indian philosophy is a fusion of the Vedic and śramaṇa traditions. The Vedas posed questions about natural law, cosmic origin, and humanity's place, famously in the Nasadiya Sukta's open-ended hymn of creation. Vedic thought described cosmic order as rta, causal law as karma, and nature (prakriti) as woven from three qualities (sattva, rajas, tamas).

The śramaṇa movement viewed saṃsāra as suffering and emphasized renunciation, ahiṃsā (non-violence), and liberation (mokṣa). It produced Jainism, Buddhism, and the materialist Cārvāka (Lokāyata) school. In the Axial Age, Mahavira (599–527 BCE) shaped Jainism and Gautama Buddha (c. 480–c. 400 BCE) founded Buddhism. Classical thought was organized into six orthodox schools: Nyaya (logic), Vaisheshika (atomism), Samkhya (dualism of consciousness and matter), Yoga (psychophysical discipline), Mimamsa (ritual interpretation), and Vedanta (Upanishadic metaphysics). Adi Shankara systematized Advaita Vedanta, the non-dualist view that the self and ultimate reality are one.

## Ancient Chinese philosophy

Chinese philosophy flourished from the 6th century to 221 BCE, during the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods, known as the Golden Age of Chinese philosophy. The Book of Han lists ten major schools. The most influential were Confucianism, which taught self-cultivation and the virtues ren and yi; Daoism, which followed the Tao through compassion, moderation, humility, and wu wei (action through inaction); Legalism, which built administrative apparatus around state power; Mohism, which advocated universal love and empiricism grounded in sense experience; and Naturalism (Yin-Yang), which synthesized yin-yang and the Five Elements. Minor schools included the Logicians (Gongsun Long), Agrarianism, Diplomatic strategists, and Military thinkers (Sunzi).

The Qin dynasty imposed Legalism and suppressed rivals. The Han dynasty adopted Confucianism as state doctrine, integrated by Dong Zhongshu with Yin-Yang theory, and tolerated Daoism. In the 3rd–4th centuries CE, Xuanxue (Neo-Daoism) debated whether Being or Not-Being came first. Buddhism, entering around the 1st century CE, became dominant by the Sui and Tang periods, and its Chan (Zen) school proved especially influential across East Asia.

The Axial Age set the conceptual vocabulary that later religious and political institutions inherited, so that questions first posed about nature, knowledge, and moral order kept recurring under new vocabularies in medieval and modern thought.
