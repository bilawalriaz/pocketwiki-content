# Altered state of consciousness

An altered state of consciousness (ASC) is any condition that departs meaningfully from a person's normal waking awareness. The change is temporary and reversible, and is recognised subjectively as a shift away from the everyday baseline. It is a multidimensional shift, not a single faculty getting weaker or stronger, and the same kind of state can be reached by drugs, by physical or psychological practices, by accident, or by illness.

## Defining the term

No single definition exists, because any attempt must first define a "normal" state of consciousness. Arnold M. Ludwig gave the first academic definition in 1966: a state induced by physiological, psychological, or pharmacological means that the individual (or an observer) recognises as a clear deviation from their usual alert functioning. Charles Tart, who popularised the term from 1969, focused on the qualitative pattern of subjective experience, calling an ASC an alternate pattern distinct from a baseline state. Gordon Farthing (1992) added that the state is short-term or reversible and may not even be recognised as altered at the time, and proposed fourteen dimensions of changed experience, of which several must shift to qualify. A 2016 working definition for neuroscience, by Schmidt, treats an ASC as anything subjectively judged to diverge significantly from a person's normal wakeful variability, and requires it to be brief (minutes to hours) so it can be compared against a baseline.

## A short history

Humans may have used ASCs for at least 30,000 years, through mind-altering plants, fasting, and extended rhythmic dancing, often in religious or shamanic contexts such as the rites of Dionysus, the Eleusinian Mysteries, and yogic and meditation traditions. The expression appeared in print by 1892 in connection with hypnosis, was used in 1904 by Max Mailhouse in relation to epilepsy, entered academia with Ludwig in 1966, and reached common usage through Tart's 1969 book *Altered States of Consciousness*.

Scientific study was delayed by early-20th-century behaviourism, which dismissed subjective reports and treated ASCs as symptoms of intoxication or possession. William James helped restore interest by treating mystical and drug-induced states as legitimate objects of study and by rehabilitating introspection. The 1960s opened the field further, as researchers such as Abraham Maslow, Walter Pahnke, Stanislav Grof, and Tart studied potentially beneficial states like peak experiences and psychedelic-assisted mystical experiences. Pahnke's Good Friday Experiment showed that psilocybin can trigger mystical-type experiences, and Rick Doblin's follow-up found that participants rated those experiences among the spiritual high points of their lives.

## How an ASC is reached

Five induction routes are commonly distinguished. Pathological causes include epilepsy, brain injury, infections such as meningitis, oxygen deficiency, sleep deprivation, and psychotic episodes, all of which tend to reduce self-awareness or environmental awareness. Pharmacological causes are psychoactive substances that cross the blood–brain barrier, including cannabis, MDMA, opioids, cocaine, LSD, and alcohol, each acting on neurotransmitter systems differently (LSD activates serotonin-2A receptors in the cerebral cortex, while alcohol amplifies GABA and suppresses glutamate). Physical and physiological routes include sensory deprivation. Psychological routes include hypnosis, meditation, breathwork, and music. Spontaneous routes include day-dreaming and near-death experiences.

## Classifying the states

Classifications usually split ASCs by how they were induced, or by the kind of experience they produce. Dieter Vaitl's widely used scheme organises induction into pathological, pharmacological, physical/physiological, psychological, and spontaneous categories, and describes experience along four aspects: activation, awareness span, self-awareness, and sensory dynamics. Adolph Dittrich's empirical work identified three underlying dimensions: oceanic boundlessness, dread of ego dissolution, and visionary restructuralization. Ken Wilber adds that the personal meaning of an ASC is shaped by the individual's psychological development. Michael Winkelman groups many induced states (psychedelics, hypnosis, meditation) under an "integrative mode" alongside ordinary waking, deep sleep, and REM dreaming.

## Two neurobiological models

The entropic brain hypothesis, proposed by Robin Carhart-Harris in 2014, treats ASCs as moves along a spectrum from "primary" to "secondary" consciousness. Primary consciousness is high-entropy, less constrained cognition, as in REM sleep, the psychedelic state, or early psychosis. Secondary consciousness is the low-entropy, constrained cognition of normal waking, anaesthesia, or depression. Psilocybin appears to push the brain from secondary back toward primary by disrupting the coupling between the default mode network, which supports self-reflection, theory of mind, and memory, and the medial temporal lobes.

A complementary model, the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) loop, explains how psychedelics and some psychotic states disrupt the thalamus's filtering of sensory and cognitive information, causing underactivity in frontal regions (hypofrontality) and a flood of unfiltered input that erodes the boundary between self and environment. Hallucinogens act mainly as agonists or antagonists at serotonin-2A receptors, producing patterns that share phenomenological features with early acute schizophrenia, which is why the same gating system is implicated in both drug-induced and psychotic ASCs. The "synthetic surprise" framework extends this: psychedelics work by generating prediction error, a mismatch between what the brain expects and what it receives, which loosens entrenched beliefs and may underlie the therapeutic potential of psilocybin and LSD.
