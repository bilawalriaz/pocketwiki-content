# Philosophy of artificial intelligence

The philosophy of artificial intelligence is a branch of philosophy of mind and philosophy of computer science that asks whether machines can think, whether human and machine intelligence are the same, whether machines can have minds and consciousness, and what answering these requires. AI researchers care about behaviour, cognitive scientists about mechanism, philosophers about mind and meaning.

## Framing claims

Five propositions anchor the debate. Turing's "polite convention": if a machine behaves as intelligently as a human, it is as intelligent. The 1956 Dartmouth proposal: every feature of intelligence can in principle be described precisely enough for a machine to simulate it. Newell and Simon's physical symbol system hypothesis: a physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means of general intelligent action. Searle's strong AI hypothesis: an appropriately programmed computer with the right inputs and outputs would have a mind in exactly the sense humans do. Hobbes' mechanism: reasoning is nothing but reckoning, the adding and subtracting of consequences of agreed-upon names.

## Defining intelligence

Two definitions dominate. The Turing test, proposed in 1949, replaces "can machines think?" with a behavioural test: a judge communicates with one hidden human and one hidden machine, and the machine passes if it cannot be reliably distinguished. Critics including Russell and Norvig object that this only measures humanness, not intelligence, since aeronautical engineering texts do not aim to make machines that fly so exactly like pigeons they fool other pigeons.

Modern AI research prefers a goal-based definition. John McCarthy called intelligence "the computational part of the ability to achieve goals in the world." Russell and Norvig formalised this with intelligent agents: an agent perceives and acts in an environment according to a performance measure that defines success; an agent maximising expected value of that measure is intelligent. The advantage is precision; the disadvantage is that even a thermostat arguably counts.

## Can a machine display general intelligence? Four arguments

The brain can be simulated. If the nervous system obeys physics and chemistry, its behaviour should be reproducible on some physical device. Hans Moravec argued this in 1988, and Ray Kurzweil estimated a complete brain simulation by 2029. A 2005 simulation of a thalamocortical model of human-brain scale took 50 days on 27 processors to simulate one second of brain activity. Even Searle concedes this is possible in principle, but replies that anything can in principle be simulated, so the criterion collapses. Copying a bird feather by feather without understanding aerodynamics shows that simulation alone proves nothing about the nature of intelligence.

Human thinking is symbol processing. Newell and Simon argued in 1963 that symbol manipulation is the essence of both human and machine intelligence. Most AI from 1956 to 1990 used high-level, word-like symbols. Modern statistical AI does not, putting this hypothesis in tension with current practice.

Gödelian anti-mechanist arguments. Kurt Gödel's 1931 incompleteness theorems show that any consistent formal system contains true statements it cannot prove. John Lucas (from 1961) and Roger Penrose (from 1989) argue that, since the human mind can supposedly decide any well-grounded statement, the mind exceeds any Turing machine. Penrose speculated that quantum-gravitational processes in neurons might explain this. The mainstream reply is that real human reasoning is inconsistent, that any consistent idealised reasoner must suspend belief in its own consistency, and that Gödel's results do not distinguish humans from machines. Penrose's quantum hypothesis also lacks a plausible neural mechanism and runs against quantum decoherence timescales.

Dreyfus: the primacy of implicit skills. Dreyfus argued that human expertise relies on fast intuitive judgements, not step-by-step rules, so it can never be fully captured formally. Turing anticipated this in 1950 and replied that not knowing the rules is not evidence they do not exist. Cognitive science has since vindicated much of Dreyfus through Kahneman's dual-process theory, which distinguishes fast "System 1" intuition from slow "System 2" deliberation, and AI has shifted toward neural networks and statistical methods that model intuitive reasoning.

## Can a machine have a mind? Searle and the Chinese room

Searle distinguishes weak AI (machines can act intelligently) from strong AI (machines can have minds). Most AI researchers accept weak AI and ignore strong AI; Turing wrote that the mysteries of consciousness need not block answering whether machines can think.

Searle's Chinese room thought experiment drives the debate. A person locked in a room follows English instructions on cards to manipulate Chinese characters passed through a slot, producing fluent Chinese from outside. The person understands no Chinese; the cards and room understand nothing. Searle concludes that symbol manipulation alone, however fluent, cannot produce understanding. Mental states require the actual physical-chemical properties of real brains: "brains cause minds."

Variations on the same thought include Leibniz's mill (the brain expanded to factory size), Lawrence Davis's telephone-exchange brain (1974), Ned Block's Chinese Nation (1978), and Block's "Blockhead," simple if-then rules stripped of any mystery.

Replies take several forms. The systems reply says the whole room-plus-man-plus-cards understands, not the man alone. The robot reply (Moravec) says a body with sensors and actuators is needed for real meaning. The brain simulator reply says if the rules simulate a real Chinese speaker's neurons, understanding seems more plausible. The other-minds reply notes that Searle's argument generalises the problem of other minds: if we cannot prove other humans truly think, we should not expect to prove it of machines. Daniel Dennett adds that natural selection could not preserve consciousness if it had no behavioural effect, so either consciousness does not exist as Searle describes it, or a Turing-style test could in principle detect it.

## Computationalism

The computational theory of mind holds that mind relates to brain as software relates to hardware. Its roots run through Hobbes, Leibniz, Hume, and Kant; modern versions are associated with Hilary Putnam and Jerry Fodor. If brains are a kind of computer, machines can be both intelligent and conscious, collapsing the practical and philosophical questions into one.

## Behaviour, danger, and the Singularity

Whether machines can think is distinct from whether they can harm us. "Hostile" splits into a behavioural reading (dangerous) and a mental-state reading (intentional), the latter just being the consciousness question again. Vernor Vinge's "Singularity" warns of a sudden large jump in machine intelligence, and Eliezer Yudkowsky's "Friendly AI" argues that safety must be designed in from the start. Turing recorded and dismissed the theological objection that thinking requires an immortal soul: building thinking machines is no more an usurpation of divine power than procreation, since in both cases we provide vessels for what is created.
