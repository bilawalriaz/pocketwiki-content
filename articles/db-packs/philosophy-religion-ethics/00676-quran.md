# Quran

The Quran is Islam's sacred scripture, believed by Muslims to be the literal, uncreated word of God (Allah), delivered orally to the Prophet Muhammad by the angel Gabriel. It functions as a text of divine guidance, the highest authority in Islamic law and theology, and the finest model of classical Arabic prose. The revelation began in 610 CE during a retreat to the Cave of Hira, continued in fragments for 23 years, and ceased shortly before Muhammad died in 632 CE. Because the book is regarded as a direct transmission from God, every Arabic consonant carries doctrinal weight, and translation is treated as interpretation rather than the Quran itself.

## Structure

The Quran contains 114 chapters called sūras, each divided into verses called āyāt (singular: āyah, "sign" or "miracle"). Sūras are ordered roughly by length, not chronology, and most open with the formula Bismillah ("In the name of God"); only the ninth omits it. A Meccan sūra was revealed while Muhammad was in Mecca (about two-thirds of the text); a Medinan sūra was revealed after the migration (hijra) to Medina. The text weaves narratives of earlier prophets, ethical instruction, legal rulings, and eschatological warnings, and roughly one-third of its content addresses the afterlife and the Day of Judgment. The Quran describes itself as "a guide for humanity" (2:185), and its allusive style, which references events without full narration, is why it requires commentary to interpret.

## Compilation and Standardization

Muhammad died without leaving a bound book. Under Caliph Abu Bakr (632–634 CE), the scribe Zayd ibn Thabit gathered the verses from palm leaves, bones, and the memories of companions. Under Caliph Uthman (644–656 CE), the text was standardized into the Uthmanic codex and copies were dispatched to major cities to settle disputes over variant readings. Some scholars argue the process was messier than tradition describes, pointing to the Sanaa palimpsest, an early manuscript whose underlying layer shows variants the Uthmanic recension appears to have removed, and to the lost codices attributed to companions such as Ibn Mas'ud and Ubayy ibn Ka'b. Muslims generally hold that God guarantees the text's preservation (Quran 15:9), while academic critics cite manuscript evidence for gradual evolution; the question is unresolved.

## Canonical Readings

As Islam spread, legitimate recitations diverged. In the 10th century, the scholar Ibn Mujāhid fixed seven canonical readings (qirā'āt), each traced through a continuous chain of reciters back to a Companion of the Prophet. A related term, ahruf, refers to the original modes of recitation, whose exact nature is still debated. Western North Africa continues to favour the Warsh reading, while the 1924 Cairo edition, the standard printed Quran, follows the Ḥafṣ reading. The dominance of this single printed edition has marginalised alternatives, and no critical edition based on the earliest manuscripts has yet displaced it.

## Style and Inimitability

The Quran's idiom is rhymed prose, with rhythmic cadence, repetition, parallelism, and self-reference. Muslim theology crystallised this aesthetic into the doctrine of i'jāz: the claim that no human speech can match the Quran's eloquence. The claim is reinforced by the tradition that Muhammad was illiterate (ummi), so the literary excellence is read as miracle rather than craft. The Mu'tazila rationalist school in the 9th century first developed i'jāz into a systematic argument. Western scholars have approached the text through philology, textual criticism, and historical comparison with Jewish and Christian traditions; the extent of borrowing versus shared oral substrate versus independent revelation remains contested, as does the related claim of taḥrīf (corruption of earlier scriptures).

## Interpretation

Tafsīr is the formal discipline of commentary. Classical scholars divided verses into muḥkam (clear) and mutashābih (ambiguous), with the latter requiring contextual and symbolic reading. Two principal methods dominate: tafsīr bi'l-ma'thūr, which grounds meaning in transmitted reports about Muhammad, and tafsīr bi'l-ra'y, which applies reasoned judgment. Sufi traditions pursue ta'wīl, an esoteric reading of the text's inner (bāṭin) meaning. Modern academic study adds historical-critical analysis of the Quran's sources, composition, and intertextual relations.

## Worship and Law

The Quran is recited in full or in part during the five daily prayers (ṣalāt) and throughout the month of Ramadan, following the rules of tajwīd, which govern pronunciation, rhythm, and articulation. As a legal source, it supplies injunctions on governance, inheritance, marriage, and criminal justice; jurisprudence (fiqh) derives rulings from these verses, producing the several schools of thought that structure Sunni and Shia practice. Core rituals of Islam, including fasting (ṣawm), almsgiving (zakāt), and pilgrimage (ḥajj), are rooted in Quranic commandments, while hadith literature, the recorded sayings and actions of Muhammad, supplies the second source of guidance that fills in what the Quran states only in outline. The Quran's role in fixing classical Arabic grammar and generating commentarial scholarship makes it a foundational document of the Arabic literary tradition as well as of Islam.
