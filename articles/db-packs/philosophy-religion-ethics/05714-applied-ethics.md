# Applied ethics

Applied ethics is moral philosophy applied to real decisions. Where pure ethics asks what makes an action right or wrong in the abstract, applied ethics asks what to do about concrete problems: whether to permit euthanasia, how to allocate scarce donor organs, whether a company must clean up its pollution, when a whistleblower is obligated to speak. Because the answers depend on the details of medicine, business, law, or technology, the field is multi-professional rather than purely academic.

The modern field emerged in the early 1970s, shaped by rapid medical and technological advances that pushed philosophers out of the seminar room into hospitals, boardrooms, and legislatures. Ethical codes of conduct now exist in almost every profession, and applied ethics is an established subdiscipline of moral philosophy.

## Where applied ethics sits in moral philosophy

Moral philosophy is usually divided into three layers.

| Branch | Question it asks |
|---|---|
| Meta-ethics | What is the nature of ethical properties, statements, and judgments? |
| Normative ethics | What standards determine whether an action is right or wrong? |
| Applied ethics | What should we do about specific real-world moral problems? |

The three are distinct but interdependent. Applied work usually draws on the normative theories described below, and the puzzles it surfaces can feed back into the more abstract branches.

## Principlism: the dominant toolkit in bioethics

In bioethics and health care, the most widely used method is the four-principle approach, or principlism, developed by Tom Beauchamp and James Childress. It treats four principles as prima facie, meaning each must be respected unless it conflicts with another of equal or stronger weight in the case at hand.

- **Autonomy**: respect the decision-making capacity of persons.
- **Non-maleficence**: avoid causing harm.
- **Beneficence**: act to benefit others.
- **Justice**: distribute benefits, risks, and costs fairly.

Principlism gives clinicians and committees a shared vocabulary for organising a case, but it does not say why one principle should outweigh another when they collide.

## The normative theories applied ethics draws on

When a real dilemma is too tight for a checklist, practitioners reach for one of three normative traditions.

**Consequentialism** judges acts solely by their outcomes. The paradigmatic version is utilitarianism, classically formulated by Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill, which holds that an act is right if it maximises net aggregated wellbeing. Bentham and Mill distinguished act utilitarianism, which evaluates each act, from rule utilitarianism, which evaluates the rules whose general adoption would maximise welfare. Henry Sidgwick later added the importance of motive, and R. M. Hare added preference, asking which outcomes best satisfy the preferences of those affected. Prioritarianism, a related view, gives extra weight to the welfare of the worst-off rather than summing happiness evenly.

**Deontological ethics** holds that some acts are right or wrong regardless of consequences. Immanuel Kant's categorical imperative is the central example: act only on principles that could be universal laws, and treat persons as ends in themselves rather than as mere means. Natural law, heavily developed by Thomas Aquinas and central to Catholic moral teaching, is another deontological tradition. Threshold deontology blends the two: deontological rules govern up to a point, but once consequences cross a stipulated threshold of severity, consequentialist calculation takes over.

**Virtue ethics** traces to Aristotle and Confucius and shifts the question from "which rule?" to "which person?" The right action is the one a suitably virtuous agent, someone with trained character, would choose in the circumstances.

## Casuistry: reasoning from cases rather than theory

One response to clashes between these traditions is case-based reasoning, or casuistry, associated with Albert Jonsen and Stephen Toulmin in *The Abuse of Casuistry* (1988). Rather than starting with a theory and deducting an answer, casuists start with the concrete facts of a particular case, identify its morally significant features, and compare it with settled paradigm cases. Ethical theory enters as one resource among several, not as the master framework. Jonsen and Toulmin observed that medical ethics committees often reach consensus on hard cases when participants focus on shared facts rather than on ideological commitments: a rabbi, a Catholic priest, and an agnostic may agree that extraordinary care should be withheld for a particular patient while disagreeing completely about why. Working from the case upward increases the chance of agreement where working from theory downward does not.

## Boundaries

Applied ethics is now distinguished from a younger neighbour, applied epistemology, which sits under the broader umbrella of applied philosophy. Applied ethics handles moral questions in practice; applied epistemology handles questions of knowledge, evidence, and belief in practical settings such as science policy, journalism, and law. They share an applied stance but target different problems, and recognising the split matters because imported methods from one can mislead when the underlying question belongs to the other.
