# Hinduism

Hinduism is an umbrella term for a wide range of Indian religious and spiritual traditions that share adherence to *Dharma*, the cosmic order maintained through rituals and righteous living. With roughly 1.17 billion adherents, it is the world's third-largest religion. It has no single founder, no single scripture binding on all followers, and no single creed. Scholars often describe it less as one religion than as a family of related traditions, or as a "way of life."

The word "Hindu" was originally a geographic label, derived from Sanskrit *Sindhu* (the Indus River), applied to people living beyond that river. "Hinduism" itself only entered widespread use in the 19th century, through figures like Raja Ram Mohan Roy and as Indians distinguished themselves from Muslims and Christians under British rule. Adherents frequently prefer *Sanātana Dharma* ("eternal dharma") or *Vaidika Dharma* ("Vedic dharma") to signal that the tradition predates the colonial-era label.

## Historical Development

The Vedic period (c. 1900–1400 BCE) produced the Vedas, the oldest sacred texts. Between roughly 800 and 200 BCE, the Upanishads added a philosophical layer, asking what underlies the ritual order and positing an ultimate reality, *Brahman*. From about 500 BCE to 300 CE, a "Hindu synthesis" emerged, blending Vedic ritual practice with broader Indian traditions; the epics *Mahabharata* and *Ramayana* and the early Puranas were composed in this era. The Gupta Empire (c. 320–650 CE) is often called a "Golden Age," during which the six classical schools of Hindu philosophy took shape: Samkhya, Yoga, Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Mīmāṃsā, and Vedanta. The Bhakti movement (c. 1250–1750 CE) emphasised personal devotion to a deity and spread under both Hindu and Islamic rulers, a period that also saw persecution of Hindus. The 19th century brought reform movements such as Brahmo Samaj and Arya Samaj, the rise of Neo-Vedanta, and the modern spread of Hinduism through diaspora communities and groups like ISKCON. The 1947 Partition of India established a Hindu-majority republic and reshaped the religion's political context.

## Scriptures

Hindu texts fall into two broad categories. *Śruti* ("heard") refers to revealed scripture: the four Vedas, Rigveda, Samaveda, Yajurveda, and Atharvaveda, with associated works such as the Upanishads. *Smṛti* ("remembered") covers human-authored works including the epics, the Puranas, the Bhagavad Gita, and the Yoga Sutras. Authority flows from these texts, from gurus, and from tradition, yet internal debate and reinterpretation have always been part of the tradition, so no single text or teacher speaks for all Hindus.

## Core Concepts

Four ideas recur across most Hindu thought: *Dharma* (ethics, duty, and the right way to live in accordance with cosmic order), *Karma* (moral cause and effect, where actions shape future circumstances), *Saṃsāra* (the cycle of birth, death, and rebirth driven by karma), and *Moksha* (liberation from that cycle, the ultimate spiritual goal). The four *Puruṣārthas*, or aims of human life, are Dharma, Artha (prosperity), Kāma (desire), and Moksha. Together they describe a life that balances moral order, material wellbeing, and the pursuit of liberation, rather than demanding rejection of the world.

Beliefs about the divine range widely. Some Hindus worship many gods; others, following Advaita Vedanta, hold that the individual self (*Ātman*) is identical with the single ultimate reality (*Brahman*). The idea of *avatara*, a divine incarnation, most famously Rama and Krishna, is central to Vaishnavism.

## Denominations and Practice

Four major denominations structure most Hindu worship: Vaishnavism (devotion to Vishnu), Shaivism (to Shiva), Shaktism (to the Goddess Shakti), and Smartism (worship of five deities). Practice includes *Bhakti* (devotion), *Puja* (ritual worship with offerings), *Yajna* (fire rituals), meditation, and Yoga. *Saṃskāras* mark life stages from birth to death, and *Tirtha-yatra* (pilgrimage) to sacred sites such as Varanasi draws millions. Festivals like Holi and Diwali are widely observed.

## Society

Traditional Hindu society is described by the four *varṇas*: Brahmins (priests and scholars), Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), Vaishyas (merchants and farmers), and Shudras (servants), and by the four *Āśramas*, or life stages: student, householder, retired, and renunciant. Whether the *varṇa* system is a religious prescription or a social custom remains debated, and modern Hindus hold varying views on its applicability. Hindu culture expresses itself in temple architecture, a lunisolar calendar, and ethical principles like *ahiṃsā* (nonviolence), which underpins vegetarian practice and reverence for life.

## Open Debates

Hinduism resists tidy definition. Scholars continue to argue whether it is one religion, a family of related religions, or partly a colonial construct, since the unifying label postdates most of the traditions it covers. The caste system's religious status, the absolute authority of the Vedas (challenged by Tantric and other traditions), and the nature of Moksha, whether liberation is achievable in life (*Jivanmukti*) or only after death (*Videhamukti*), remain live questions within Hindu thought.
