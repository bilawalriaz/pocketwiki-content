# Commercial bank

A commercial bank is a financial institution that accepts deposits from the public and lends to clients to facilitate commerce, earning a profit from the gap between what it pays depositors and what it charges borrowers. The term also describes a division of a larger bank that serves corporate, large, or middle-market clients, distinguishing it from retail banks (which serve individuals) and from investment banks (which serve capital markets). Commercial banks exist in both the private and public sectors. Central banks are different: they are not profit-driven and cannot become insolvent in the same way as commercial banks under a fiat currency system, despite the common "bank analogy" that conflates the two.

## Role and credit creation

The general role of commercial banks is to provide financial services to households and businesses in support of economic and social stability and sustainable growth. Their most significant function is credit creation. When a commercial bank approves a loan, it does not hand the borrower cash from its vault. It opens a deposit account in the borrower's name and credits it with the loan amount, creating new deposit money. As the borrower spends those funds, the money moves through the banking system as cheques and transfers, re-depositing at other banks and becoming the basis for further lending. This conversion of loans into derivative deposits, multiplied across the system, is what gives commercial banks their core economic function.

## Core services

Commercial banks accept deposits (savings, fixed or term, and transaction or checking accounts) and lend in several forms: overdrafts, cash credit (a revolving credit line against a borrower's account), bill discounting, money-call loans, and demand or term loans, both secured and unsecured. They also process payments through telegraphic transfers, electronic point-of-sale systems, internet banking, bank drafts, and bank cheques.

Secondary services fall into two groups. Agency services include collecting and clearing cheques, dividends, and interest warrants; making rent and insurance payments on a customer's behalf; handling foreign exchange; buying and selling securities; acting as trustee, attorney, correspondent, or executor; and accepting tax proceeds and returns. Utility services include safe deposit boxes, money transfers, traveller's cheques, bill-payment acceptance, and issuing credit and debit cards.

## Regulation

In most countries, commercial banks are supervised by the central bank, which imposes reserve requirements (a minimum fraction of deposits the bank must hold in reserve) and minimum capital requirements (a minimum level of shareholder equity the bank must maintain as a cushion against losses), along with other conditions to keep individual banks solvent and to protect the stability of the payments system.

## History

The English word *bank* comes from the Italian *banco*, meaning desk or bench, used by Florentine bankers of the Italian Renaissance who transacted across cloth-covered tables. Banking activity itself goes back to antiquity. In the United States, the term "commercial bank" took on a specific legal meaning after the Great Depression, when the Glass–Steagall Act of 1933 required commercial banks to engage only in banking activities while confining investment banks to capital-market activities. That separation was largely repealed in 1999 by the Gramm–Leach–Bliley Act, allowing both activities to operate under one roof.
