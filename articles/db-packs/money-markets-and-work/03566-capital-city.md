# Capital city

A capital city is the municipality that serves as the seat of government for a country or subnational division, typically housing its central political institutions. The word "capital" derives from the Latin *caput*, meaning "head," echoing the 1st-century BC phrase "Roma Caput Mundi" (Rome, head of the world). The earliest known capital is Damascus, cited from around 2500 BC. Historically, major economic centres often became political capitals through conquest or federation, as with ancient Babylon, Rome, and Constantinople. In medieval Western Europe, governments were itinerant (wandering); Versailles served as the seat of government for France from 1682 to 1789 while Paris remained the traditional capital.

## How capitals are chosen and where they sit

Most modern capitals sit near a country's geographic centre for accessibility and defence, or in a neutral location to balance rival regions. **Compromise capitals** include Canberra (Australia), Washington, D.C. (United States), and Ottawa (Canada); Washington was established through the 1790 Residence Act to mediate between Northern and Southern states. A **forward capital** is deliberately placed in a peripheral area for strategic or economic reasons; Islamabad and Myanmar's Naypyidaw (founded 2005) are examples. Capitals also carry symbolic weight: ruined Athens was made the capital of newly independent Greece in 1834, and Saint Petersburg was built to orient Russia toward Europe. Governments sometimes construct entirely new, planned capitals such as Brasília, Canberra, and Washington, D.C. to relieve overcrowding or balance competing power centres.

## Federal and unitary arrangements

In federations such as Germany, Russia, and the United States, each constituent state has its own capital alongside the national one. In unitary states containing multiple nations, like the United Kingdom, a single city (London) often serves as both the national capital and the capital of one constituent country.

## Multiple capitals and city-states

Several countries separate their official capital from the actual seat of government:

| Country | Constitutional capital | Seat of government |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| South Africa | Pretoria (administrative), Cape Town (legislative), Bloemfontein (judicial) | All three |
| Bolivia | Sucre | La Paz |
| Netherlands | Amsterdam | The Hague |

Monaco and Singapore are city-states with no distinct capital separate from the country itself. Disputed capitals appear in divided territories: Nicosia in Cyprus and Jerusalem, claimed by both Israel and Palestine, with final status unresolved pending negotiations.

## Status by law versus by convention

A capital's status may be defined by law or constitution, or simply recognised by long-standing practice. London and Paris are capitals by convention rather than explicit legal designation, and the Portuguese constitution does not mention Lisbon as the capital at all.

## Capitals as military targets

Militarily, capturing a capital was historically decisive in highly centralised states such as ancient China, where the fall of the capital signalled the fall of the regime. In decentralised states, resistance could continue from the provinces after a capital fell, so its capture was less conclusive. Capitals also attract skilled administrators and may coincide with religious centres, as with Rome and Jerusalem.
