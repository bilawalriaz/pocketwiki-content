# Welfare economics

Welfare economics is the branch of microeconomics that evaluates how well an economy serves the people in it. It provides the theoretical foundation for public economics and instruments like cost–benefit analysis. Combined with behavioral economics, it forms the subfield of behavioral welfare economics.

## Efficiency and distribution as separate questions

Any economic outcome requires two distinct judgments. An efficiency judgment asks whether production and consumption are organized so that no waste remains. A distribution judgment asks whether the resulting goods and utilities are shared fairly. Welfare economics separates these questions because each requires different methods, with efficiency assessed through Pareto and Kaldor–Hicks criteria, and distribution handled separately by a social welfare function.

## Pareto efficiency

An outcome is Pareto efficient if no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off. If Smith holds an apple but prefers an orange, while Jones holds an orange but prefers an apple, both gain by trading, so the initial allocation is inefficient.

A Pareto-efficient state requires four conditions to hold simultaneously. First, every consumer has the same marginal rate of substitution between any two goods, so reallocating goods between consumers cannot make both happier. Second, every producer of two goods has the same marginal rate of transformation, so reallocating production cannot raise total output. Third, every producer of a good gets the same marginal physical product from each factor, so costs cannot be cut by reshuffling inputs. Fourth, the marginal rate of substitution in consumption equals the marginal rate of transformation in production for every pair of goods, so producers cannot make consumers happier by switching the output mix.

Common sources of inefficiency include monopoly, monopsony, oligopoly, and monopolistic competition; factor allocation errors; externalities; asymmetric information, including principal–agent problems; declining average costs in a natural monopoly; taxes and tariffs; and government price or quantity restrictions. A condition inefficient in a "first-best" world may become desirable in a second-best setting: a tax on tires, for instance, can offset an externality that causes overproduction.

## Compensation tests

Because most policy changes help some and hurt others, compensation tests ask whether gainers could in principle compensate losers and still come out ahead. The Kaldor criterion treats a change as desirable if the maximum winners would pay exceeds the minimum losers would accept. The Hicks criterion treats it as desirable if the maximum losers would offer winners to block the change is less than the minimum winners would accept to allow it. Kaldor takes the winners' side, Hicks the losers'. When both pass, the change satisfies Kaldor–Hicks efficiency and moves the economy toward Pareto optimality. When the two tests disagree, the result is the Scitovsky paradox.

## The two fundamental theorems

The first fundamental theorem states that, under weak assumptions, competitive markets produce Pareto efficient outcomes, the formal version of Adam Smith's invisible hand. Beyond price-taking behavior and complete markets, the only extra assumption is local non-satiation, meaning consumers always want slightly more of any good at the margin. The theorem guarantees only that some Pareto efficient point will be reached, not that the "best" one will be.

The second theorem states that, under stronger restrictions, any Pareto efficient outcome can be supported as a competitive market equilibrium. Convexity of preferences and production sets is a sufficient but not necessary condition. A benevolent social planner can use lump-sum transfers to redistribute endowments so that a chosen Pareto efficient allocation is supported as a competitive equilibrium. More broadly, redistribution should avoid distorting prices so the post-trade outcome remains efficient, a logic that resembles predistribution. Because welfare economics is so tied to social choice theory, Arrow's impossibility theorem is sometimes listed as a third fundamental theorem.

## Three approaches

The earliest cardinal approach, developed by Edgeworth, Sidgwick, Marshall, and Pigou in the Benthamite tradition, treats utility as measurable. With stable preferences and diminishing marginal utility, social welfare becomes the sum of individual utilities, a measure sensitive to the distribution of income but not to the distribution of final utilities.

The ordinal approach, called the new welfare economics and associated with Pareto, Kaldor, Hicks, and Scitovsky, drops cardinality. It ranks bundles with indifference curves and evaluates efficiency through Pareto and Kaldor–Hicks criteria, leaving distribution to a separately specified social welfare function.

The Austrian school, especially Murray Rothbard, rejects both. It holds that interpersonal comparisons of utility and aggregated social welfare functions violate methodological subjectivism, and proposes a reconstruction based solely on demonstrated preference: only voluntary market exchanges can be unambiguously welfare-enhancing. Centralized attempts to maximize social welfare are said to face insurmountable knowledge and calculation problems.

The behavioral consensus behind the cardinal and ordinal programs largely collapsed after Kenneth Arrow's 1951 impossibility result, which showed that no rule for aggregating individual preferences into a social ordering can satisfy a small set of reasonable conditions, including the Pareto principle, and later utility-representation theorems showed the underlying frameworks to be mathematically self-contradictory, violating transitive preferences. Amartya Sen later argued that Arrow's framework excluded information about individuals' social position needed for income-allocation decisions, and pushed for broader data and explicit discussion of ethics in welfare research.

## Social welfare maximization and the equity–efficiency tradeoff

A social welfare function formally ranks economic states by society's value judgments about how to weight different citizens' interests. Drawing a social utility frontier, also called a grand utility frontier, from the outer envelope of utility possibilities traces out every Pareto efficient allocation. Among those, only one maximizes social welfare, the "point of bliss," where the social utility frontier is tangent to the highest attainable social indifference curve.

Because redistribution is itself costly, a permanent tension exists between equity and efficiency, and a large body of research has measured the efficiency losses caused by the tax-and-transfer programs that implement it.
