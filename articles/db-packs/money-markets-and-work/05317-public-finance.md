# Public finance

Public finance is the study of how governments raise and spend money, and how those choices shape the economy. It is a subfield of economics and political economy that examines government revenue, government expenditure, and the effects of adjusting one or the other.

## The three purposes

Public finance addresses three questions about government in an economy:

1. Efficient allocation of available resources.
2. Distribution of income among citizens.
3. Stability of the economy.

In 2010, policy advisor Jonathan Gruber proposed a framework organised around four questions: when government should intervene (because of market failure or redistribution of income and wealth), how it should intervene (through public provision, taxation, or subsidization), what the empirical effects are, and why governments choose particular interventions, which is the domain of political economy.

## Market failure and the rationale for intervention

Under ideal conditions, private markets allocate goods efficiently and the resulting income distribution is acceptable, leaving little role for government. In practice, these conditions often fail, producing market failure. Common causes include externalities, public goods, informational advantages, strong economies of scale, and network effects.

A public good is non-rival and non-excludable: one person's consumption does not reduce availability to others, and no one can be barred from using it. National defense is the standard example. Private markets undersupply such goods because producers cannot capture the full value, creating an efficiency-based rationale for government provision. Public provision is in turn vulnerable to government failure, where the cure performs as badly as the disease.

## Separating spending from taxation

Under broad assumptions, the choice of how much to spend can be made independently from the design of the tax system that finances it. This is the Diamond-Mirrlees separation: public programs are first sized to maximise social benefits minus costs through cost-benefit analysis, then the required revenue is raised through the tax system that distorts economic activity least. In practice, public budgeting is messier and routinely produces inefficient outcomes.

## Financing government spending

Governments fund spending through taxes, non-tax revenue (profits from state-owned enterprises, sovereign wealth funds, asset sales, or seigniorage, the net revenue from issuing currency), and borrowing. A deficit is the gap between spending and revenue in a given period, and the accumulation of deficits over time is the public debt. Unlike private finance, where income is direct, public finance draws on indirect sources, mainly taxes, which is why tax design matters so much.

Borrowing spreads tax burdens across generations rather than replacing taxes. Governments issue bonds, bills, or loans from commercial banks or institutions such as the IMF or World Bank to run deficits and smooth tax burdens over time, though accumulated debt narrows the choices of future governments.

## Classifying expenditure

Government expenditure falls into three categories:

- **Government consumption**: purchases of goods and services used immediately.
- **Government investment**: purchases intended to yield future benefits, such as infrastructure or research.
- **Transfer payments**: outlays that move money between groups without a purchase, such as social security.

Some transfers deliberately redistribute income, paying pensions from workers to retirees, disaster relief to those who suffered losses, and public education to families with school-age children. Even non-transfer spending has distributional effects, since road construction benefits users and builders, and war spending benefits the sectors it mobilises.

## Taxation as the core tool

Taxation is the central revenue instrument of modern public finance. A tax is an enforced pecuniary contribution imposed by legislative authority, not a voluntary donation, and may be direct (such as personal or corporate income tax, proportional in structure) or indirect (such as sales tax, VAT, excise, stamp duty, or customs duties). A regressive system falls more heavily on the poor; the Romans used one. Adam Smith's Canons of Taxation remain a touchstone: equality, certainty, convenience, and economy.

The choice of tax and its design changes both the distribution of income and the efficiency of markets. Tax incidence examines who actually bears a tax after prices and behaviour adjust, which is often not the person who remits it.

## History of the field

Principles of public finance appear in the Bible's discussions of Sunday-trade, slavery, and aid to the poor, and in ancient Greek practice, where government funded both art and war. Rome developed one of the first real tax systems, using tributa (land and poll taxes) and vectigalia (inheritance, sales, and postage taxes), with regional assessments collected by aristocrats. Feudal England relied on food rent, where regions delivered a share of produce to the king.

The 17th-century laissez-faire movement, led by the Physiocrats in France, held that government's central role was to protect private property, funded by a single tax on agricultural surplus. Adam Smith accepted a larger role: protection, justice, regulated money and banking, patents, copyrights, and public works and education. He also first articulated the idea of a public good, whose value to society exceeds its value to any individual buyer. By the early 19th century, thinkers like John Ramsay McCulloch had helped establish public finance as an independent field.

## Measuring the public sector

The internationally accepted methodology for compiling fiscal data is the Government Finance Statistics Manual 2001 (GFSM 2001), consistent with the System of National Accounts. It defines the general government sector as non-market producers funded mainly by compulsory levies, with four sub-sectors: central government, state government, local government, and social security funds. Public corporations are excluded from general government but included in the broader public sector. GFSM 2001 uses accrual accounting and a full set of financial statements (operations, balance sheet, cash flow), data investors use to assess sovereign risk.
