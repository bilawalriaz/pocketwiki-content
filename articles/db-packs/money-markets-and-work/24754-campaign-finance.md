# Campaign finance

Campaign finance refers to the funds raised to promote candidates, political parties, or policy initiatives and referendums. Donors and recipients include individuals, corporations, political parties, and charitable organizations. Modern campaigns are expensive: staff, consulting, and advertising all cost money, and the bill varies by country. In the United States, campaigns must purchase television advertising time, while in many other countries the state provides it. Because campaigns must raise so much money, ties to representative democracy weaken, since large contributors gain influence over politicians.

Most contributors give to parties or candidates with whom they are already in agreement. The public widely perceives, however, that donors expect government favors in return, such as specific legislation being enacted or defeated, and many equate campaign finance with political corruption and bribery. These views have driven governments to reform campaign financing in the hope of reducing the influence of large donations.

## Private financing

In many countries, private donors finance campaigns. These include individuals and groups such as trade unions and for-profit corporations. Campaigns raise money through direct mail, online solicitation, the candidate asking directly, and dedicated fundraising events. A US survey found that 23 percent of candidates for statewide office spent more than half of their scheduled time fundraising, and over half of candidates surveyed spent at least a quarter of their time on it.

Supporters argue that private financing avoids government limits on speech, encourages civic involvement, lets a diversity of views be heard, and prevents the state from tilting the scales toward those in power. Critics counter that private money lets votes be bought and widens the resource gap between parties. One randomized study found that political donations significantly increase a donor's access to policymakers.

Most countries that rely on private donations require extensive disclosure, typically the donor's name, employer, and address, so that other campaigns and good-government groups can police undue influence. The United States allows undisclosed "dark money," spending exempt from disclosure rules that has reached hundreds of millions of dollars in each recent US presidential election.

## Public financing

Many countries, especially across South America and Europe, use public money to run campaigns. Mechanisms range from direct subsidies to political parties, to government matching funds for small private donations, to non-cash support such as discounted postage or required low rates for television air time. Supporters argue that public financing lowers corruption and can boost civic participation and trust. Opponents object to the cost and to the state subsidizing political speech. Some systems let candidates who reach a vote threshold claim a rebate by submitting audited expenses; in the 2008 Hong Kong Legislative Council election, candidates could receive up to HK$11 per vote. Germany and the United States run hybrid systems that mix private and public money; US public-financing tools include democracy vouchers, matching funds, and lump-sum grants.

## Regulation and consequences

Because campaign finance touches free and fair elections, effective governance, and the regulation of corruption, the United Nations Convention Against Corruption urged members to enhance transparency in the funding of political parties and candidacies. Researchers at the International Foundation for Electoral Systems identified several common understandings: money is necessary for democratic politics but is never unproblematic; regulation must not kill healthy competition; strategy has to fit local context; effective regulation and disclosure help control the worst effects of money in politics when well designed; and oversight works only when regulators, civil society, and the media act together in the open. Campaign finance regulations can reduce political corruption.
