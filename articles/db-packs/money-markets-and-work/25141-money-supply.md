# Money supply

Money supply is the total stock of money available in an economy at a point in time. Standard measures include currency (banknotes and coins) plus bank deposits that can be quickly spent. In practice, deposits overwhelmingly dominate: in the UK, deposit money outweighs central-bank currency by more than 30 to 1, and in the US by more than 8 to 1.

## The M0–M3 ladder

Central banks and statistical agencies sort money into a spectrum from narrow to broad. Narrow aggregates (currency, checkable deposits) are most directly tied to monetary policy. Broader aggregates add less liquid assets (savings accounts, certificates of deposit, money market funds). They are usually labelled M0, M1, M2, M3, and sometimes M4.

| Aggregate | What it adds |
|---|---|
| **M0 / MB** (monetary base) | Physical currency in circulation plus central-bank reserves |
| **M1** | M0 plus demand deposits, traveler's checks, other checkable deposits |
| **M2** | M1 plus savings deposits, small time deposits, retail money market funds |
| **M3** | M2 plus large time deposits, institutional money market funds, repurchase agreements |

The US Federal Reserve published M3 until 2006, then stopped, stating that M3 conveyed no extra information beyond M2. From 24 April 2020, savings deposits were folded into US M1, closing a long-standing distinction between "transaction" and "savings" accounts.

## How money is created

In a fractional-reserve system, two kinds of money coexist. Central bank money (MB) consists of currency and bank reserve accounts at the central bank. Commercial bank money (the rest of M1–M3) consists of deposits.

Commercial banks create deposits whenever they make a loan: the new deposit appears in the borrower's account at the same instant the loan is booked. Repaying principal destroys the matching deposit. Money supply therefore moves with banks' lending decisions and the public's preference for cash versus deposits, both of which are heavily shaped by the central bank's interest-rate setting. The central bank can also act more directly through open-market operations: buying government bonds injects reserves and pushes interest rates down; selling them withdraws reserves and pushes rates up.

## The quantity theory

The monetarist school grounds its claim that money growth causes inflation in Irving Fisher's 1911 equation of exchange:

M × V = P × Q

M is the money stock, V is the velocity of money (how often each unit is spent per year), P is the average price level, and Q is real output. The equation is an identity by definition, since V is just PQ divided by M. Monetarists assume V is stable, so changes in M translate predictably into changes in P. Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz documented this money–inflation link for the US over 1867–1960 in their 1963 work.

## Why money-supply targets were abandoned

During the 1970s and 1980s major central banks (the Federal Reserve, Bank of England, Bundesbank) officially targeted steady money-supply growth. Starting in the mid-1970s the empirical correlation between money and prices broke down as velocity proved unstable. The Fed's 1979 attempt under Paul Volcker to target money was abandoned as unworkable. Today most developed-country central banks target inflation directly by setting policy interest rates, allowing the money stock to fluctuate endogenously to satisfy whatever money demand prevails. According to Benjamin Friedman, the number of central banks actively targeting money supply is shrinking toward zero.

Money-supply data are still monitored as one of many indicators that may carry information about bank behaviour, employment and inflation.
