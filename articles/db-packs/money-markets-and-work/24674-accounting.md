# Accounting

Accounting is the process of recording an organization's economic transactions, summarizing them, and communicating the resulting numbers to people who need to judge or steer the entity. Those readers include investors, creditors, managers, and regulators. The people who do this work are called accountants, and "accounting" and "financial reporting" are often used as synonyms.

## The core activity

Every accountant, whatever their specialty, does the same basic job: capture transactions, classify them, and produce reports. Bookkeeping is the day-to-day recording step; accounting adds analysis and presentation. The dominant system is double-entry bookkeeping, in which every transaction is entered as both a debit and a matching credit, so the books stay self-balancing. The modern version of this system was published in 1494 by the Italian friar Luca Pacioli, sometimes called the "Father of Accounting," though bookkeeping itself is far older: forms of it appear in ancient Mesopotamia and Iran, and the Romans under Emperor Augustus maintained detailed public accounts.

## Main branches

Accounting splits into a few large specialties that share the same underlying records but face outward in different directions.

- Financial accounting prepares standardized statements for outsiders such as investors, creditors, and regulators, following rules called Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP). The statements are past-oriented and are typically published six to ten months after period-end, either annually or quarterly.
- Management accounting produces internal reports that help managers run the business. It is not bound by GAAP and uses cost–benefit reasoning. Its reports can be historical or forward-looking, such as budgets, and often mix financial and non-financial figures for specific products or departments.
- Tax accounting applies specialized rules for computing taxable income and filing returns. In the United States, tax principles can differ from GAAP, and corporate and personal income face different marginal and average rates that vary with income level.
- Auditing is the independent examination of financial statements, intended to give an outside user confidence that the statements are fairly presented in all material respects in line with GAAP.
- Forensic accounting supports disputes and litigation, working to standards suitable for a court of law.
- Intercompany accounting records transactions between related entities such as a parent and its subsidiaries.

Accounting information systems, often built on enterprise resource planning software and increasingly augmented with artificial intelligence, support all of these functions.

## Standards and who sets them

GAAP is issued by national bodies: the Financial Accounting Standards Board in the United States and the Financial Reporting Council in the United Kingdom. Alongside these, the International Accounting Standards Board issues International Financial Reporting Standards, which as of 2012 were adopted or targeted for convergence by all major economies. International standards on auditing, ethics, education, and public-sector accounting come from independent boards supported by the International Federation of Accountants.

## The people and firms

Professional designations include the chartered accountant and the U.S. Certified Public Accountant. Major professional bodies are the American Institute of CPAs, the Institute of Chartered Accountants in England and Wales, the Institute of Chartered Accountants of Scotland, and the Association of Chartered Certified Accountants, the largest, with over 320,000 members and a 14-exam qualification; together with hundreds of others they form the International Federation of Accountants. Entry generally requires a bachelor's or master's degree in accounting or a related field; academia requires a doctorate.

The audit market is dominated by the Big Four accounting firms: Deloitte, Ernst & Young, KPMG, and PricewaterhouseCoopers. They were once the Big Five until the collapse of Arthur Andersen after the Enron scandal of 2001, when irregular accounting through the 1990s led Andersen to dissolve and Enron to file for Chapter 11 bankruptcy.

## Fraud, error, and regulation

Accounting fraud is an intentional misstatement, a criminal act that may also breach civil law and often involves collusion. An accounting error is unintentional and may instead breach civil duties such as negligence. The primary duty to prevent and detect both rests with management, not the auditor. The string of U.S. reporting scandals around 2001, including Enron, WorldCom, Qwest, and Sunbeam, exposed how weak standards and incentives could produce manipulated results. The response was the U.S. Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002, which sharply raised criminal penalties for securities fraud, for destroying or fabricating records, and for schemes to defraud shareholders.
