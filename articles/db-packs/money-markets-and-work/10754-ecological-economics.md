# Ecological economics

Ecological economics treats the human economy as a subsystem of the global ecosystem, not a self-contained machine. Because the biosphere sets hard physical limits, the field argues that mainstream economics' habit of pricing everything in money and assuming technology can replace any depleted natural input is the root of its blind spots.

## Allocation, scale, distribution

Mainstream economics is built around efficient allocation: given preferences and technology, prices guide resources to their best use. Ecological economics adds two questions allocation alone cannot answer: how big should the economy be relative to the ecosystems it depends on (scale), and how are costs and benefits divided between people, including those not yet born (distribution). Growth in output can be "uneconomic" when, beyond some point, its marginal ecological and social costs exceed its marginal benefits, even if measured GDP keeps rising. Ecological economists are also technological sceptics: they favour the precautionary principle, the view that action should prevent harm before full scientific proof, because some environmental damage is irreversible.

## The entropy argument

The deepest argument draws on thermodynamics, especially the second law: entropy never decreases in a closed system. Every production process transforms low-entropy energy and materials into high-entropy waste, so the economy is sustained by a one-way flow of low-entropy inputs from the environment, not a circular exchange of value. Frederick Soddy, a Nobel-winning radiochemist, attacked the idea of the economy as a "perpetual motion machine" in *Wealth, Virtual Wealth and Debt* (1926). Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen extended this in *The Entropy Law and the Economic Process* (1971), which Herman Daly later called a founding text. "Entropy pessimists" conclude that some natural-capital inputs are irreplaceable. Energy return on energy invested (EROEI) captures the same idea in practice: as the easiest coal, oil, and gas deposits are exhausted, the net energy gained per unit of energy invested declines.

## Source and sink functions

Natural systems support the economy in two ways. The source function supplies materials and energy; the sink function absorbs waste. Both are finite. When waste output exceeds what sinks can absorb, long-term damage accumulates, especially for pollutants that ecosystems break down slowly or not at all, such as persistent organic pollutants and nuclear waste. Robert Costanza's 1997 attempt in *Nature* to value the world's ecosystem services produced a headline figure of US$33 trillion per year, with a range of $16–54 trillion, compared to 1997 global GDP of $27 trillion. Half the value came from nutrient cycling; estuaries, swamps, and seagrass beds had the highest per-hectare values. The paper was controversial: critics argued that averaging across unrelated studies was not meaningful, and that reducing life-support systems to dollars degrades them to "worthless" in any cost-benefit analysis that refuses to register infinity.

## Weak versus strong sustainability

This split is the field's defining policy fault line. Weak sustainability holds that human-made capital can, in principle, replace any natural input. Strong sustainability holds that certain stocks (climate regulation, the ozone layer, a pollinator community) are irreplaceable on human time scales, so policy has a fiduciary duty to preserve them. Ecological economics generally favours strong sustainability; a German survey found ecological and environmental economists diverge sharply here, with the former rejecting substitution as a general rule.

## Externalities as cost shifting

Mainstream economics frames pollution and depletion as "externalities," rare exceptions to an otherwise efficient system, fixable with the right tax or permit. Following K. William Kapp, ecological economists argue this is a misnomer. Shifting costs onto workers, communities, ecosystems, and future generations is the normal way modern enterprises make profit, not a glitch. The Yasuní region of Ecuador illustrates the stakes: it holds large bitumen deposits but is one of the most biodiverse places on Earth, with estimates of over 200 undiscovered medical compounds in its genomes. Ecuador's government once tried to auction a "leave it in the ground" lease for US$350 million. Treating rainforest purely as timber and oil misses the real value, and treating it as a natural-capital line item is an improvement, but still risks commodifying the uncommodifiable.

## Brief history

Antecedents include Thomas Malthus on population limits and John Stuart Mill's stationary state. Sergei Podolinsky tried to anchor a labour theory of value in embodied energy in 1880; Otto Neurath pushed calculation in kind (tracking physical resources directly rather than collapsing them into money) during the 1919 Bavarian Soviet Republic. Modern ecological economics was named at a 1982 meeting in Sweden, organised at Lois Banner's instigation, and was institutionalised in 1989 with the founding of the International Society for Ecological Economics and its journal *Ecological Economics*, with Robert Costanza as first president. Key figures include Kenneth Boulding, E. F. Schumacher (*Small Is Beautiful*, 1973), Daly (*Steady-State Economics*, 1977), Georgescu-Roegen, Costanza, Joan Martinez Alier, and Clive Spash.

## Schools and offshoots

Clive Spash groups work in the area into new resource economists, new environmental pragmatists, and social ecological economists. The more radical branch overlaps with degrowth, which calls for an equitable downscaling of production and consumption to stay within biophysical limits, and with non-Western traditions such as Buen Vivir and Ecological Swaraj. Critics, including new resource economists, counter that green-economy approaches and renewable-energy transitions can deliver sustainability without abandoning growth.

## The monetisation debate

Valuing ecosystems in money polarises the field. Defenders say it makes nature visible to decision-makers; critics reply that financial valuation makes conservation hostage to market swings, as when Costa Rican coffee plantations were replanted with pineapple after coffee prices fell, dropping the bees' pollination value to zero. McCauley argues that ecosystems do not act "for" any one species, that human ingenuity keeps inventing artificial substitutes, and that monetisation is, in his phrase, "selling out on nature."
