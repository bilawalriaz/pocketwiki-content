# Trans-Saharan trade

Trans-Saharan trade was the exchange of goods, people, and ideas between North Africa and sub-Saharan Africa across the Sahara Desert. It peaked between the 8th and early 17th centuries, carried by camel caravans that linked Saharan oases. The trade ran on a simple complementarity: West Africa held abundant gold, North Africa held scarce salt, and each side wanted what the other had.

The desert had always carried lighter contact, but the camel transformed what was possible. Camels appear in the region in the 3rd century CE, and they can travel days without water, carry heavy loads, and tolerate heat. With camels, regular large-scale routes became viable. Two main corridors emerged: a western route from Morocco south to the Niger River bend, and an eastern route from Tunisia to Lake Chad. Ancient tracks such as the Forty Days Road (Darb El Arba'īn) and the Ghadames Road connected oasis towns across the central Sahara. The Garamantes of Libya had controlled eastern routes since at least 1500 BCE, using underground irrigation channels called foggaras, maintained with slave labor, to grow crops in the desert.

The medieval period was the golden age. Ghana, Mali, and Songhai rose to wealth by taxing and channeling the gold-salt exchange, while cities such as Sijilmasa, Timbuktu, and Gao grew rich as caravan termini. Sijilmasa peaked as a gold-trading and minting center in the 11th and 12th centuries. A single caravan might involve as many as 12,000 camels, guided by Berber experts who knew the wells and the rhythm of water. Salt came from Saharan mines such as Taghaza; gold came from the forests south of the Sahel. In 1050, Ghana lost the trading town of Aoudaghost to the Almoravids, a North African Islamic movement, and the profits shifted to the Malinke. The eastern route fed the Kanem–Bornu Empire around Lake Chad.

People moved with the goods. The trans-Saharan slave trade ran for centuries, used by the Garamantes for irrigation in antiquity and continuing through the medieval period. Enslaved people worked as domestic servants, concubines, and soldiers in North and West African households and armies. Historian John Wright estimates an average of about 5,000 people per year over roughly 1,250 years, a cumulative total of around 6–7 million, with the majority moved after 1600. The exact number remains uncertain, since modern figures rest on historical averages and projections.

Islam spread along the same routes, carried by Muslim merchants who offered traders a shared language (Arabic), a shared moral framework, and a trusted network of coreligionists. Conversion often began with ruling elites and then widened; Islam reached the Western Sudan by the 10th century and Hausaland by the 12th–13th centuries.

The trade collapsed in stages. Portuguese ships along the West African coast in the 16th century gave European buyers a sea-based route to gold, bypassing the desert. The decisive blow was the Moroccan invasion of 1591–92, the Battle of Tondibi, whose armies sacked Timbuktu and Gao and shattered the Songhai networks that had policed the western caravan routes. French colonization of the Sahel in the 1890s, the building of railways, and later post-independence borders and conflicts such as the Tuareg rebellions cut the traditional paths further. Lightly used camel lines called azalais, such as the one from Agadez to Bilma, still run today, but as relics rather than arteries.
