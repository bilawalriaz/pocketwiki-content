# Financial market

A financial market is a marketplace where securities, commodities, currencies, derivatives, and other financial instruments are priced and traded. The term can describe a single exchange, such as the New York Stock Exchange, or the broader process by which that trading is organised. Historically these were physical venues; today most are electronic, with NASDAQ as the leading example. Decentralised platforms, including cryptocurrency networks, and direct transactions such as corporate actions also form part of the modern global market.

## What markets do

Financial markets exist to move money from those who have it to those who need it. Lenders temporarily hand over funds in exchange for interest or other returns; borrowers use the funds for spending, investment, or cash-flow needs. Without organised markets, borrowers would struggle to find counterparties and savers would struggle to put idle money to work.

Most people are lenders without realising it: a savings account, a pension contribution, an insurance premium, or a government bond all lend money. Companies with surplus cash, and banks, which can create new deposit money and lend it out, are also lenders.

Borrowers include individuals taking short-term loans or mortgages, companies funding working capital, expansion, or buyouts, and governments. When government spending exceeds tax revenue, the gap is financed by issuing bonds. Governments sometimes allow inflation to erode the real value of that debt rather than paying it off directly.

Between the two sides sit intermediaries, including banks, investment banks, pension funds, and mutual funds, that pool small savings and channel them to large borrowers. The matching itself happens on exchanges, interbank lending desks, and foreign-exchange platforms. The price that emerges from many simultaneous bids and offers is called *price discovery*; it tells borrowers and savers how scarce capital is.

## Types of markets

Markets are usually grouped by what is traded and by the maturity of the instruments.

Capital markets finance long-term needs. Stock markets trade shares of company ownership, letting firms raise money by selling part of themselves to investors. Bond markets trade debt issued by governments and firms. Capital markets are split into a *primary market*, where new securities are first issued (as in an IPO), and a *secondary market*, where existing securities are resold. Liquidity, the ease of selling without a price concession, matters because it lets investors exit when they want; an illiquid security may force a sale at a large discount.

Money markets trade short-term debt of one year or less, used by firms and governments for temporary funding. Commodity markets trade primary-sector goods such as crops, livestock, metals, and oil. Foreign exchange markets trade currencies, both on the spot and forward. Derivative markets trade contracts whose value is derived from an underlying asset and are used either to insure against price moves or to speculate on them; the four main types are futures, forwards, options, and swaps.

## Derivatives and regulation

Derivatives expanded rapidly through the 1980s and 1990s and are now essential to the financial system. Their opacity amplified losses during the 2008 crisis, prompting major regulation: the US Dodd-Frank Act, which forces over-the-counter derivatives through central counterparties to increase transparency, and the EU's MiFID II, which strengthened transaction transparency and investor protection.

## How prices move

Two schools try to explain short-term price changes. *Technical analysis*, rooted in Charles Dow's Dow Theory, reads past price trends as signals about the near future. Many academics reject this and point instead to the *random walk hypothesis*, under which successive price changes are independent and only fresh information moves prices.

Volatility, the typical size of price swings over a given period, is amplified by human psychology: fear can drive prices down sharply and greed can inflate bubbles. High-frequency algorithmic trading now reacts to news within milliseconds, and an ECB study found that high-frequency trading correlates strongly with public announcements such as interest-rate decisions and trade-balance releases. Large price jumps also occur more often than a bell-curve model predicts, a pattern that Benoit Mandelbrot modelled with Lévy stable distributions.
