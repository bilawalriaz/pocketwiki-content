# Bank run

A bank run happens when many depositors rush to withdraw their money because they fear the bank is about to fail. Under fractional-reserve banking, banks keep only a fraction of deposits as cash and lend the rest in long-term loans. This asset–liability mismatch means no bank holds enough reserves to satisfy all depositors at once. A run turns fear into reality: as withdrawals mount, the bank liquidates long-term assets at a loss, deepening insolvency and triggering more withdrawals, a self-fulfilling prophecy. Even a false rumor can start one, because each depositor's best response to suspected panic is to withdraw first. Robert K. Merton cited bank runs as a prime example of the concept he named. Mervyn King, then governor of the Bank of England, observed that starting a run is irrational but joining one once it has begun is rational.

A banking panic hits many banks at once as a cascading failure, and a systemic banking crisis wipes out nearly all banking capital in a country. Across systemic crises from 1970 to 2007, average fiscal cleanup costs reached 13% of GDP and output losses averaged about 20% of GDP over the first four years.

The Diamond–Dybvig model explains why runs happen despite banks serving a real function. Banks intermediate between borrowers who want long-maturity loans and depositors who want instant access to cash. By the law of large numbers, only a small share of depositors withdraw on any given day, so banks can lend long while keeping modest cash reserves. Coordinated withdrawal breaks that assumption, and the forced fire sale of long-term assets can bankrupt both the bank and its borrowers.

The Great Depression showed the damage at scale. U.S. banking panics began in the South in November 1930, spread through correspondent networks in Tennessee and Kentucky, and reached New York and Philadelphia by December. Bernanke attributed much of the Depression's economic damage directly to bank runs; Friedman and Schwartz identified the mechanism: hoarding by nervous depositors forced banks to liquidate loans, shrinking the money supply between the 1929 crash and the March 1933 bank holiday. Canada avoided runs in the same era because its branching rules concentrated risk differently.

Individual banks borrow cash from other banks or the central bank, promote term deposits to reduce on-demand balances, schedule prominent cash deliveries to project solvency, suspend withdrawals (suspension of convertibility), or arrange emergency acquisition by a stronger institution. Before deposit insurance, banks also built large lobbies to prevent visible queues that might look like a run.

Systemic defenses aim to remove the incentive to run. Deposit insurance, run by the U.S. Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC), guarantees deposits up to a limit so a depositor need not fear being last in line. The FDIC keeps takeovers secret and reopens branches under new ownership the next business day. Capital requirements, especially Basel III, force banks to hold more loss-absorbing equity and liquid buffers. The central bank acts as lender of last resort, a role Walter Bagehot analyzed in *Lombard Street*. Higher reserve ratios reduce but do not eliminate runs, and full-reserve banking would eliminate them at the cost of removing credit creation.

Even insured depositors can flee if they fear losing access during a reorganization, and insurance fails if the government itself looks unable to pay. A silent run occurs when depositors withdraw below the insured limit, or when fear of a government's unbooked losses on zombie banks pushes up their funding costs and squeezes healthier competitors, transferring wealth from healthy banks and taxpayers.

A newer variant is the cyber run: rapid wholesale withdrawals triggered by a cyber-attack that threatens depositors' access to funds rather than the bank's solvency. In March 2023, customers withdrew $42 billion, nearly a quarter of Silicon Valley Bank's deposits, within a day after a digital operational shock. Research on systemically important U.S. banks found they hold enough high-quality liquid assets for a 75% 30-day outflow, but cyber-run outflows can arrive far faster than the Liquidity Coverage Ratio assumes, and a major cyber incident disabling payment nodes could spill liquidity shortfalls into the real economy even when individual banks stay liquid.
