# Labour law

Labour law mediates three overlapping relationships: a single worker and an employer (individual labour law), workers acting through unions and employers (collective labour law), and both parties and the state. The field responds to industrialisation, the growth of unions, and the state's effort to balance employer flexibility, worker protection, and social stability.

## Origins

Contracted work predates modern industry. The Code of Hammurabi (c. 1755–1750 BC) fixed a 2-shekel wage per 60-gur vessel built, plus daily freight rates for charters. Roman *collegia* — legally recognised associations including craft guilds and burial clubs — required Senate or imperial approval under the *Lex Julia* of Julius Caesar. Inscriptions show guildhalls for shipbuilders and merchant mariners at Ostia and Portus.

Modern labour law emerged with the Industrial Revolution as workshops gave way to large factories. Early industrial Britain produced dangerous conditions, prompting the Factory Acts. The Health and Morals of Apprentices Act 1802 capped apprentices' hours at twelve per day. The Cotton Mills and Factories Act 1819 barred children under nine. The Factories Act 1833 limited children's hours, banned night work, and created a state inspectorate. The Factory Act 1847 capped the workday at ten hours for women and children. Reformers Anthony Ashley-Cooper (Earl of Shaftesbury) and Michael Sadler drove these changes. Similar regulation spread to other industrialising countries by the early twentieth century.

## Individual labour law

The employment relationship rests on a contract of employment, supplemented by statute and common law. Many jurisdictions require written terms covering pay, holiday, illness rights, notice, and job description.

A central distinction is between *employees* and *independent contractors*. Employees are controlled by the employer, paid regularly, supplied with tools, and owe a duty of loyalty. Independent contractors retain autonomy. The boundary matters because statutory protections apply only to employees.

Key individual rights:

- **Minimum wage.** Most industrialised countries set a legally enforceable hourly floor; statutory minimums began in the United States (1938), Brazil (1940), India (1948), France (1950), and the United Kingdom (1998). A living wage is higher, calibrated so a full-time worker can support a small family. Some countries, such as Sweden, lack a statutory minimum and rely on collective agreements covering roughly 90 percent of employees.
- **Working hours.** Pre-industrial days lasted 11–14 hours; nineteenth-century factories pushed hours to 16. The 1833 English law limited miners to 12 hours and children to 8, then the 1848 law set a 10-hour day. France's Popular Front (1936–38) established a 40-hour week and two weeks of paid leave, cut to 35 hours in 2000. The US Fair Labor Standards Act (1938) capped the workweek at 44 hours, reduced to 40 in 1950.
- **Health and safety.** Regulation began with textile mills in 1802 and expanded through the nineteenth century into mines, machinery, and ventilation.
- **Anti-discrimination.** Most legal systems bar hiring or dismissal discrimination on grounds including race, gender, religion, age, disability, and sexual orientation.
- **Unfair dismissal.** ILO Convention 158 requires a legitimate motive and a chance to defend oneself before termination; many countries limit at-will employment by statute.
- **Child labour.** Industrialised states bar children under 14–16 from most employment, with light-work exceptions. Most child labour today occurs in the informal sector — agriculture, street vending, domestic service — outside the reach of inspectors.

## Collective labour law

Trade unions negotiate wages, benefits, and conditions through collective bargaining, typically producing a collective labour agreement. Some systems grant workers the right to elect part of corporate boards. Germany's Mitbestimmungsgesetz 1976 gives employees half the seats on the supervisory board. Sweden's Law on board representation entitles workers in private firms with 25 or more employees to appoint two board members.

Strike action is the main union lever. Most systems require a democratic ballot, ban sympathy and general strikes in essential services, and regulate picketing. Boycotts, go-slows, work-to-rule, and sit-ins are also used; some jurisdictions permit them, others forbid them.

## International labour law

The International Labour Organization (ILO), founded in 1919 under the Treaty of Versailles on the principle that "labour is not a commodity", issues Conventions that member states may ratify. The 1998 Declaration on Fundamental Principles classified eight Conventions as core (freedom of association and collective bargaining, abolition of forced and child labour, non-discrimination), binding all members regardless of ratification. Enforcement is weak: the ILO can only investigate and publicise violations. The Hours of Work (Industry) Convention, 1919 capped the week at 48 hours and has been ratified by 52 of 185 member states.

The World Trade Organization (WTO) carries trade sanctions but excludes labour standards. Proposals for a "social clause" allowing sanctions for breaches of core labour standards remain contested, especially by low-wage developing countries that view their standards as a comparative advantage.

When workers cross borders, conflicts-of-law rules decide which country's law applies. EU Rome I Regulation article 8 grants workers the rights of the country where they habitually work; they may claim under another country's law if they can show a close connection. The Posted Workers Directive sets minimum standards for temporary foreign workers in the host state. EU law excludes direct wage regulation and unfair-dismissal fairness, leaving those to member states, but Directives cover working time, anti-discrimination, and collective redundancies. The European Court of Justice ruled in *Laval Ltd v Swedish Builders Union* and *The Rosella* that host-state collective agreements cannot generally raise standards beyond statutory minimums for posted workers — a position later softened by Rome I Regulation recital 34.

## National variation

National systems diverge on structure. The United Kingdom built on the Factory Acts (1802, 1833) and the Master and Servant Act 1823, then expanded after 1960 through equality and EU-derived statutes; the Equal Pay Act 1970 and the National Minimum Wage Act 1998 are landmarks. The United States relies on the Fair Labor Standards Act 1938, the National Labor Relations Act 1935, Title VII of the Civil Rights Act 1964, and the Age Discrimination in Employment Act 1967. Australia uses the Fair Work Act 2009, enforced by the Fair Work Commission. South Africa's Labour Relations Act 1995 requires dismissal only for misconduct, operational reasons, or incapacity with procedural fairness, routing disputes through the Commission for Conciliation, Mediation and Arbitration. India codifies constitutional labour rights in Articles 14–16, 19(1)(c), 23–24, and 42–43A, supplemented by over fifty national and many state laws, consolidated in the New Labour Codes 2022. China operates the Labour Law 1994 and Labour Contract Law 2008, with a single state-aligned union. Sweden delegates most conditions to collective bargaining, with the Employment Protection Act as the chief statutory exception.
