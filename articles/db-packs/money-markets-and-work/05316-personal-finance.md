# Personal finance

Personal finance is the financial management an individual or family performs to budget, save, and spend monetary resources over time, while accounting for risks and future events. It draws on banking products (checking and savings accounts, credit cards, loans), insurance products (health, disability, life), and investment products (bonds, stocks, real estate), and includes managing credit scores, taxes, retirement funds, and pensions.

The field grew out of home economics in the early twentieth century. Universities such as Brigham Young, Iowa State, and San Francisco State began undergraduate and graduate personal-finance programs in the 1990s, and a standardized curriculum only emerged in the United States after the 2008 financial crisis, when the President's Advisory Council on Financial Capability pushed for national standards.

## Core principles

Universal rules are hard to fix because income, wealth, and needs vary, tax and legal rules differ by country, and markets change. University of Chicago professor Harold Pollack and writer Helaine Olen nevertheless argue that good personal-finance advice in the United States reduces to a few points:

- Pay credit card balances in full every month.
- Save and invest 10–20% of post-tax income.
- Hold an emergency fund covering at least six months of expenses.
- Maximize contributions to tax-advantaged accounts such as 401(k)s, individual retirement accounts, and 529 education savings plans.
- When investing, avoid individual securities and use low-cost, diversified mutual funds matched to a target retirement year; if hiring an advisor, require a fiduciary duty to act in the client's interest.

Sound practice also requires behavioral discipline: avoiding emotional attachment to money and keeping a medium- to long-term horizon to ride out expected-return volatility.

## The planning process

Financial planning, the core activity of personal finance, is a repeating cycle of five steps:

1. Assessment. Compile a personal balance sheet (assets such as a car, house, stocks, and bank accounts, minus liabilities such as credit card debt, bank loans, and mortgages) and a personal income statement (income versus expenses).
2. Goal setting. Define short- and long-term targets, for example retiring at 65 with a $1,000,000 net worth, or saving for a new computer next month.
3. Plan creation. Specify actions such as cutting expenses, raising earned income, or opening a fixed deposit.
4. Execution, which often requires discipline and may involve accountants, financial planners, investment advisers, or lawyers.
5. Monitoring and reassessment at regular intervals, with adjustments as life changes.

Common adult goals include paying off credit card, student, housing, or auto debt; investing for retirement; saving for children's college; and covering medical expenses.

## Why individuals need it now

Four pressures make personal finance increasingly necessary.

Formal education is uneven. OECD studies show low financial literacy in countries where it is not required, including developed economies such as Japan, and just under 30% of US high schools did not require personal finance for graduation in 2024. Graduates with financial education tend to have higher credit scores and better loan terms.

The effective employable age is shrinking. Automation, offshoring, and the entry of tech-fluent younger workers mean that mid-career employees who do not upskill can be replaced. Cyclical industries such as autos, chemicals, and construction cut staff quickly during recessions, so a shorter earning window requires earlier retirement saving.

Life expectancy has risen, gradually climbing from about 60 to 81 years and beyond, even in developing economies. A longer retirement on a shorter earnings window forces a larger retirement corpus.

Medical costs for prescriptions, hospital care, nursing, and geriatric care have risen, and many are not fully covered. In the United States, employers, private insurers, Medicare, and Medicaid split the burden, but Medicare's long-term sustainability is uncertain and out-of-pocket costs such as copayments and deductibles remain. In the European Union most care is nationally reimbursed, so newer expensive therapies are often excluded from formularies. In developing countries such as India and China, most medical expenses are paid out of pocket. These gaps make medical, accident, critical-illness, and life insurance, plus an emergency fund, essential.

## Areas of focus

The Financial Planning Standards Board groups personal financial planning into several areas.

Financial position measures net worth (assets minus liabilities at a point in time) and household cash flow (expected annual income minus expected annual expenses) to judge when goals are affordable.

Adequate protection, or insurance, addresses liability, property, death, disability, health, and long-term care risks, and benefits from tax-advantaged structures.

Tax planning matters because income tax is usually a household's largest expense. Progressive taxation means higher marginal rates at higher incomes, and the timing and use of deductions and credits can reduce a lifetime tax bill.

Investment and accumulation goals cover large purchases and life events such as a home, a car, education, and retirement. The chief threat is inflation, so portfolios must earn enough real return to outpace it, balanced through asset allocation across stocks, bonds, cash, and alternatives according to the investor's risk tolerance. Depreciating assets such as vehicles lose value over time and should be tracked separately from appreciating assets.

Retirement planning estimates the cost of living in retirement and uses structures such as IRAs and employer plans to manage tax liability. Estate planning arranges the disposition of assets after death and seeks to reduce estate taxes. Cash management, the practical core of every plan, requires tracking expenses as carefully as income. Delayed gratification, the willingness to forgo an immediate reward for a larger later one, underpins regular saving and avoiding high-interest debt.

## Housing and credit

Housing is a basic need and a major financial decision. Buying ties up capital in a down payment, mortgage payments, maintenance, insurance, and property taxes, but builds equity and can raise a credit score. Renting eliminates maintenance and property tax and eases relocation. Most mortgages run 15 or 30 years, with either a fixed rate or an adjustable-rate mortgage whose payment can change with market rates. Location, commute, school quality, safety, and amenities affect both livability and resale value.

Credit, the ability to receive goods or services now with a promise to repay later, often with interest, comes in unsecured forms (personal loans, student loans, credit cards) and secured forms (auto loans, mortgages). A credit score from 300 to 850 measures borrower trustworthiness and depends on payment history, low balances, length of credit history, recent applications, and mix of account types. Used carefully, credit builds a score, finances large purchases, bridges emergencies, and earns rewards when balances are paid in full each month. Used carelessly, it leads to overuse, compounding interest and fees, and minimum payments that grow until they overwhelm the borrower.
