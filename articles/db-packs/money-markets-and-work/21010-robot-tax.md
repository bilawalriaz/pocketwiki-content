# Robot tax

A robot tax is a proposed law that makes companies pay for replacing human workers with machines, both to slow that replacement and to fund help for the people who lose their jobs. Automation of manual work has been discussed since before the Industrial Revolution, but the debate has sharpened in the 21st century as machine learning has widened the range of tasks that can be automated.

The risk is real but uncertain. One study found 47% of the United States workforce is automatable; another found only 9% across 21 OECD countries. The gap matters: any tax built on automation forecasts will be guessing what share of work actually disappears.

## What a robot tax is supposed to do

The policy has two aims. First, raise the cost of substituting a machine for a worker so firms automate more slowly. Second, raise revenue to cushion displaced workers, who mostly cluster in low-income, routine jobs and lack the means to move into the high-skill fields that are growing. Economists call this pattern "income polarization": the middle hollows out as routine work vanishes and demand concentrates at the top.

## Main arguments for a tax

- 1940: US Senator Joseph C. O'Mahoney tabled an early automation tax bill in the Senate.
- 2017: San Francisco supervisor Jane Kim set up a task force, saying robot-driven income disparity was visible in her district.
- 2017: UK Labour leader Jeremy Corbyn called for a robot tax.
- 2019: New York mayor Bill de Blasio, while running for president, proposed making large corporations pay five years of income tax for each job they automated away. He credited Andrew Yang for popularising the issue but argued for a different remedy.
- Bill Gates has backed a robot tax, following Elon Musk's argument that universal basic income (a government cash payment to every adult) will be needed to offset job losses from machines.
- Mark Cuban endorsed the idea in 2017, citing an essay by Quincy Larson on accelerating technological unemployment.
- Tax scholar Xavier Oberson has argued that robots themselves should eventually be made tax-compliant so government revenue does not collapse as human wages shrink.

Francisco Ossandón argues a *limited* robot tax could work if it (i) targets large companies that deploy robots, (ii) is restricted to specific activities such as some industrial or financial sectors, (iii) uses a tight definition of "robot" (physical smart machines, or intelligent software in financial work), and (iv) carries a low rate. He sees no case for a broad, general tax.

## Main arguments against a tax

- Definitional problem: critics Jim Stanford and Tshilidzi Marwala point out that autonomous components are now embedded in ordinary devices that nobody calls robots, so the tax base is hard to draw. Yanis Varoufakis adds that no one can credibly say what a human worker *would have* earned in a sector that robots have dominated for decades, which makes any displaced-wage baseline arbitrary. He favours a "universal basic dividend" instead, funded broadly and paid to all citizens, as the remedy for polarization.
- Innovation penalty: robotics firms including Savioke, the Advancing Automation trade group, and ABB CEO Ulrich Spiesshofer argue the tax would punish the very investment that drives growth. Spiesshofer noted that low-unemployment countries tend to have high automation, undercutting the assumption that the two trade off against each other.
- Competitiveness: EU Commissioner Andrus Ansip warned that any country or region taxing robots would push technology firms to relocate.
- Productivity and avoidance: the 2019 World Development Report, written by Simeon Djankov and Federica Saliala of the World Bank, opposed the tax, predicting lower productivity and more tax avoidance by large corporations and their shareholders.

## Where robot taxes actually exist

Very few places have one. On 6 August 2017, South Korea under President Moon passed what is widely called the first robot tax, but it works indirectly: it does not levy a new charge, it withdraws existing tax breaks that had rewarded investment in robotics, so the effective cost of automating rises.

Earlier, a robot tax had appeared in Mady Delvaux's European Parliament bill on ethical standards for robots, but lawmakers stripped that provision from the final text.

By removing a subsidy rather than adding a levy, the South Korean design avoids the "what counts as a robot" problem that has stalled broader proposals. Its narrower base also means it cannot directly fund income support for displaced workers, which is the other half of what most proponents want.
