# Gibbs' inequality

Gibbs' inequality states that the information entropy of a discrete probability distribution P is never larger than the expected surprise of using P to score outcomes produced by another distribution Q. It is the foundation from which several other entropy bounds, including Fano's inequality, are derived, and was first presented by J. Willard Gibbs in the 19th century, before the modern language of information theory existed.

Let P = {p₁, …, pₙ} and Q = {q₁, …, qₙ} be discrete probability distributions. Then

$$-\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \log p_i \;\le\; -\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \log q_i,$$

with equality if and only if pᵢ = qᵢ for every i. The left-hand side is the information entropy of P. The right-hand side is the cross entropy between P and Q.

Rewriting in the standard form gives the most widely used consequence, the non-negativity of Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence, also called relative entropy:

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q) \;\equiv\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \log \frac{p_i}{q_i} \;\ge\; 0.$$

The base of the logarithm is optional; using base 2 makes both sides "average surprisals" measured in bits.

## Why it is true

The proof rests on a single elementary fact about the natural logarithm: for all x > 0,

$$\ln x \;\le\; x - 1,$$

with equality if and only if x = 1. Writing the KL divergence with natural logs and substituting x = qᵢ / pᵢ gives

$$-\sum_{i \in I} p_i \ln \frac{q_i}{p_i} \;\ge\; -\sum_{i \in I} p_i\!\left(\frac{q_i}{p_i} - 1\right) \;=\; -\sum_{i \in I} q_i + 1 \;\ge\; 0,$$

where I is the set of indices where pᵢ > 0. The last step uses the fact that the qᵢ sum to at most 1 over I, because some qᵢ may be zero where pᵢ is positive, so their restricted sum is ≤ 1. The general n-term version follows because terms with pᵢ = 0 contribute 0 to pᵢ ln pᵢ and are harmless on the right.

Equality requires two things. First, qᵢ / pᵢ = 1 for every i with pᵢ > 0. Second, the restricted sum of the qᵢ must equal 1, forcing qᵢ = 0 wherever pᵢ = 0. Together these mean p and q are the same distribution. Choosing a different base just multiplies everything by 1 / ln b, so the inequality and its equality condition are unchanged.

## Other ways to see it

Jensen's inequality yields the result in a few lines. Because log is strictly concave,

$$\sum_i p_i \log \frac{q_i}{p_i} \;\le\; \log \sum_i p_i \frac{q_i}{p_i} \;=\; \log \sum_i q_i \;=\; 0,$$

and the equality case of Jensen forces all the ratios qᵢ / pᵢ to share a common value σ. Summing gives σ = 1, so equality holds only when P = Q. The result also follows directly from the log sum inequality, or from the observation that the KL divergence is the Bregman divergence generated by t ↦ ln t, which makes the non-negativity a general property of Bregman divergences rather than a peculiarity of entropy.

## A direct consequence

Choosing Q to be the uniform distribution, where every qᵢ = 1 / n, produces the classical upper bound on entropy:

$$H(p_1, \ldots, p_n) \;\le\; \log n,$$

with equality when P itself is uniform. Among all distributions on n outcomes, the uniform distribution has the largest entropy.
