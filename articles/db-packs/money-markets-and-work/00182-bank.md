# Bank

A bank is a financial institution that takes deposits from the public, lets customers withdraw on demand, and lends out most of that money. Under **fractional-reserve banking**, the bank keeps only a fraction of deposits as cash reserves and lends the rest. Each new loan creates a new deposit in the borrower's account, which circulates as money; when the loan is repaid, the money disappears. The bank profits from the **interest spread** between the low rate it pays depositors and the higher rate it charges borrowers, plus transaction fees.

Because banks borrow short (deposits withdrawable at any time) and lend long (mortgages and corporate loans repaid over years), every bank carries an inherent mismatch. It is managed with reserves, marketable securities, and wholesale funding, and is ultimately backstopped by a **central bank** acting as **lender of last resort** in a crisis. Three economic functions follow directly: settlement (banks net each other's payments, saving reserves), credit quality improvement (a diversified portfolio of average borrowers becomes a high-quality debtor through diversification and capital), and money creation (each loan mints new money, each repayment destroys it).

## Risks, capital, and regulation

Banks face six main risks: **credit risk** (borrowers default), **liquidity risk** (assets cannot be sold fast enough to meet withdrawals), **market risk** (portfolio value falls), **operational risk** (systems or staff fail), **reputational risk**, and macroeconomic risk. **Capital** — equity, retained earnings, subordinated debt — absorbs losses before depositors lose anything. The **Basel Accords** set international rules for minimum risk-weighted capital, and after 2008 regulators pushed **Contingent Convertible Bonds (CoCos)**: hybrid securities that convert to equity or write down when capital falls below a trigger, soaking up losses while the bank still operates.

A banking licence requires minimum capital, vetted owners, and an approved business plan. Supervision sits mainly with central banks, though the US is fragmented across the FDIC, the Fed, the OCC, and state agencies, which creates coordination problems. Banking law implies contractual duties: honouring cheques up to balance or overdraft, collecting deposits, secrecy with exceptions, combining accounts, and lien rights on deposits.

## Channels, products, and bank types

Customers reach banks through branches, ATMs, online and mobile apps, call centres, and relationship managers. Retail products include checking and savings accounts, certificates of deposit, IRAs, debit and credit cards, mortgages, and personal loans. Commercial banking adds revolving credit, cash management, FX and derivatives, and capital-raising in equity, debt, or hybrid form.

Specialist variants include **investment banks** (underwriting and trading securities), **merchant banks** (equity capital for companies), **universal banks** (combined commercial, investment, and insurance — the latter called **bancassurance**), **credit unions** and **community banks**, **postal savings banks**, **offshore** and **ethical** banks, and **Islamic banks** (which avoid interest, or *riba*, and earn through profit markups and fees). **Central banks** sit above them all: government-owned, they supervise, set interest rates, supply liquidity, and backstop the system.

## Accounting and the legal nature of deposits

Under standard accounting, a customer deposit is a **liability** of the bank — the bank owes the customer — and increases on the credit side; a withdrawal is a debit. A positive balance appears as a credit on a statement; an overdraft appears as a debit. Legally, an ordinary deposit became classified in the 19th century as a **mutuum**: a loan from the depositor to the bank, which must return an equivalent sum, not the specific coins or notes handed over. This is why a deposit is simultaneously the depositor's asset and the bank's liability, and why the bank can lend it.

**Brokered deposits** are large, rate-sensitive sums placed by third-party brokers. High reliance correlates with risk-taking: 2008 failed banks averaged roughly four times the brokered-deposit share of survivors. Regulators restrict them; banks argue they fund deposit-poor areas.

## Brief history

Banking descends from ancient credit practices and the goldsmiths of 17th-century London, who stored gold, issued paper receipts, and discovered they could lend against those deposits — inventing fractional reserve and the assignable banknote. The **Bank of England** standardised banknotes in 1695, and the **Royal Bank of Scotland** introduced the overdraft in 1728. Modern Italian banking began earlier: the **Medici Bank** (Florence, 1397) pioneered international branches, the **Taula de canvi de Barcelona** (1401) was an early state deposit bank, and **Banca Monte dei Paschi di Siena** (1472) and **Berenberg Bank** (1590) still operate. Twentieth-century telecommunications and computing enabled consolidation and global reach. The 2008 financial crisis wiped out about 85% of profits at the world's 1,000 largest banks and triggered CoCos and tighter Basel rules. The March 2023 crisis — three US bank failures plus collapses abroad — showed how fast digital-era liquidity runs can become fatal.

## Open tensions

Five debates shape the industry. First, whether the US's fragmented multi-agency supervision creates gaps or provides useful specialisation. Second, whether brokered deposits are a lifeline for under-banked regions or, as 2008 data showed, a fuel for excessive risk. Third, whether a true "global consumer" exists to sustain cross-border retail banking; **Citigroup** retreated in 2021 and **HSBC** in 2022–23 after decades of trying. Fourth, whether large banks can keep financing fossil fuels while staying aligned with the Paris Agreement. Fifth, whether legal definitions of banking, still written around cheques, should be rewritten for an era of EFTPOS, app payments, and mobile transfers — and how to extend the regulatory perimeter to **shadow banking**, where money market funds and direct capital-market borrowing drain deposits and risks from the regulated system.
