# Bank account

A bank account is a financial record kept by a bank or other financial institution that tracks the transactions between that institution and a customer. Each institution writes its own terms and conditions for each account type it offers, and those terms form a contract once the customer is accepted. A customer may hold several accounts at once, and each carries an account number the bank uses to route transactions.

## Legal nature: a claim, not cash

Money deposited in a bank is no longer the depositor's property. In most legal systems, a deposit is not a bailment, so the physical cash handed over becomes the bank's asset. The depositor instead holds a claim against the bank for the sum deposited. The bank records the receipt as a transaction in an account opened in the name chosen by the depositor. In accounting terms, a deposit account is a liability of the bank and an asset of the depositor; the loan or credit account reverses that, since it is an asset of the bank and a liability of the borrower. Loan accounts may be unsecured, secured by collateral, or guaranteed by a third party.

## Balances and statements

An account can carry a credit balance, meaning the bank owes the customer, or a debit balance, meaning the customer owes the bank. Accounts that normally hold credit balances are deposit accounts; accounts opened to hold debit balances are loan accounts. Some accounts can flip between the two. A savings account routinely sits in credit regardless of the underlying legal rule. The bank's periodic bank statement reports the transactions on the account over a chosen period, and the balance at any moment is the customer's financial position with that institution.

## Opening and account types

Per-country laws govern who may open an account, how signatories identify themselves, and deposit or withdrawal limits. The minimum opening age is usually 18, sometimes 16, and minors' accounts are typically operated by a parent or guardian; opening an account in a false name is generally unlawful. Beyond deposit and loan accounts, institutions offer transaction accounts (checking or current accounts), savings accounts, time deposits and certificates of deposit, money-market accounts, securities accounts, joint accounts, low-cost accounts, numbered accounts, and Negotiable Order of Withdrawal accounts.
