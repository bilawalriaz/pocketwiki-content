# Business ethics

Business ethics is applied ethics that asks which actions a company, and the people inside it, ought to take when profit-seeking collides with obligations to workers, customers, communities, and the law. Normative business ethics prescribes standards of conduct; descriptive business ethics studies what firms actually do. The tension between profit and other concerns is the field's recurring problem.

## Why the field exists

Business has long involved practices now considered wrong: slavery, colonialism, and Cold War commerce were once routine. The term "business ethics" entered common use in the United States in the early 1970s. The Society for Business Ethics was founded in 1980. By the mid-1980s, over 500 courses reached 40,000 students. Early-2000s scandals including Enron and WorldCom prompted the Sarbanes–Oxley Act and a wave of ethics-officer appointments.

## Law is not ethics

Law is the minimum written standard, enforced by the state. Ethics covers everything else: customs, professional norms, and voluntary principles. Slavery was once legal in the US, yet enslaving a person was not ethical. An action can be unethical without being illegal, such as exploiting a loophole, misleading a customer, or mistreating a worker within the law. Law is retroactive: a crime must occur before a law against it can be written, so law lags moral concern.

## The central debate: purpose of the firm

Milton Friedman's doctrine holds that executives have one responsibility: maximise profit for shareholders while obeying law and ethical custom. A 2011 multi-country survey found 30 to 80% of "informed publics" endorsed this view. Critics like Ronald Duska argue that profit is the motive of business, not its purpose: society permits business to exist so it can provide goods and services. Peter Drucker said personal ethics cover all business situations and that the ultimate director duty is *primum non nocere*, first, do no harm. Corporate social responsibility (CSR) adds that a firm must act as a responsible citizen of its communities, sometimes at the cost of profit.

## Functional domains

Finance is often framed as ethically neutral because it is treated as mathematics, yet the 2008 financial crisis showed that ethics in executive suites and regulators matter. Documented abuses include insider trading, securities fraud, creative accounting, misleading financial analysis, bribery, kickbacks, and facilitation payments. Marketing came of age in the 1990s and covers pricing fairness, advertising truthfulness, consumer privacy, and avoiding greenwashing, bait-and-switch, and pyramid schemes. Human resources covers discrimination by age, gender, race, religion, or disability, plus workplace safety, privacy, comparable worth, due process, and the right to blow the whistle. Procurement requires fair treatment of suppliers, including those in their supply chains.

## Religion and philosophy

Religious traditions supply some of the oldest frameworks. The Tamil *Tirukkuṛaḷ* (dated variously 300 BCE to the 7th century CE) treats business ethics explicitly. Sharia prohibits interest on loans. Confucian thought discourages pure profit-seeking. Jewish, Christian, Hindu (Dharma versus Adharma), and Buddhist thought all treat business conduct as a moral domain.

## Property and intellectual property

Property is a bundle of rights among people, not a thing-owner relationship. Custodianship brings obligations alongside rights. Neoliberals treat private property as a natural right; critics treat it as a political construction. Intellectual property treats ideas and expressions as a form of property enforceable by state-granted monopolies, including patents and copyrights. Critics argue IP creates artificial scarcity and slows innovation, while defenders point to the need to recoup development costs, for example in pharmaceuticals. The 39 pharmaceutical companies' suit against South Africa's 1997 HIV-medicines law is often cited as evidence of harm.

## Implementation

Many firms adopt ethics codes and appoint ethics officers. The Defense Industry Initiative was created in the mid-1980s after defense-industry scandals. The Federal Sentencing Guidelines for Organizations (1991) rewarded firms that maintained effective compliance programmes. Sustainability has expanded the scope to include environmental stewardship and social issues such as workers' rights, child labour, and human trafficking. In 2025, some companies began reducing or eliminating diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) commitments. Critics note a frequent gap between written codes and actual practice, raising the question of whether codes are genuine guidance or reputational cover.
