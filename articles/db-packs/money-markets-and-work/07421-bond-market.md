# Bond market

The bond market is the financial market where debt is created and traded. New debt is sold in the **primary market**; existing debt is bought and sold in the **secondary market**. The instrument is usually a bond, but notes, bills, and similar debt securities are included. The market is also called the debt market or credit market.

A bond is a loan with a fixed contract: the borrower (issuer) receives money up front and promises to repay the principal on a set date, plus scheduled interest (the coupon). Most bonds pay a fixed coupon; some pay a floating rate reset before each payment. Zero-coupon bonds pay no interest and are sold at a deep discount that represents the implied interest.

## Size and structure

In 2026 the global bond market was estimated at about $143 trillion outstanding, with the United States at roughly 40% (around $58 trillion), according to SIFMA (the Securities Industry and Financial Markets Association). The global credit market, bonds plus bank loans, is about three times the size of the global equity market. As a share of world GDP, the bond market grew from about 80% in the early 2000s to over 140% in 2011, driven largely by increased government issuance after the 2008 slowdown.

SIFMA divides the broader market into five segments: corporate, government and agency, municipal, mortgage-backed and other asset-backed (including collateralized debt obligations), and funding. A 2017 snapshot of the US market: Treasury 35.2%, mortgage-related 22.6%, corporate debt 21.8%, municipal 9.6%, agency securities 5.0%, asset-backed 3.5%, and money markets 2.4%, totaling about $39.7 trillion. Around 10% of US bonds are held directly by individuals; the rest sits with institutions such as pension funds, banks, and mutual funds. There are over 500,000 unique corporate bonds outstanding in the US.

## How it is organized and traded

Almost all US bond trading takes place over the counter (OTC) between broker-dealers and large institutions. Only a small share, mostly corporate bonds, is listed on exchanges. Prices and volumes are reported through FINRA's Trade Reporting and Compliance Engine (TRACE). Standard retail trade sizes run in $1,000 to $10,000 increments; for dealers, anything below $100,000 is an odd lot. Bonds are priced as a percentage of par (100), so a quote of 98 means 98% of face value. Interest on most bonds is taxed as ordinary income, whereas qualified stock dividends are taxed at lower rates, though many government and municipal bonds are exempt from some taxes. Smaller investors can participate through bond funds, closed-end funds, unit-investment trusts, and exchange-traded funds (ETFs).

## Bonds versus bank loans

Bonds and bank loans together form the credit market, but they differ in important ways. In the US, bonds are generally securities under the Securities and Exchange Act and are more heavily regulated; bank loans are not. Bonds are usually unsecured, are sold in small denominations, can be held by retail investors, and trade more frequently than loans, though still less than equity.

## Why interest rates drive prices

The defining mechanic of the bond market is the inverse relationship between bond prices and interest rates. When market rates rise, newly issued bonds pay higher yields, making existing lower-yielding bonds worth less. When rates fall, existing bonds with higher coupons become more valuable. Anyone who holds a bond to maturity is unaffected by price swings along the way; principal and interest are paid on the pre-set schedule.

This sensitivity makes the bond market a leading indicator of expected interest rates and of the **yield curve**, the line drawn through yields across different maturities. Before the mid-1970s, each maturity was treated as a separate market; traders at Salomon Brothers then began drawing a continuous curve through those yields, an innovation that reshaped pricing and gave rise to modern quantitative finance.

Government bonds, especially US Treasuries and German bunds, are treated as the closest practical proxy for a risk-free rate, because their issuers are seen as the least likely to default. Other bonds in the same currency must offer higher yields to compensate for the additional risk that the borrower will fail to pay on time or in full.

## How a bond issuer reaches investors

A bank's debt capital markets desk splits this work in two. The origination and syndication desks, the "manufacturers," structure the deal for the issuer and place it. The sales and trading desks, the "distributors," then sell the new bonds to end investors. Once outstanding, the bonds can be resold in the secondary market, which gives the market its depth and liquidity. Participants are either issuers raising funds or investors supplying them, and most large players do both. The main groups are governments, financial institutions, institutional investors such as pension funds and mutual funds, and individuals. Because individual issues are highly specific and many smaller ones are illiquid, institutions hold the vast majority.

## Volatility

For buy-and-hold investors, volatility is irrelevant; cash flows are locked in. For traders, volatility is dominated by interest-rate changes. Rates themselves respond to expected monetary policy and the business cycle. Economic data releases move the market when they differ from consensus, because traders reprice their expectations of future rates; "in-line" data causes little movement, while surprises cause sharp repricing, with the size of the move depending on how much uncertainty existed beforehand.

## Brief history

The first known bond, dating to around 2400 BC in Nippur, Mesopotamia, guaranteed payment in grain, with a surety bond covering the issuer's obligation. In ancient Sumer temples acted as banks under priestly oversight, lending at a customary 20% interest rate later written into the Code of Hammurabi. The modern bond market began in 12th-century Venice, whose government issued war bonds called *prestiti*, perpetuities paying a fixed 5%, the first widely traded sovereign debt instruments. After the Hundred Years' War, English and French monarchs defaulted on large debts to Venetian bankers, collapsing Lombard banking in 1345; Venice then banned its bankers from trading government debt, but the idea of tradable debt survived.

The first sovereign bond in the modern sense was issued in 1693 by the newly formed Bank of England, to fund conflict with France. The United States followed, issuing Treasury bonds to finance the Revolutionary War and again as Liberty Bonds in 1917. Non-investment-grade public companies were first allowed to issue corporate debt in the late 1970s, and the 1980s brought derivatives, residential mortgage-backed securities, collateralized debt obligations, and the broader structured products industry.
