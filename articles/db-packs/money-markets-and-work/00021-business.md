# Business

## Overview

Business is the practice of making a living or making money by producing, buying, and selling goods and services. It is any activity entered into for profit. The term is legally distinct from "company" (a corporation or cooperative), though the two are used interchangeably in everyday speech. Business is the main source of employment and economic value creation in nearly all countries, and it operates within a layered system of legal forms, management practices, and regulations.

## Forms of Ownership

The legal shape of a business depends on jurisdiction. A **sole proprietorship** is owned by one person who carries unlimited liability, meaning personal assets cover all business debts. A **partnership** is owned by two or more people, with most forms granting each partner unlimited liability. A **corporation** gives owners **limited liability** and has a legal personality separate from its owners; it can be government- or privately-owned and organized for profit or non-profit. A **cooperative** has members rather than shareholders, who share decision-making authority. A **franchise** is a system where entrepreneurs buy the right to open and run a business from a larger corporation; in the US, one in twelve retail businesses is franchised, employing about 8 million people.

Other forms include a **company limited by guarantee** (often used for clubs or charities), a **company limited by shares** (the most common form, with shareholder liability limited to their investment), and an **unlimited company**, where member liability is not limited. Companies split into **public companies** (shares trade publicly) and **private companies** (no publicly traded shares). A **parent company** owns enough voting stock in another firm, its **subsidiary**, to control management and operations.

## Classifications by Activity

Businesses are grouped by economic activity. **Agriculture** involves growing crops, raising livestock, and producing lumber. **Mining** extracts natural resources such as petroleum and ores. **Service businesses** offer intangible work, for example interior decorators or dry cleaners. **Financial services** include banks, insurance companies, and investment firms that profit through capital management. Other categories include **transportation** (railways, airlines), **utilities** (water, electricity), **entertainment and mass media** (film studios, publishers), **sports organizations**, **industrial manufacturers** of tangible goods, **real estate**, and **retailers and wholesalers** who act as middlemen.

## Core Activities

A business coordinates several recurring activities. **Accounting** measures, processes, and communicates financial information; Luca Pacioli established the modern field in 1494. **Commerce** is the exchange of goods and services, including trade and auxiliary services like logistics and finance. **Finance** keeps a firm solvent by managing cash flow, servicing debt, and maximizing value. **Human resources (HR)** finds, screens, recruits, and trains job applicants. **Information technology (IT)** supports the computer systems a firm depends on. **Manufacturing** produces merchandise using labour and machines. **Marketing** creates, communicates, delivers, and exchanges offerings of value, and now includes **digital marketing**. **Research and development (R&D)** is the first stage of building a new product or service. **Safety** management reduces incidents and insurance costs. **Sales** is the activity of selling goods or services.

## Management, Organization, and Capital

**Management** is the efficient and effective operation of a business, with branches covering finance, marketing, and human resources. **Business process management (BPM)** aligns an organization with client needs and improves processes over time. The factors that shape business organization are size, sector, country, tax advantages, disclosure requirements, and coordination needs.

Corporations face **corporate tax rates**, which can produce **double taxation**, where profit is taxed at the corporate level and dividends are taxed again when paid to shareholders. "Going public" through an **initial public offering (IPO)** requires disclosing information and meeting stricter rules. Businesses raise **capital** by selling securities on exchanges such as the NYSE, NASDAQ, or London Stock Exchange, or through venture capital and bank loans. **Intellectual property** (patents, copyrights, trademarks, and trade secrets) often needs legal protection for a company to stay profitable.

## Regulation and Commercial Law

Jurisdictions set the forms of ownership a business can take and the rules for commercial dealings; the need to regulate trade helped create law and courts. Regulations govern labour, worker protection, discrimination, minimum wage, and working hours, and specialized fields such as law, medicine, and liquor sales require licenses. Public companies face governance rules enforced by bodies such as the SEC in the US, the CSRC in China, the MAS in Singapore, and the SFC in Hong Kong. The complexity of business law has driven growing specialization in corporate law, including securities, tax, and intellectual property.

## Exploitation of Labour and Trade Unions

Some businesses lobby for immigration of foreign workers who accept lower salaries than domestic workers. A **trade union** is an organization of workers who unite to protect their trade, improve safety, and win higher pay, benefits, and better working conditions. Union leadership bargains with employers on behalf of members and negotiates labor contracts through **collective bargaining**, covering wages, work rules, complaint procedures, hiring and firing rules, benefits, and workplace safety.
