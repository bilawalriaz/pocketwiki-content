# Corporation

A corporation is a state-authorised legal person, distinct from the people who own it. That separation is the central idea: the entity can own property, sign contracts, sue and be sued, and keep existing long after its founders have died, while the people behind it risk only what they choose to invest. Those two properties, **legal personality** and **limited liability**, are what make the modern corporation possible.

## Origins

Rome recognised corporate bodies (*collegia*, *Universitas*) for municipalities, guilds, and religious cults. After the *Lex Julia* (49–44 BC) they required Senate or Imperial approval to act as legal units. Justinian's *Corpus Juris Civilis* (527–565) preserved the concept, and 11th–14th century European jurists revived it, drawing on the "body politic" metaphor. Medieval Europe incorporated churches, municipalities, and a few commercial ventures such as Stora Kopparberg (chartered 1347), but most business collaboration still relied on common-law partnerships and regulated guilds.

## Chartered monopoly and crisis

In the 17th century the corporate form became a tool of state power. The Dutch East India Company (VOC, 1602) and English East India Company (1600) were chartered colonial-trade monopolies; the VOC issued tradable shares and embedded limited liability in its charter. The South Sea Company collapsed in 1720, shares falling from £1,000 to under £100, and Parliament responded with the Bubble Act, banning unchartered companies for a century. The Act's 1825 repeal, under pressure from industrial-era ventures forced into unincorporated associations, reopened incorporation to ordinary business.

## The legal revolution, 1844–1862

Britain rebuilt company law in four statutes, and other jurisdictions copied the result.

| Year | Act | What changed |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1844 | Joint Stock Companies Act | Created a Registrar and allowed incorporation by registration, no charter needed. |
| 1855 | Limited Liability Act | Capped each shareholder's loss at the unpaid part of their shares (firms over 25 members; insurance excluded until 1862). |
| 1856 | Joint Stock Companies Act | Codified registration and limited liability together. |
| 1862 | Companies Act | Consolidated the earlier laws; remained in force through *Salomon*. |

The 1844 Act gave companies legal personality but not limited liability, so early incorporators still faced unlimited losses. Adding limited liability in 1855 was the decisive step: dispersed savers could now fund large ventures without risking their other property. *The Economist* later ranked the idea alongside Watt's steam engine and Stephenson's locomotive as a pillar of the Industrial Revolution.

The new freedom produced a railway boom and a wave of insolvencies. Germany's 1892 *GmbH* law extended separate personality to single-owner firms, and the House of Lords' 1897 decision in *Salomon v. Salomon & Co.* confirmed that even a one-person company is a separate legal person shielded from its owner. In the United States, New Jersey (1896) and Delaware (1899) passed general enabling statutes; New Jersey repealed its law in 1913, and Delaware's permissive regime has dominated US incorporation ever since.

## Ownership and control

In a **joint-stock company** ownership is divided into transferable shares, and shareholders' voting power, dividends, and say in the firm scale with their holdings. **Non-stock corporations** (cooperatives, credit unions, charities) have no shares; membership is defined by the founding document.

Day-to-day control sits with a board. Common-law countries usually use a **single board** mixing executive and non-executive directors; civil-law countries often use a **two-tier structure** with a supervisory board over a managing board. Germany adds **co-determination**, a statutory right for workers to elect part of the supervisory board. In practice, ownership and control diverge: dispersed shareholders rarely manage, and the people who run the firm answer to the people who own it only loosely.

## Formation

Modern incorporation is paperwork. The founder files **articles of incorporation** (purpose, authorised stock, initial directors) with the state registrar, pays a fee, designates a registered agent to accept lawsuits, and adopts bylaws for internal rules. Under the **internal affairs doctrine**, the law of the state of incorporation governs disputes between shareholders and managers, even if the company operates everywhere. Operating in another state requires registering as a **foreign corporation** and appointing a local agent; the host state's law still applies to employment, contracts, and torts. Names usually need a suffix (*Inc.*, *Ltd.*, *LLC*) to put third parties on notice that liability is limited, and must be unique within the registering jurisdiction.

## Personhood and open questions

Because a corporation is a legal person, it can hold rights and bear duties in its own name: own property, sue, be sued, and in some jurisdictions be convicted of crimes such as fraud or corporate manslaughter. It can be dissolved by statute, court order, or shareholder vote; insolvency more often leads to restructuring than liquidation. A corporation generally cannot own its own shares except as **treasury stock**, treated as unissued capital rather than an asset.

That bundle of rights is contested. Joel Bakan argues that the legal duty to maximise shareholder profit produces a personality that externalises harm onto workers, communities, and the environment. David Runciman reads corporate personhood as evidence that the modern state is itself built from corporate forms, blurring the line between public power and private capital. Older disputes persist: whether limited liability lets owners dodge responsibility for harm, whether stakeholder governance can replace shareholder primacy, and whether competition between jurisdictions drives corporate-law standards down to the floor or up to the ceiling.

The working model: a corporation is a legal machine for pooling capital from strangers. The machine runs on two inventions, separate legal personality and limited liability, both of which became freely available through registration between 1844 and 1862.
