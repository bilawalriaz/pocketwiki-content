# Energy return on investment

Energy return on investment (EROI) is the ratio of usable energy delivered by a resource to the energy expended to deliver it:

EROI = Energy Delivered / Energy Required to Deliver that Energy

An EROI of 1 is break-even: 1 unit invested yields 1 unit returned, so the net energy gain is zero. Below 1 the source becomes a net "energy sink" that consumes more energy than it produces. A resource generally needs an EROI of at least 3:1 to function as a prominent fuel or energy source, though researchers argue for higher thresholds when full supply-chain costs are included. A related ratio, ESOEI (energy stored on energy invested), evaluates storage devices rather than primary sources.

The concept was popularised by systems ecologist Charles A. S. Hall, whose 1984 paper on the cover of *Science* brought energy analysis to wide attention.

## How EROI is calculated in practice

Only human-applied energy counts as the input. Solar insolation driving photosynthesis for biofuels, or the stellar energy that originally produced fissile uranium, is not counted; only the energy used to plant, harvest, and process crops, or to mine and enrich uranium, enters the denominator. On the output side, only useful energy counts: waste heat is excluded unless it is actually captured (for example, in district heating or desalination), and such capture remains rare in practice.

EROI and net energy gain measure the same property of a source in different ways:

(Gross Energy Yield ÷ Energy Expended) = EROI

(Net Energy ÷ Energy Expended) + 1 = EROI

A process with an EROI of 5 that expends 1 unit of energy produces 4 units of net energy. The time needed to recoup the invested energy is the energy payback time (EPBT).

## Measured EROIs across energy sources

Reported values vary widely because methods, system boundaries, and insolation (the solar radiation received per unit area) assumptions differ. Insolation matters for solar technologies because output depends on local sunlight, not only on the hardware itself.

Storage devices are rated instead by ESOEI. Lithium-ion batteries sit near 32, while pumped hydroelectric storage (704) and compressed-air storage (792) score far higher, meaning that pairing wind with batteries alone, without pumped storage, yields a poor combined return.

## Why EROI trends downward

When oil was first exploited in the United States, roughly one barrel of oil was needed to find, extract, and process about 100 barrels recovered. The US fossil-fuel discovery ratio fell from about 1000:1 in 1919 to roughly 5:1 in the 2010s as the easiest reserves were depleted. Projections for oil liquids and natural gas show the same pattern, with weighted-average EROIs expected to decline sharply over the 21st century as remaining resources become more diffuse, deeper, or more energy-intensive to process. When measured at the stage of useful energy delivered to end users (rather than at the point of generation), 2024 research finds that fossil fuels score lower than renewable electricity sources even after accounting for intermittency.

## Why EROI matters economically and historically

When the EROI of an economy's main energy sources falls, energy becomes harder to obtain and its relative price tends to rise, squeezing the surplus available to fund everything else in society. The concept of "energy slaves" captures this: high-EROI fuels effectively multiply human labour.

Historians have used EROI analysis to study past civilisations. Thomas Homer-Dixon estimates that Roman agrarian production yielded roughly 1:12 for wheat and 1:27 for alfalfa (about 1:2.7 for the oxen that ate it), barely sustaining a peak imperial population. Ecological damage, including deforestation and soil-fertility loss in southern Spain, southern Italy, Sicily, and especially North Africa, began degrading those returns in the 2nd century CE, and by 1084 Rome's population had fallen from about 1.5 million under Trajan to roughly 15,000. Similar dynamics have been proposed for the Mayan and Cambodian collapses. Joseph Tainter argues that diminishing EROI returns are a general cause of societal collapse, with peak wood (the depletion of accessible timber) often the binding limit in pre-industrial societies.

## How far should the input accounting go?

Measuring output is relatively straightforward; measuring input is not. Should the energy used to make the steel in an oil derrick be counted? The energy to build the steel mill? The roads that transport the equipment? The steelworkers' breakfasts? Researchers have no agreed answer. A 2010 paper by Murphy and Hall proposed an "extended" boundary that includes the full supply chain, and argued that an EROI of 5 under that protocol is the minimum threshold for sustainability. Under Hall's broader methodology, 12–13 is considered the minimum required to support a society that maintains technological progress and high art. The International Energy Agency has promoted a narrower method, especially for photovoltaics, that counts only factory-process energy and produces higher, more favourable numbers. Hall has observed that much published work is produced by advocates or those with business ties to the technologies being assessed, and that funding for neutral analysis has been limited.

Two related concerns extend the analysis. Energy cannibalism describes a situation in which rapid growth of an energy technology consumes so much of its own product during construction that it slows its own expansion; a "solar breeder," a PV manufacturing plant powered by its own rooftop panels, is designed to avoid this. And energy quality matters: electricity can be converted to motion more efficiently than thermal energy because of its lower entropy, so two fuels with identical EROIs can differ substantially in real usefulness depending on whether they yield electricity, liquid fuel, or low-grade heat.
