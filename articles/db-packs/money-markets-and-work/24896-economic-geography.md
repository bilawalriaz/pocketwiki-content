# Economic geography

Economic geography is the subfield of human geography that studies where economic activity occurs and what shapes those locations. It is also treated as a subfield or method within economics. Practitioners examine industrial location, economies of agglomeration (cost advantages firms gain by clustering, also called "linkages"), transportation, international trade, real estate, ethnic and gendered economies, core-periphery theory, urban form, environment-economy interactions, and globalization.

## Two traditions with similar names

Two intellectual traditions now dominate, and their near-identical names cause confusion.

The first is Marxist political economy in geography, associated with David Harvey, which treats spatial outcomes as products of capitalist power relations. The second is the "new economic geography" developed mainly by economists. Paul Krugman applied spatial reasoning to international trade theory and called his model the "new economic geography," even though geographers use the same phrase for something different. To reduce confusion, some scholars prefer "geographical economics" for the Krugman-style approach. Jeffrey Sachs has also contributed to the field. The older classical tradition, in the line of Alfred Weber, focused on industrial location using quantitative models of transport and production costs.

## A short history

Economic geography predates its name. Chinese maps of the State of Qin from the 4th century BC and Strabo's *Geographika* nearly two thousand years ago already mixed descriptions of resources, climate, and peoples with economic observation. Later European colonial maps catalogued what American, African, and Asian territories could yield, and travel journals described local productivity. These accounts helped launch transcontinental trade and the era of mercantilism, the 16th–18th century policy of maximising exports and hoarding gold.

Lindley M. Keasbey argued in 1901 that economic geography should be its own discipline because "economics is impossible without geography, and geography is incomplete without economics." World War II popularised geographical knowledge, and post-war reconstruction turned the discipline into a growth field. Ellsworth Huntington's climatic determinism, the now-discredited idea that climate directly shapes cultural and economic capacity, was influential at the time but later heavily criticised. Von Thünen's and Weber's location theories, Walter Christaller's central place theory, which explains the size and spacing of towns as nested service areas, and core-periphery models supplied core frameworks. Fred K. Schaefer's 1953 article "Exceptionalism in Geography" attacked regionalism as unscientific and pushed the field toward quantitative methods, a shift led by William Garrison, Brian Berry, Waldo Tobler, Peter Haggett, and William Bunge.

## Major approaches

| Approach | Focus |
|---|---|
| Theoretical | Models of spatial arrangement and distribution of economic activities |
| Regional | Economic conditions of specific regions or countries |
| Historical | How centres of population and activity shift and specialise over time |
| Evolutionary | Applies evolutionary economics to cities, regions, and economic systems |
| Critical | Critical-geography perspective on spatial economics |
| Behavioral | Cognitive processes behind locational choices by firms and people |

Economists and geographers differ in style: economists study how space affects the economy, while economic geographers study how economic processes reshape spatial structures, treating space, place, and scale as integral rather than abstracting them away.

## The New Economy and its geographies

The "New Economy" describes the recent shift toward globalisation, information and communications technology, knowledge goods, and the feminisation of labour. Service sectors that rely on computers and the internet have replaced manufacturing as the dominant arena. Because software and similar outputs depend on intense cooperation and tacit knowledge, know-how that is hard to write down or transfer formally, firms cluster geographically to share trust and skilled workers.

Diane Perrons splits work on this into two strands. New Economic Geography 1 (NEG1) uses spatial modelling to explain uneven development and clusters through centripetal forces that pull activity together, such as economies of scale and lower transport costs, and centrifugal forces that push it apart, such as congestion and competition for land. New Economic Geography 2 (NEG2) keeps the cluster puzzle but emphasises relational, social, and contextual factors, especially tacit knowledge. Both treat the firm as the central unit and focus on growth more than development, which Bjorn Asheim and Gernot Grabher argue undertheorises firm behaviour in NEG1 and undercontextualises it in NEG2.

Danny Quah highlights the unusual properties of knowledge goods: infinite expansibility, since one copy can serve unlimited users at near-zero marginal cost; weightlessness, since delivery costs do not depend on distance; and nonrivalry, since one person's use does not reduce availability to others. These traits deepen spatial divisions, including a digital divide between regions that attract talent and those that do not, and gendered and ethnic employment segregation in lower-paid service jobs, with limited pathways into higher-skilled work.

## How geography shapes economic history

Geography has repeatedly determined where civilisations could emerge. The Nile and Yangtze enabled ancient transport and farming; the Mississippi still moves bulk goods efficiently. Deserts, mountains, and oceans created barriers. Sahara trade depended on oases; the Himalayas isolated Tibet; passes like Khyber nonetheless carried commerce. Sea routes later connected continents and powered trades such as the Atlantic slave trade.

Climate sets the ceiling for agricultural productivity. Moderate climates such as the Mediterranean support denser farming economies through crops like olive oil and wine, while arid regions force innovation around scarce water. Early farming settlements concentrated in the Fertile Crescent, where geography permitted. Colonial powers exploited geographic opportunity to extract resources.

The pattern persists. Coastal nations gain from maritime trade; landlocked countries pay higher transport costs and tend to grow more slowly. Modern technology reduces but does not erase these geographic constraints.
