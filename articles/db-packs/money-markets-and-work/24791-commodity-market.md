# Commodity market

A commodity market trades raw, primary-sector goods rather than manufactured products. The primary sector covers three families: agricultural products, energy products, and metals. Soft commodities are perishable and harvested (wheat, corn, soybeans, cotton, sugar, cocoa, coffee, orange juice); hard commodities are usually mined (gold, silver, copper, iron ore, crude oil). To make trade possible at a distance, each commodity must be reduced to an agreed specification. U.S. soybean futures, for instance, are deliverable only if they are non-screened No. 2 yellow soybeans of specified U.S. origin; identical wording on GMO status distinguishes them from organic-grade lots. Without such standardization, no one could buy a cargo of "oil" or a tonne of "copper" without inspecting it.

The dominant instrument in modern commodity markets is the derivative, a contract whose value is derived from an underlying asset. Four forms matter, in roughly increasing complexity:

- Spot contract: immediate delivery and payment, used in physical markets such as a farmers' market.
- Forward contract: a private agreement to exchange a fixed quantity at a fixed price on a future date, used in seventeenth-century Japan to trade rice.
- Futures contract: a standardized forward, transacted through an exchange that fixes product, grade, quantity, location and delivery month, leaving only price to be negotiated. Agricultural futures have been used in the United States for more than 170 years, with modern agreements beginning in Chicago in the 1840s as grain elevators linked Midwestern farms to eastern cities.
- Options and swaps: a call option gives the buyer the right, not the obligation, to buy at a strike price by an expiry date, for a premium; a swap, introduced in the 1970s, exchanges the cash flows of two financial instruments.

Futures and options trade on commodities exchanges, the most important being the CME Group (formed by the 2007 merger of the Chicago Board of Trade and the Chicago Mercantile Exchange), the London Metal Exchange, the Intercontinental Exchange, and exchanges in Tokyo, Dalian, Mumbai, and São Paulo. The CME Group recorded 26.3 million contracts in October 2025. Over-the-counter (OTC) contracts are privately negotiated between two parties, lack the transparency of exchange trading, and historically settle through clearing houses that reduce counterparty risk.

Exchange-traded commodities (ETCs) and commodity exchange-traded funds (ETFs) opened the markets to retail investors. The first gold ETF, Gold Bullion Securities, launched on the ASX in 2003; the first silver ETF, iShares Silver Trust, on the NYSE in 2006; by November 2010, SPDR Gold Shares was the second-largest ETF by market capitalization. Some of these products hold physical metal in vaults; others implement a futures strategy that rolls front-month contracts and can diverge sharply from the spot price. ETCs were introduced partly in response to the tight supply of 2000, when record-low inventories met surging demand from emerging economies.

The price of a commodity is tracked by indices. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics began a daily Commodity Price Index in 1934 and published a Spot Market Price Index in 1952 covering 22 sensitive basic commodities. The Dow Jones Commodity Index, the first investable futures index, began in 1933; the Goldman Sachs Commodity Index (GSCI), created in 1991, was the first widely used benchmark, followed by the Reuters/CRB and Rogers indices.

Commodity markets are central to inflation. Fossil-fuel and food prices drove the 2021–2022 spike, worsened by Russia's invasion of Ukraine, and in 2025 primary-commodity prices fell 2.6% from March to August, leaving inflation near 3% in major economies. In the United States the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) regulates futures markets, with the National Futures Association (formed 1976, operational 1982) acting as self-regulator. The 2010 Dodd–Frank Act tightened position limits after the 2008 crisis; in the European Union, MiFID II, adopted by the European Parliament on 26 October 2012, imposed position limits on commodity derivatives to curb abusive speculation that the World Bank estimated had driven global food prices up 10% in July 2012.

Brent crude, assessed daily by S&P Global Commodity Insights as Dated Brent, prices more than 75% of globally traded oil cargoes; West Texas Intermediate (WTI), a lighter, sweeter grade, is the U.S. benchmark on NYMEX, trading under CL, with Brent under BRN on ICE and BZ on CME. Precious metals are quoted by the troy ounce on COMEX; industrial metals (copper, aluminium, lead, tin, nickel, cobalt, molybdenum, and steel since 2007) are quoted by the metric ton on the London Metal Exchange, where iron-ore swaps pioneered by Deutsche Bank in 2008 drove a market that more than doubled annually through 2012.
