# Economies of scale

Economies of scale are the cost advantages a firm gains as its scale of operation grows. The defining feature is that average cost per unit falls when output expands, so the long-run average cost curve slopes downward. The opposite, where average cost rises with size, is diseconomies of scale. The concept traces to Adam Smith's analysis of the division of labour in *The Wealth of Nations* (1776).

The simplest formal statement: one firm producing two quantities of a good together costs less than two separate firms producing each quantity alone.

TC((Q₁+Q₂)X) < TC(Q₁X) + TC(Q₂X)

Capacity-utilisation gains, where a single plant moves from under-use toward full output, lower average cost without requiring a larger plant. Georgescu-Roegen (1966) and Kaldor (1972) argued these are not true scale economies.

## Sources of cost savings

*Purchasing and transaction economies.* Larger orders secure bulk discounts and lower per-unit input prices; fixed costs of supply contracts are spread over more units. Nothing physical changes, which is why these are called pecuniary economies.

*Physical and engineering economies (the square–cube law).* Surface area grows with the square of a vessel's dimensions while volume grows with the cube, so capital cost per unit of capacity falls with size. Heat loss from pipes and tanks scales similarly, and drag losses on ships and aircraft rise less than proportionally with cargo volume, lowering fuel use per ton.

*Balancing of production capacity.* When production phases use indivisible, complementary equipment, a small plant may leave some machines idle. A larger scale can balance these capacities and cut idle time.

*Division of labour and superior techniques.* Larger scale permits finer specialisation of workers and managers and the adoption of techniques that only pay off at high volume. Replicable routines and managerial specialisation add further savings as the firm grows.

*Learning and growth economies.* Costs fall with cumulative output as workers and engineers improve their methods, the experience curve. A firm may also gain a one-off benefit from expanding, for instance when a supermarket chain raises the value of nearby land it already owns. Growth economies disappear once expansion is complete.

Capital cost for process equipment roughly follows the "six-tenths rule": cost scales with capacity raised to about 0.6. DuPont engineer Roger Williams Jr. published the rule in 1947, and it has since been adopted across chemicals, power generation, and mining. Crew size on ships, trains, and aircraft rises far more slowly than capacity, so labour cost per unit falls with size, which is why many aircraft have been "stretched" to carry more payload with the same cockpit crew. Marx noted that large-scale manufacturing also makes economical use of byproducts, as in the chemical, petrochemical, and pulp-and-paper industries.

## Returns to scale versus economies of scale

Returns to scale describe how output responds when all inputs scale up proportionally, a relationship captured in a production function. Economies of scale describe how average cost changes with the size of the firm. When the firm is a perfect competitor in every input market, so input prices do not change with quantity purchased, the two notions coincide: economies of scale ⇔ increasing returns, diseconomies ⇔ decreasing returns. In long-run perfect competition, firms settle at the minimum of their long-run average cost curve, the borderline between scale economies and diseconomies.

Once input prices can vary with the quantity bought, the two concepts diverge. A firm large enough to push up the price of an input can face diseconomies even with increasing returns in production, while bulk discounts can produce economies even where production has decreasing returns. Economies of scale is the more general concept because it includes these price effects.

## Internal versus external economies

Internal economies of scale lower costs for an individual firm as it grows. External economies of scale lower costs across an industry as the industry itself grows, through shared infrastructure, specialised services, and a deeper labour pool, and these tend to be more common in practice. External economies help explain why industries cluster geographically.

## Limits and diseconomies

Scale economies are bounded. Common limits include exhausting nearby raw material supply, saturating regional markets and forcing uneconomic shipping distances, reduced energy efficiency at very large scales, and higher defect rates. Large plants are efficient only at long runs of a standard grade; switching to specialty grades is costly, which is why smaller, often older, plants survive by producing specialty output.

## Real-world implications

Scale economies explain three observable patterns. First, why some industries tend toward natural monopoly, where a single large producer serves the whole market at lowest cost. Second, why free trade can be welfare-improving: a domestic market alone may be too small to support a firm at minimum efficient scale, as with a hypothetical carmaker serving only Liechtenstein, while exporting lets it spread fixed costs. Third, why large and productive firms are the ones that export: only firms with enough net revenue can cover the fixed costs of foreign markets, and trade liberalisation reallocates resources toward these more productive firms.

Marx, drawing on Babbage in *Das Kapital* (1867), argued that scale economies drive continual increases in production volume and a concentration of capital, which requires constant expansion of the market. If the market fails to expand in step, overproduction crises follow, so scale economies imply, in his analysis, two simultaneous tendencies: concentration and crisis. Piero Sraffa argued that if perfect competition is strictly maintained, scale economies are largely excluded from equilibrium analysis, a critique that shaped later theories of imperfect competition.

The "Cournot dilemma" names the tension between observed coexistence of firms of different sizes and the prediction that scale economies should produce monopoly. The resolution is that competitive advantages from capability development and transaction management can offset scale advantages, so product quality, flexibility, and customer service let heterogeneous firms coexist with scale economies.
