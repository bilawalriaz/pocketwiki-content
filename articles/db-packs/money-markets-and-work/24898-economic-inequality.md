# Economic inequality

Economic inequality describes how income (money people are paid), wealth (assets people own), and consumption (money people spend) are distributed across a population. Each can be measured between nations, within a nation, or between subgroups, and each behaves differently. The standard tool is the **Gini coefficient**, which runs from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (one person has everything); values above about 0.50 are considered high.

## Between countries versus within countries

Inequality has a dual character. Inequality *between* nations peaked around the 1970s, when global income was split into rich and poor country clusters. In 1820 the top 20% of the world earned about3 times the bottom 20%; by 1991 it was 86 times. Since then, growth in developing economies has converged average incomes, and the share of global earnings going to the poorest half roughly doubled between 1970 and the 2010s.

Inequality *within* nations has moved the opposite direction. In OECD countries it is at its highest level in half a century, and a 2020 UN study found within-country inequality rose for about 70% of the world's population between 1990 and 2015. Globalization has narrowed the gap between countries while widening the gap inside them.

## How concentrated wealth has become

The 2022 World Inequality Report found the bottom half of humanity owns about 2% of global wealth, the top 10% owns 76%, and the top 1% owns 38%. A 2026 update reported the richest 0.001% hold about three times the wealth of the poorest half. To sit in the global top 1% of after-tax income in 2023 required roughly $60,000 for a single adult, $130,000 for two adults with one child, and $160,000 for a family of four.

COVID-19 accelerated these trends. Oxfam estimated billionaires gained $3.9 trillion during the pandemic while roughly 500 million additional people fell below $5.50/day. By 2026, Oxfam reported about 3,000 billionaires holding $18.3 trillion combined.

## Why within-country inequality has risen

Several reinforcing mechanisms are cited in the research:

- **Skill-biased technology and automation.** Demand has grown for skilled workers while machines replace routine work, pushing wages up at the top and stagnating them below.
- **Decline of unions and rise of neoliberal policy.** Lower union density, deregulation, and reduced public spending compress wages and weaken redistribution.
- **Assortative mating and education access.** Top earners increasingly marry each other; the OECD found 40% of dual-earner couples fall in the same earnings decile, up from 33% two decades earlier.
- **Globalization.** Trade with poorer countries lowers wages for low-skilled workers in rich countries while raising them in poor countries.
- **Wealth concentration dynamics.** Thomas Piketty argues that when the return on capital (r) exceeds the rate of economic growth (g), existing fortunes compound faster than new wages, a gap that worsens when growth slows.

Historical evidence on reversal is sobering. Since 1300, the only episodes that significantly reduced wealth inequality in Europe were the Black Death, the two World Wars, and Communist revolutions.

## Demographic fault lines

Inequality runs along predictable demographic lines. A study of Armenia, Georgia, and Azerbaijan found gender pay gaps exceeding 50% after controlling for other factors. Racial gaps are stark and intergenerational. In the United States, Black Americans owned roughly 0.5% of national wealth in 1863 and about 1.5% in 2019, a persistence linked to policies such as redlining that excluded Black families from homeownership. Similar colonial legacies leave indigenous and non-white populations poorer in Latin America, Africa, and parts of Asia. Income also tends to rise with age.

## Why it matters

Empirical work links higher inequality to worse health outcomes, lower social trust, higher homicide rates, reduced civic participation, greater political polarization, and civil conflict. A 2016 meta-analysis found inequality's drag on growth is stronger in poorer countries, and wealth inequality harms growth more than income inequality does. Research also links inequality to climate breakdown: the wealthiest 10% produced over half of CO2 emissions from 1990 to 2015, and the top 1% emit more than 1,000 times the bottom 1%. More than 200 economists warned the UN in 2023 that unreduced inequality "will entrench poverty and increase the risk of climate breakdown."

## Mitigating factors and policy levers

Social connectedness to higher-income peers is a strong predictor of upward mobility for poor children. Government tools include progressive taxation, the earned income tax credit, public education, wage-ratio legislation, and minimum wages. Saez and Piketty recommend top marginal rates as high as 50–90%; the OECD argues public spending is vital to closing wealth gaps. A 2025 Pew survey found majorities across 36 countries view economic inequality as a major problem. United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 10 targets significant reduction in inequality by 2030.
