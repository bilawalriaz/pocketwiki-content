# DNA bank

A DNA bank is the secure, long-term storage of an individual's genetic material. DNA is most often extracted from blood, but saliva, hair, skin, sperm, and other tissues also work when the sample contains intact DNA. Banks allow genetic material to be conserved, compared, and analyzed later. Stored material has three main uses: preserving the genetic heritage of endangered or agriculturally important organisms, predicting and treating disease in humans, and identifying people in the criminal justice system.

## Conservation and agriculture

DNA banking supports biodiversity threatened by deforestation and climate change. Genetic material from rare or endangered species can be stored in lambda phage or plasmid vectors, keeping it available for future breeding programs or research. The National Institute of Agrobiological Sciences (NIAS) DNA Bank, for example, collects DNA from agricultural organisms such as rice and fish, and most of its samples support work on more productive or more environmentally resilient species.

## Human health and identity

Human DNA can be banked at any point in a person's life, and the same sample can later be compared with other DNA, screened for disease-linked genes, or matched against crime-scene material.

Comparing banked DNA made the Human Genome Project possible by mapping the genes on human DNA. Continued access to banked samples also supports preventive genetics: once a gene pattern is linked to a disorder, carriers can adjust their lifestyle or, in some cases, receive gene therapy to lessen its effects.

In forensics, DNA banking entered the criminal justice system in the 1980s. A suspect's genetic code can rule them in or out of a case, and once stored, a profile remains in the system permanently, making repeat identification straightforward. This permanence is the main criticism: some argue that indefinite storage of citizens' DNA is an invasion of privacy.

## How DNA is read

Two techniques dominate. Restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP), introduced in 1988, uses restriction enzymes to cut DNA into short fragments, which are then sorted by length through gel electrophoresis. The pattern of band lengths lets specialists tell whether two samples came from the same person. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is more common today because it is more efficient and works with much smaller samples. Both methods rely on the fact that any two human nucleotide sequences differ by only about 0.1 percent, which still leaves roughly three million bases of variation to compare.

## Examples of large banks

The UK Biobank holds DNA from 500,000 individuals aged 40 to 69 when their samples were taken between 2006 and 2010. The Human DNA Bank in Lucknow, India, founded by Dr. Saeed Ahmad under IQRA Biotech Services in a public–private partnership with Biotech Park, stores members' DNA for 50 years alongside biometrics and issues a UID DNA card. The same organization runs a cord blood and stem cell bank whose stored cells could later treat spinal cord injuries, cerebral palsy, leukemia, Parkinson's disease, anemia, and cancer. Some funeral homes now offer DNA banking capsules so the genetic material of the deceased can be preserved.
