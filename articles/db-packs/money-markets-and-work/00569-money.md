# Money

Money is any item or record a community accepts as payment for goods, services, and debts, including taxes. It is a social technology: it works only because people collectively believe others will accept it tomorrow. A modern economy cannot run on barter, where each party must want what the other offers, because that "coincidence of wants" is rare. Money solves the problem by serving as a universally tradable token.

## Functions and properties

William Stanley Jevons (1875) defined four roles. **Medium of exchange**: accepted in trade. **Unit of account**: prices are quoted in it, giving a common yardstick for value. **Store of value**: it can be held and retrieved without losing worth. **Standard of deferred payment**: debts are denominated in it, so a loan made in year one can be repaid in year ten. The medium and store roles sit in tension: a good medium of exchange must circulate, while a good store of value must be held. Modern texts often fold the deferred-payment role into the other three.

To perform these functions reliably, money must be **fungible** (every unit interchangeable), **durable**, **divisible**, **portable**, **acceptable**, and **scarce** (limited supply preserves value).

## A brief history

Anthropological evidence shows no large society has run mainly on barter; gift economies and informal credit dominated instead. The first formal money was **commodity money**, whose value came from the material itself: barley measured in *shekels* in Mesopotamia around 3000 BC, cowry shells across the Americas, Asia, Africa, and Australia, and stamped gold and silver coins in Lydia around 650–600 BC.

As trade grew, merchants holding deposited gold issued paper receipts that circulated as money. This **representative money** was redeemable for a fixed quantity of commodity. China pioneered state-issued paper during the Tang (618–907) and Song (960–1279) dynasties; the *jiaozi* banknote became uniform national currency by the mid-13th century, enabled by woodblock and movable-type printing. Europe followed with Stockholms Banco's banknotes in 1661.

The **gold standard** replaced circulating gold coins with notes convertible into fixed gold amounts during the 17th–19th centuries; by 1900 most industrial nations had adopted it. The 1944 Bretton Woods agreement fixed other currencies to the U.S. dollar, itself fixed to gold. In 1971 the U.S. suspended dollar-to-gold convertibility, and most currencies became **fiat money**: no intrinsic or commodity backing, value resting on government decree (legal-tender law) and, per Modern Monetary Theory, on the state's power to tax.

## How money is created

Most modern money is not cash. In developed economies, the bulk of the money supply exists as bank deposits created by commercial banks when they issue loans. Under **fractional-reserve banking**, banks hold only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend the rest, so a new loan appears as a new deposit, money that did not exist before. Central banks influence but do not directly control this creation; they steer it through policy interest rates, reserve requirements, and open market operations.

The money supply is measured in tiers by liquidity:

| Aggregate | Includes |
| :--- | :--- |
| M0 (base) | Central bank currency plus bank reserves at the central bank |
| M1 (narrow) | Currency plus demand deposits |
| M2 (broad) | M1 plus savings accounts and small time deposits (under $100,000) |
| M3 | M2 plus large time deposits and institutional money-market funds |

M0 is what the central bank issues; M1 and M2 are mostly bank-created deposits. **Market liquidity** describes how easily an asset trades; money is the most liquid asset.

## Types of money

- **Commodity money**: the material is the value (gold, barley, shells, cigarettes in POW camps).
- **Representative money**: tokens redeemable for a fixed quantity of commodity, such as gold-standard notes.
- **Fiat money**: no intrinsic value; legal tender by government order, replaceable if damaged.
- **Commercial bank money**: demand deposits created by lending, carrying insolvency risk.
- **Digital money**: by 1990 all U.S. interbank transfers were electronic; most money is now database entries. **Bitcoin (2008)** introduced decentralised, trustless digital currency using distributed consensus.

## Locality, policy, and crime

Money is valid only within a specific socio-economic context. Governments enforce a single currency through tax requirements and fraud laws. When a currency collapses, communities switch, as Brazil did from the cruzeiro to the real, or hoard foreign cash and commodities. **Gresham's Law** names the related pattern: debased or overvalued money circulates while scarce, high-quality money is hoarded.

Central banks (the Fed, ECB, BoJ, PBOC, BoE) conduct **monetary policy** targeting low stable inflation, maximum employment, and moderate interest rates. In the 1970s–80s, monetarism tried to target money-supply growth directly but failed because money demand proved unstable. Since the 1990s, most central banks target inflation using interest rates. Policy failure can produce hyperinflation, stagflation, or monetary collapse, as in post-Soviet Russia.

Two persistent crimes distort the system. **Counterfeiting** is unauthorised imitation of currency, historically deterred by hard-to-copy materials. **Money laundering** transforms criminal proceeds into assets that appear legitimate, often using securities, digital currencies, or cash.
