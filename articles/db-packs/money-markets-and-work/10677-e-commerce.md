# E-commerce

E-commerce is the buying and selling of goods or services over the internet, the same commerce that happens in physical stores, but transacted through websites, apps, and the data networks that connect buyers to sellers. The term was coined by Robert Jacobson and written into law in California's Electronic Commerce Act of 1984, an early formal recognition of the activity. E-commerce is a subset of retail, and it is the largest segment of the electronics industry, pushed forward by the same semiconductor advances that make consumer devices faster and cheaper.

Any transaction has three steps: a buyer decides what they want, money moves from buyer to seller, and the product or service reaches the buyer. E-commerce digitizes all three. Catalogues become web pages, cash becomes electronic funds transfer and online payment processing, and physical delivery becomes a logistics problem solved by warehouses, shippers, and increasingly by *drop shipping*, where the seller never holds inventory but forwards the order to a manufacturer or wholesaler that ships directly. Supporting technologies include mobile commerce, supply chain management software, inventory management systems, electronic data interchange (EDI, a standard format for business documents), and automated data collection.

## Who sells to whom

E-commerce is organized by the relationship between the parties:

- **B2B** (business-to-business): one company sells to another, such as a parts supplier selling to a manufacturer.
- **B2C** (business-to-consumer): a company sells to an individual shopper, like Amazon selling a book.
- **C2C** (consumer-to-consumer): individuals sell to each other, as on eBay.
- **C2B** (consumer-to-business): individuals offer goods or services to companies.
- **D2C** (direct-to-consumer): a brand sells straight to the end customer, bypassing retailers, common on Shopify, TikTok Shop, and Instagram Checkout.
- **B2G** (business-to-government): companies sell to government agencies.

A second axis is what is sold: digital content like ebooks and streaming media, delivered instantly over the network, and physical goods, which require the full logistics chain.

## How it differs from physical retail

A physical store is limited by shelf space, so it stocks fewer items and prices them to cover rent and staff. An online store faces no shelf constraint, can offer a nearly unlimited catalogue, and often prices based on delivery speed rather than store traffic. Under drop shipping, online retailers hold no inventory at all, since the manufacturer ships directly to the customer.

The trade-off is experience and trust. Customers cannot touch a product before buying, which creates uncertainty about quality and fit, and returns require the buyer to package and ship the item back, which is harder for large, fragile, or expensive goods. Security of online transactions remains a real concern, which is why many shoppers stay loyal to well-known retailers. Tools that close the gap include virtual fit platforms for clothing, augmented reality previews of furniture in a home, and user-generated reviews from sites like Trustpilot and Yelp.

## Scale and geography

E-commerce crossed $1 trillion in global sales in 2012. By 2017, worldwide retail e-commerce reached $2.3 trillion, while total e-commerce including business-to-business sales reached $29.267 trillion, of which $25.516 trillion was B2B and $3.851 trillion was B2C. China has been the largest single retail e-commerce market, reaching $899 billion in 2016, about 42.4% of the world total. Alibaba dominated the Chinese market with 80% share in 2013, and its 2014 NYSE debut under the ticker BABA was, at the time, the largest IPO in U.S. history. In 2015 Amazon accounted for more than half of all e-commerce growth in the United States.

Two forces have reshaped the geography of the field. Mobile commerce expanded the buyer base to populations without traditional computers, and analysts projected that 25% of e-commerce purchases would happen on mobile devices by 2017. Cross-border trade let small businesses reach foreign customers without opening overseas offices; cross-border e-commerce between China and other countries grew 32% to 2.3 trillion yuan ($375.8 billion) in 2012, and in China this expansion produced Taobao villages, rural clusters of e-commerce businesses that became part of national rural revitalization strategy.

## Regulation

Because e-commerce crosses borders easily but laws do not, regulation is a patchwork. In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission enforces truthfulness in online advertising under the Federal Trade Commission Act and oversees the CAN-SPAM Act for marketing email; the Ryan Haight Online Pharmacy Consumer Protection Act of 2008 regulates online pharmacies; and California added the California Privacy Rights Act in 2020. For international harmonization, the United Nations adopted the UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Commerce in 1996. The European Commission completed a major enquiry into e-commerce in 2015–16 that flagged selective distribution systems, where manufacturers restrict which retailers can sell their products online, as a practice that could limit consumer choice. The United Kingdom oversees payment services under regulations derived from the EU's Payment Services Directive, implemented through the Payment Services Regulations 2009. India governs e-commerce under the Information Technology Act 2000, and China built its framework beginning with the September 2000 Telecommunications Regulations and the 2004 Electronic Signature Law, its first dedicated e-commerce statute.

## Broader effects

E-commerce reshapes supply chains by integrating the three flows of commerce: physical goods, money, and information. Software like SAP ERP, Xero, and Megaventory lets firms track all three in real time, closing gaps that older systems could not. Employment effects are mixed: the sector creates jobs for skilled workers who manage data, logistics, and customer service, while reducing demand for retail clerks, postal workers, and travel agents. Environmentally, individual shipping carries a packaging cost. North America generated 1.3 million short tons of container cardboard for e-commerce in 2018, and Amazon, the largest user, has cut its packaging weight by 19% since 2016 and runs an 85-person team devoted to reducing it. Cross-border shipping has also been linked to the spread of invasive species, since seeds and pests occasionally travel inside packages.

The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated these trends. Global retail website traffic hit 14.3 billion visits in March 2020, online sales rose 25% in the United States, online grocery shopping more than doubled, and surveys found 29% of U.S. and 43% of UK consumers expected to keep their new online habits after lockdowns ended.
