# Stock market

A stock market is the collection of buyers and sellers trading shares, which are ownership claims on businesses. Some shares trade on formal exchanges; others trade privately through equity crowdfunding platforms. Worldwide market capitalization grew from US$2.5 trillion in 1980 to US$111 trillion by the end of 2023. As of January 2022 the United States held about 59.9% of global value, Japan about 6.2%, and the United Kingdom about 3.9%.

Modern exchanges trace back to 13th-century Bruges, where commodity traders met at an inn owned by the Van der Beurze family. The Italian, French, German, and other European words for a stock exchange (borsa, bourse, börse) all derive from that name.

## How exchanges work

A stock exchange is a marketplace, physical or electronic, where stockbrokers and traders buy and sell shares. Most exchanges also act as guarantors of settlement, collecting and delivering shares and ensuring payment, which eliminates the risk that one party defaults.

A buyer bids a specific price for a share; a seller asks a specific price. The difference is the bid-ask spread. When the two match, a sale occurs on a first-come, first-served basis. A market order accepts whatever bid or ask is currently available.

The New York Stock Exchange operates as a hybrid: orders arrive electronically from anywhere, then route through floor brokers to a designated market maker (DMM) assigned to each stock. The DMM posts both buy and sell orders to keep trading continuous and may use its own capital to bridge the spread when no other counterparty is available. NASDAQ is fully electronic but works similarly, with market makers always quoting bid and ask prices. The Paris Bourse, now part of Euronext, shifted from open-outcry trading to a fully automated order-matching system in 1986.

## Who participates

Participants range from individual retail investors to institutions such as banks, insurance companies, pension funds, mutual funds, index funds, exchange-traded funds, and hedge funds. In 2021 the value of world stock markets rose 26.5% to US$22.3 trillion, with Asia and Oceania accounting for 45%, Europe 37%, the Americas 16%, and Africa 2%.

## Why the market matters

Stock markets let companies raise capital by selling ownership shares to the public. The resulting liquidity, meaning the ability to convert shares to cash quickly, distinguishes stocks from less liquid assets like real estate. Market performance is a widely watched indicator of a country's economic strength, and central banks monitor it as part of maintaining financial stability. When markets function smoothly, capital moves from savers to borrowers, supporting investment, production, and employment.

## How prices move

In the efficient-market hypothesis (EMH), prices reflect all available information, so only new information should move them. EMH struggles to explain large unexplained swings, such as Black Monday on October 19, 1987, when the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 22.6% in a single day with no agreed-upon cause. Research on fifty of the largest one-day U.S. moves since World War II found many occurred without identifiable news.

Behavioral research offers a complement: people see patterns in noise, and good news can trigger overreaction that drives prices beyond fundamentals. A "soft" version of EMH holds that while individual mistakes cancel out, traders cannot systematically exploit temporary mispricings. Studies also find that stock-price distributions are non-Gaussian, with fatter tails and more extreme moves than a normal distribution predicts. In calm times the market behaves like a game of known probabilities; in stressed times, herding and other investors' psychology dominate decisions.

## Crashes and circuit breakers

A crash is a sharp drop in share prices, often ending a speculative bubble and shaking public confidence. The Wall Street Crash of 1929 lost 50% of the Dow Jones and helped trigger the Great Depression. Black Monday of 1987 spread from Hong Kong around the world, with Hong Kong down 45.5% and the United States down 22.68% by month's end. The S&P 500 fell 57% from October 2007 to March 2009 during the financial crisis, recovering to its 2007 level only by April 2013. A sudden COVID-19-driven crash ran from February 20 to April 7, 2020.

To limit panic selling, exchanges adopted circuit breakers that halt trading when the S&P 500 drops 7% (a 15-minute pause if before 3:25 p.m.), 13%, or 20% (a full-day stop).

## Who actually owns stocks

Participation in the United States is sharply unequal by income. In 2025 the wealthiest 1% owned nearly 50% of the stock market, the top 10% owned 87.2%, and the bottom half owned 1.1%. In the bottom income quintile, 5.5% of households own stock directly and 10.7% indirectly through retirement accounts; in the top decile, those figures are 47.5% and 89.6%.

Direct ownership barely changed from 1992 to 2007, rising from 17.8% to 17.9%, while indirect ownership through retirement accounts jumped from 39.3% to 52.6%. The gap reflects taxation: pension and 401(k) (a U.S. employer-sponsored retirement account) contributions are taxed only at withdrawal, whereas direct purchases and dividends are taxed immediately. Research by Annette Vissing-Jørgensen found that a fixed cost of about $200 per year is enough to explain why nearly half of U.S. households do not participate at all.

## Short selling, margin, and derivatives

Short selling lets a trader profit from a price decline by borrowing shares, selling them, and buying them back later at a lower price. Margin buying borrows money to amplify a purchase, up to 50% of the purchase value in the U.S., with a margin call triggered if the account's value can no longer cover the loss. Derivatives such as stock options, equity swaps, and stock-index futures derive their value from underlying stock prices and can control large positions with relatively little capital, but they also concentrate risk.

## Returns and the long run

The S&P 500 has averaged roughly 10% annual growth over the long term, though any single year can deviate sharply: from 2012 to 2021 the average was 14.8%. Inflation typically erodes 2% to 3% of that each year, so the real return is somewhat lower. Robert Shiller's data on long-term S&P returns shows that investors who entered when price-earnings ratios were low did substantially better over the following twenty years than those who entered when ratios were high.
