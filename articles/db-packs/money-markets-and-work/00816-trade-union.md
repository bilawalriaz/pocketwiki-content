# Trade union

A trade union (British English) or labor union (American English) is a continuous association of wage earners who band together to bargain collectively with employers over wages, benefits, hours, safety, job security, and grievance procedures. The 1894 definition by Sidney and Beatrice Webb captures this: "a continuous association of wage earners for the purpose of maintaining or improving the conditions of their employment." Members pay dues that fund the organisation, elect leaders democratically, and ratify contracts through votes. The leverage comes from threatening to withhold labour: a single worker is replaceable; a coordinated group is not.

## Why unions emerged

Modern unions predate Marx's *Communist Manifesto* (1848) by nearly a century. The 18th-century Industrial Revolution in Britain pulled displaced peasants and immigrants into cities, where they sold their labour for wages instead of working their own land. That loss of control over the pace and product of work created the conditions for collective organisation. Marx highlighted one early function: preventing wages from falling below traditional living standards.

Early unions were treated as illegal conspiracies. Britain's Combination Acts of 1799 and 1825 banned collective bargaining, and the 1820 Rising in Scotland saw 60,000 workers strike before being crushed. In the United States, unions were prosecuted under antitrust laws such as the Sherman Antitrust Act. Legal acceptance came in stages: Britain legalised unions in 1872 after a Royal Commission found them beneficial to both sides; Germany's unions formed in 1897 after Bismarck's Anti-Socialist Laws were repealed; French unions emerged after the 1884 Waldeck-Rousseau laws. Earlier precedents include Roman collegia, legalised under the Lex Julia in 44 BC, and medieval guilds, though guild masters employed unorganised apprentices and journeymen, so the link to modern unions is disputed.

## How unions are organised

Three main models structure membership. **Craft unionism** organises workers by specific skilled trade (electricians, plumbers). **General unionism** accepts members across trades. **Industrial unionism** organises all workers in an industry, regardless of skill. Most national unions are federated into local chapters, then national bodies, then international confederations like the ITUC. Japan uses enterprise unions bound to a single company; Western Europe often has professional associations performing union functions for white-collar staff. Internally, leaders are democratically elected, though research shows they tend to be more politically committed than the average member.

Two operating philosophies coexist. The **service model** maintains members' rights and resolves disputes one case at a time. The **organising model** runs confrontational campaigns to recruit broadly and exercise collective power. In practice, unions use elements of both.

## Legal frameworks and bargaining structures

Three regulatory traditions dominate. **Continental European** systems rely on strong labour law. **Anglo-Saxon** systems (US, UK) leave more to direct employer negotiation with weaker legal backing. **Nordic** systems have limited legislation but very high coverage through nationwide collective agreements. In Germany and the Netherlands, workers sit on corporate supervisory boards under **co-determination**, sharing management decisions. In Austria, Denmark, Germany, and Sweden, **sectoral bargaining** sets wages industry-wide; in the US, unions bargain company by company.

Different countries also define who must join a union in a unionised workplace. A **closed shop** requires union membership before hiring; a **union shop** requires it after; an **agency shop** requires non-members to pay a fee; an **open shop** requires nothing. The US Taft–Hartley Act (1947) outlawed the closed shop, and 1980s UK laws under Margaret Thatcher restricted closed and union shops; all agreements that compel a worker to join are now illegal there. The US also has state-level right-to-work laws banning such agreements.

## Prevalence and decline

Union density measures the share of workers who belong to a union. Across OECD countries, density fell from 35.9% in 1998 to 27.9% in 2018. Three forces drove this: deindustrialisation (manufacturing, once a union stronghold, shrank), globalisation (employers could threaten to move production across borders), and policy shifts. Right-wing governments in the UK and US restricted union formation, while social policies such as minimum wage and parental leave reduced the perceived need to join. Density remains highest in Nordic countries; developing economies may see growth as manufacturing relocates there.

## Political and economic effects

Evidence links unions to lower inequality. The decline of U.S. unionisation since the 1960s tracks the rise of income inequality and middle-class income loss. Higher density correlates with fewer occupational fatalities, lower suicide and overdose death rates, more responsive legislators on poor constituents' concerns, and greater adoption of parental leave. Membership also raises political knowledge, especially among less-educated workers.

Critics counter that unions can benefit insiders at the expense of outsiders (non-union workers, consumers, shareholders), and some studies find negative effects on profitability and employment growth that vary by sector. Whether unions primarily benefit members or harm broader economic efficiency remains contested: some research shows productivity gains and reduced inequality, while other studies find negative employment effects.
