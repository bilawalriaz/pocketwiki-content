# Hydrogen economy

A hydrogen economy treats hydrogen as an energy carrier that complements electricity in sectors where direct electrification is difficult or impossible. Nearly all hydrogen produced today is "gray hydrogen" made by reacting steam with natural gas (steam methane reforming). In 2021 that process emitted roughly 915 MtCO₂, about 1.8% of global greenhouse gas emissions. Replacing gray hydrogen with low-carbon hydrogen, either "blue" (steam reforming paired with carbon capture) or "green" (electrolysis of water using renewable electricity), is the central aim of the concept.

## Current production and use

Global hydrogen output reached about 100 million tonnes in 2021. Around 70% came from natural gas reforming and 30% from coal gasification, almost entirely in China; less than 1% was low-carbon. Gray hydrogen emits 6.6–9.3 tonnes of CO₂ per tonne of H₂ produced. Electrolyser costs fell about 60% from 2010 to 2022, then rose 50% between 2021 and 2024, showing the technology's volatility.

Almost all hydrogen feeds existing chemical demand: oil refining (40 Mt, mainly for hydrocracking, breaking heavy oils into lighter fuels using hydrogen) and industrial uses (54 Mt) split between ammonia for fertilizer (34 Mt), methanol (15 Mt), and direct reduced iron (5 Mt, an iron-making route that can substitute coal-derived coke with hydrogen). There are no real substitutes for hydrogen in these processes.

## Where hydrogen matters for climate

Because green hydrogen will remain scarce and expensive for years, climate benefit is maximised by directing it to hard-to-decarbonise uses: heavy industry, including high-temperature heat, feedstock for green ammonia and organic chemicals, and replacement of coal-derived coke in steelmaking; long-haul transport such as shipping (via hydrogen-derived ammonia and methanol), aviation, and to a lesser extent heavy trucks; and long-term energy storage, absorbing surplus renewable electricity that would otherwise be curtailed and releasing it later.

Light-duty vehicles and building heating have largely fallen out of the future hydrogen economy. Batteries and heat pumps beat hydrogen on cost and efficiency in those sectors, and using hydrogen for home heating would require two to three times as much electricity as a heat pump while diverting scarce clean hydrogen from industrial uses.

## Costs and outlook

Levelized cost of hydrogen (LCOH, the all-in per-kg price including capital and energy) estimates vary widely. In 2021–2022, gray hydrogen cost roughly $1–8/kg, blue hydrogen $1.5–8.6/kg, and green hydrogen $2.8–9.5/kg, depending on region, energy price, and electrolyser type. Green hydrogen is cheapest when powered by curtailed renewables. Goldman Sachs projected cost parity with gray hydrogen by 2030, sooner under a carbon tax. The U.S. Department of Energy's Hydrogen Hotshot Initiative targets $1/kg green hydrogen by 2031.

## Practical challenges

Hydrogen has a very low boiling point (−253 °C), making liquefaction energy-intensive. As the smallest molecule it leaks readily from containers and embrittles steel pipelines and storage vessels. It ignites at concentrations as low as 4% in air, its flame is nearly invisible, and roughly 70% of ignition incidents have no identifiable source. These properties demand dedicated infrastructure.

## History and policy

The hydrogen economy concept was proposed in 1923 by geneticist J. B. S. Haldane, who envisioned wind-powered electrolysis for long-term storage. John Bockris coined the term during a 1970 talk at General Motors. A 1970 University of Michigan report by Lawrence W. Jones framed it as a response to fossil fuel depletion and pollution. After a 2000s boom widely called a hype cycle, in which investors lost money, interest resurged in the 2010s with the 2017 formation of the Hydrogen Council. National strategies multiplied from 28 in 2021 to 60 by 2024, though most pursue "scale first, clean later," adding regulations to support green hydrogen without mandating it, and few appear on track to meet their 2030 targets. By the end of 2022, only 70,200 hydrogen fuel-cell vehicles had been sold worldwide, compared with 26 million plug-in electric vehicles.

The IEA's 2022 Net Zero Emissions Scenario projects hydrogen meeting about 30% of heavy-truck energy demand and 2% of rail demand by 2050, with 90% of rail travel electrified, leaving hydrogen as a supplement to electricity in transport and industry.
