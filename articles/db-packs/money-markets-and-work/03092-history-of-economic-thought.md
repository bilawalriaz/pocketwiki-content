# History of economic thought

Economic thought began as a branch of moral philosophy about how households, communities, and states should manage resources, then matured into a mathematical discipline in the twentieth century. Across roughly 2,400 years the central question has stayed stable: what creates wealth, what governs its exchange, and how much should governments intervene? The answers have swung between poles, each new school defining itself against the last.

## Ancient and medieval foundations

Xenophon's *Oeconomicus* (c. 360 BC) treats economics as household management, the original meaning of the Greek *oikonomia*. Aristotle's *Politics* (c. 350 BC) extended this to the ethics of acquisition, distinguishing between provisioning a household and the unlimited "art of acquisition," which he treated with suspicion. The medieval School of Salamanca, and Thomas Aquinas in his *Summa Theologica* (1273), recast Greek ideas inside Christian morality, arguing that exchange requires a "just price" that covers costs and reflects communal need. Ibn Khaldun offered an early theory of civilisation cycles and the role of money in state expansion.

## Mercantilism and its critics (16th–18th centuries)

From the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries, European states pursued mercantilism: protect domestic industry, export more than you import, and accumulate gold and silver bullion. Nicolaus Copernicus's monetary writings (1517) included one of the first arguments for the quantity theory of money, the idea that prices rise with the money supply. The French Physiocrats, led by François Quesnay, argued that only agricultural surplus creates real wealth and that markets should be left to clear (*laissez-faire*). John Locke and David Hume added property-rights theory, a labour-based account of value, and the self-regulating market, the conceptual scaffolding Adam Smith would assemble.

## Classical economics (late 18th–19th century)

Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776) is the founding text of classical political economy. Its core claim is a "system of natural liberty" in which individuals pursuing self-interest are guided, as if by an "invisible hand," to efficient outcomes through competition and the division of labour. David Ricardo's *Principles of Political Economy and Taxation* (1817) refined value theory, developed the theory of rent, and formulated comparative advantage, the principle that two countries gain from trade even when one is more efficient at everything. The classical school, including John Stuart Mill, broadly favoured free markets while debating population, distribution, and the long-run tendency of profits to fall.

Karl Marx's *Das Kapital* (1867) is classical economics turned against itself. Accepting a labour theory of value, Marx argued that workers receive only a fraction of the value they create, that capitalism contains internal contradictions, and that recurrent crises would culminate in revolution. Marx's significance lies less in his predictions than in establishing a tradition that analyses capitalism as a dynamic, crisis-prone system.

## The marginalist revolution (1870s)

In 1871 a parallel shift occurred almost simultaneously in three countries. William Stanley Jevons in England, Carl Menger in Austria, and Léon Walras in Switzerland each argued that value is not determined by the labour needed to produce a good but by *marginal utility*, the additional satisfaction a consumer gains from one more unit. This insight dissolved old paradoxes, such as why diamonds cost more than water, and founded neoclassical economics. Alfred Marshall synthesised the marginalist approach around supply-and-demand diagrams and popularised the word "economics." Walras and his Lausanne School developed general equilibrium theory, a model of how prices in all markets clear at once, while Menger's Austrian followers stressed deductive reasoning and subjective value.

## Keynes and the twentieth century

The Great Depression exposed a hole in classical theory: no self-correcting mechanism restored full employment. In *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936), John Maynard Keynes argued that output and employment are driven by aggregate demand, the total spending in an economy, and that wages and prices do not fall fast enough to clear labour markets in a downturn. The remedy is fiscal policy: government spending and taxation to lift demand when the private sector is weak. Keynesian ideas dominated postwar policy in many Western states, often combined with neoclassical microeconomics in the "neoclassical synthesis."

From the 1950s the Chicago School, led by Milton Friedman's *Capitalism and Freedom* (1962), pushed back. Monetarism holds that the money supply is the chief driver of inflation and growth, so central banks should target it rather than have fiscal authorities fine-tune demand. The 1970s saw New Classical Macroeconomics add rational expectations, the idea that people anticipate policy using all available information, which limits how much governments can systematically surprise the economy. New Keynesians responded by giving Keynesian results microeconomic foundations: sticky prices, imperfect competition, and information asymmetries (situations where one party in a transaction knows more than the other).

Paul Samuelson's *Foundations of Economic Analysis* (1947) cemented mathematics, especially calculus and optimisation, as the discipline's working language. Game theory, information economics (George Akerlof, Joseph Stiglitz), and behavioural economics widened the toolkit in the late twentieth century. Douglass North and the new institutional economics stressed that rules, property rights, and organisations shape performance as much as markets do. Amartya Sen redirected development economics away from GDP and towards capabilities, poverty, and inequality.

## The 2008 crisis and after

The 2008 global financial crisis revived Keynesian policy debates. With interest rates near zero, governments questioned whether austerity was the right response and whether debt-financed stimulus was needed. Climate change, rising inequality, and the geopolitics of supply chains pushed questions of measurement, sustainability, and global trade to the centre of the field.

## Persistent debates

Four unresolved questions structure modern economics. First, the role of the state: how far should governments intervene to correct market failures, redistribute income, or stabilise the cycle? Second, the theory of value: labour, marginal utility, scarcity, and social estimation each anchor a different school. Third, the causes of crises: internal dynamics such as debt cycles and asset bubbles, external shocks, or policy error. Fourth, what to measure: GDP per capita, broader human development as Sen proposed, or environmental sustainability, with each metric implying different policy priorities.

What divides the tradition is how much weight to give markets, mathematics, and moral purpose when analysing wealth, exchange, and policy.
