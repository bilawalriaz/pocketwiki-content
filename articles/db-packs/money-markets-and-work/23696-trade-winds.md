# Trade winds

The trade winds are Earth's east-to-west prevailing winds, blowing toward the equator in the tropics. In the Northern Hemisphere they come from the northeast; in the Southern Hemisphere from the southeast. The name "trade" originally meant "path" or "track" in early 14th-century English and shifted to its commercial sense only after centuries of ships relying on these winds for regular crossings.

Trade winds are the surface branch of the Hadley cell. Warm air rises near the equator, cools, loses moisture as precipitation, and descends around 30° latitude in both hemispheres into subtropical high-pressure belts called subtropical ridges. Once it reaches the surface, that dry air flows back toward the equator, where the Coriolis effect deflects it westward, producing the northeast and southeast trades. The equator itself sits in a low-pressure belt of calms called the doldrums, also known as the Intertropical Convergence Zone, where the two trade wind systems meet and air rises again.

Because the descending air aloft is warm and dry while the surface air is cool and moist, a temperature inversion forms within the trade wind layer. This trade wind inversion caps shallow cumulus clouds at roughly 4 km, preventing deep convection. Rainfall over nearby land depends on wind strength: weaker trades let moist equatorial air linger and produce more rain over landmasses like Central America, while stronger trades suppress it. Trade winds are stronger in winter and during the warm phase of the Arctic oscillation.

Trade winds steer tropical storms across the Atlantic, Pacific, and southern Indian Oceans and shape regional rainfall in East Africa, Madagascar, North America, and Southeast Asia. All tropical oceans except the northern Indian Ocean have extensive trade wind belts.

The winds also transport millions of tons of nutrient-rich Saharan dust each year westward across the Atlantic, carrying phosphorus and other fertilizers that replenish depleted Amazon soils. The same dust reaches the Caribbean, southeastern North America, and Florida, where it suppresses rainfall, turns skies hazy white, intensifies red sunsets, raises airborne particulate counts, and has been linked to declining coral reef health since the 1970s.

For centuries, sailors exploited these patterns. Portuguese navigators mapped Atlantic routes in the 15th century, sailing west and northwest from West Africa to the Azores before turning east to Europe, and going far out to sea, west toward Brazil, and east again around 30°S to reach South Africa, because hugging the African coast southbound means sailing upwind. The full Pacific circulation, combining easterly trades with higher-latitude westerlies, was only worked out after Andrés de Urdaneta's 1565 voyage. Manila galleons could not sail into the wind, so prevailing wind patterns dictated which parts of the globe were easy to reach and directly shaped European empire-building. Matthew Fontaine Maury's 1847–1849 wind and current charts later formalized the knowledge for global shipping.
