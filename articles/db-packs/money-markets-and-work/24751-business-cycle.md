# Business cycle

A business cycle is the recurring pattern of expansion and recession in aggregate economic activity, where most sectors rise and fall together over periods of one to ten years. Schumpeter divided each cycle into four phases: expansion (rising output, low interest rates), crisis (stock crashes, bankruptcies), recession (falling output and prices, high rates), and recovery. The simplest definition calls any two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth a recession. The US National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) defines it more carefully as a significant decline in real GDP, real income, employment, industrial production, and wholesale-retail sales lasting more than a few months.

Cycles differ from long-term growth driven by technology and from short noise like strikes or weather. Typical periodicity ranges from 2 to 10 years. Economists have catalogued cycles at several frequencies: the Kitchin inventory cycle (3–5 years), the Juglar fixed-investment cycle (7–11 years, identified by Clément Juglar in 1860), the Kuznets infrastructural swing (15–25 years), and the Kondratiev technological wave (45–60 years).

What drives cycles remains contested. Mainstream economics treats them as the random summation of random shocks, an idea Eugen Slutzky illustrated in 1927 by summing lottery digits to produce cycle-like patterns. Because recessions are essentially stochastic, expansions can last decades (Australia had none from 1991 to 2020), and longer expansions do not produce more severe subsequent recessions. The most reliable leading indicator is an inverted yield curve, meaning short-term bond yields rise above long-term yields: every US recession since 1970 was preceded by the 10-year Treasury yield falling below the 3-month yield.

Keynesian economics attributes cycles to fluctuations in aggregate demand, often producing short-run equilibria away from full employment. Paul Samuelson's multiplier-accelerator model shows how an initial shock can generate self-sustaining oscillations, while Richard Goodwin's model ties cycles to the distribution between wages and profits: tight labor markets lift wages, squeeze profits, and trigger downturns. Marxian economics treats recurrent crisis as intrinsic to capitalism, driven by a falling rate of profit and the boom-bust pattern of capital accumulation. The Austrian School blames credit expansion under fractional-reserve banking, especially when central banks hold rates artificially low, creating malinvestments that must eventually be liquidated.

Credit-based theories, including Irving Fisher's debt deflation and Hyman Minsky's Financial Instability Hypothesis, hold that rising private debt during booms plants the seeds of the next recession, with banks and asset bubbles at the center. Real business cycle theory, associated with Kydland and Prescott, attributes fluctuations entirely to exogenous shocks (mostly technology), rejecting monetary causes. Political business cycle models argue that incumbents stimulate the economy before elections and contract it afterward.

Modern governments respond to recessions with stabilization policy: expansionary fiscal policy (higher spending, lower taxes) or expansionary monetary policy (lower interest rates, quantitative easing). These tools gained dominance after the Keynesian revolution that followed the 1929–1939 Great Depression, and the post-1945 period saw generally milder cycles until the 2008 financial crisis. In 2003 Robert Lucas declared the problem of depression-prevention solved; five years later the worst downturn since the 1930s arrived.
