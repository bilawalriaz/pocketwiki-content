# Commerce

Commerce is the organised system through which goods, services, and other forms of value move from producers to consumers at scale, governed by agreed parameters of time, place, quantity, quality, and price. It exists because natural resources are unevenly distributed, human wants differ, and people specialise according to comparative advantage (producing whatever they make most efficiently relative to others). By enabling specialisation, commerce tightens economic interdependence, lifts productivity, and creates jobs.

## Trade and its aids

Commerce has two layers. The first is trade: the buying and selling of goods (raw materials, intermediates, finished products) and services for an agreed price, carried out at physical or online marketplaces. Services trade covers intangibles such as consulting, technical work, and training, and has grown rapidly with digital delivery. Trade is classified by geography as domestic (retail or wholesale) or international (import, export, entrepôt, or re-export). Specialised exchanges handle currencies, commodities, and securities under finance and investment.

The second layer is aids to trade: auxiliary services that remove the friction trade would otherwise face. They include banking and credit, transportation, packaging, warehousing, communication, advertising, insurance, and commercial intermediaries. Around them sit laws (including intellectual property and antitrust), tariffs and trade barriers, supply chain management, market dynamics, and macroeconomic conditions.

Commerce drives growth, interdependence, cultural exchange, and innovation. Unregulated commerce can concentrate wealth, depress living standards for the worst-off, encourage overconsumption, and degrade the environment. Sustainability, ethical sourcing, and circular-economy practices (which keep materials in use through reuse, repair, and recycling) have become central responses.

## Distinguishing commerce, business, and trade

Business refers to any activity or organisation that earns a living by providing goods or services, including individual firms. Trade is the actual transaction: a buyer and seller exchanging goods or services, almost always for money, at wholesale or retail scale, domestically or internationally. Commerce is the wider envelope: trade plus its aids, plus the legal, logistical, financial, and cultural systems that make exchange possible. Trade is a part of commerce, and commerce is one aspect of business.

## A short history

Commerce predates writing. Archaeological finds of ornaments and weapons far from their sources suggest prehistoric exchange, with the earliest long-distance exchange dated to around 150,000 years ago. In ancient Egypt, geographic isolation kept commerce modest until the New Kingdom (from about 1600 BC), when caravan routes linked Egypt with Phoenicia, Syria, and the Red Sea. By about 3000 BC, Babylon had become a major trading centre in the accessible Mesopotamian valleys, exchanging grain, wool, precious metals, and building materials, and Assyrian and Persian rule later unified the Near East under a single commercial authority.

The Phoenicians were antiquity's first people whose prosperity rested primarily on commerce, driven by scarce local resources. From about 1500 BC they ran regular maritime routes across the Mediterranean and into the Atlantic, exporting cloth, glass, metalware, and Tyrian purple dye, and importing tin from Britain, copper from Cyprus and Spain, silver from Spain, and gold and ivory from Africa. Greek geography, with its harbours and grain shortages, fostered maritime trade, and by 1000–600 BC Greek colonies had displaced the Phoenicians across the Mediterranean. Athens and Piraeus became the leading commercial centre in the fifth century BC, and Alexander's conquests shifted leadership to Alexandria, Seleucia, Antioch, and Rhodes.

Rome was not primarily a trading city, but the Pax Romana secured roads and order across western Europe, supporting trade as far as India and China. In parallel, Austronesian peoples had by at least 3000 BC built an Indian Ocean network linking East Asia, South Asia, the Arabian Peninsula, and East Africa, exchanging crops, spices, and boat technology. Overland, the Silk Road, named after Chinese silk, took organised shape during the Han dynasty around 130 BC and linked China with the Parthian and Roman worlds, channelled largely through Sogdian intermediaries.

Barter proved cumbersome for long-distance trade, and manufactured coins appeared independently in India, China, and the Aegean in the seventh century BC, with the earliest modern-style coinage usually credited to Lydia in Asia Minor. After the fall of western Rome, feudal fragmentation, insecure roads, and subsistence manors reduced European commerce to regulated local markets. Recovery came around the year 1000, accelerated by medieval banking, town-regulated markets, and the Age of Discovery, which gave commerce an intercontinental reach.

From the 15th to the early 20th century, European colonial powers, through companies such as the Dutch and British East India Companies, ran a globally unprecedented commercial system. In the 19th century, modern banking, international capital markets, and the Industrial Revolution reshaped commerce again. The post-colonial 20th century saw U.S.-led free trade spread through GATT (the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade) and the WTO, and standardised shipping containers in the mid-20th century made intermodal freight (cargo moving across ships, trains, and trucks using a single container standard) efficient, lifting developing countries' share of world trade from a quarter to a third by century's end.

In the 21st century, commerce is technology-driven through e-commerce, artificial intelligence, and automation, and is shaped by platforms, the gig economy, multilateral blocs such as the European Union and BRICS, climate-resilient practices, geopolitical friction, and trade wars.

## Participants and regulation

Commercial transactions are classified by who is exchanging with whom. Business-to-business (B2B) covers manufacturers, wholesalers, and service providers trading with each other, usually in larger volumes under negotiated terms. Business-to-consumer (B2C) is retail and direct online selling to individuals. Consumer-to-consumer (C2C) runs through intermediary platforms. Consumer-to-business (C2B) covers freelancers, user-generated content, and licensing. Business-to-government (B2G) and government-to-business (G2B) handle public procurement, licensing, customs, and digital regulatory services.

Domestic commerce is shaped by ministries and legislatures. International commerce is governed by bilateral treaties and, since the second world war, primarily by multilateral arrangements including GATT and its successor the World Trade Organization, with the International Chamber of Commerce setting private rules and resolving disputes.
