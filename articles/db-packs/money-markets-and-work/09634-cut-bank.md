# Cut bank

A cut bank is the outer bank of a meander, the curving section of a stream or river. It is the side of the bend that is continually worn away, sometimes called a river cliff or river-cut cliff. The opposite bank of the same bend, on the inside of the curve, is the point bar, where eroded material from upstream is deposited. Erosion here is paired with deposition there: the sediment building up on the inner bank is largely material scraped off cut banks somewhere upstream.

## How the shape forms

A meander is a bend in a stream. Water does not travel straight through a bend; its momentum carries it toward the outer bank, where the current strikes the bank directly and cuts into it. The result is a steep, often nearly vertical face, shaped roughly like a small cliff. On the same bend, the inner bank is the slow side: water there loses energy and drops its sediment, building a gently sloping point bar outward over time.

## Why erosion happens there

Two facts about the water explain the erosion. The outside of a bend is where the stream flows fastest, and it is also where the water is usually deepest. Faster, deeper flow carries more energy, concentrated where the current collides with the bank. The bank is undercut, so soil, vegetation, and sometimes large trees lose their support and topple or slide into the channel. Floods increase the damage, because higher water pushes harder against an already weakened face and can take out nearby property.

## Why this matters over time

Cut banks are not stable ground. The same combination of outer-bank erosion and inner-bank deposition is what slowly swings a meander across its floodplain. As the outer bank retreats and the inner bank grows, the loop becomes so exaggerated that the river cuts a shorter path across the neck of the bend. When the two sides reconnect, the abandoned loop is sealed off and becomes an oxbow lake, a crescent of still water marking where the river used to flow.
