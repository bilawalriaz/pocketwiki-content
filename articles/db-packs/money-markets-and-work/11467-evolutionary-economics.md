# Evolutionary economics

Evolutionary economics is a school of economic thought inspired by evolutionary biology. It treats the economy as a process rather than an equilibrium, emphasising qualitative, organisational, and structural change, innovation, complex interdependencies, self-evolving systems, and limited rationality. Initially a critique of mainstream neoclassical economics, by the early 21st century it had become part of the mainstream itself.

The field rejects fixed assumptions about preferences and decision-makers. It studies non-equilibrium processes that transform the economy from within, driven by diverse agents with bounded rationality (the realistic constraint that people have limited information and cognitive capacity) who learn from experience and whose differences generate change.

## Historical roots

The idea that human society evolves is ancient. Hesiod's *Works and Days* described five declining Ages of Man, a pattern echoed in Ovid's *Metamorphoses* and cycles such as the Hindu Yugas and the Aztec Five Suns. Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776) described economic development as a feedback process following a natural order. Nineteenth-century socialists including Proudhon and Marx treated economic systems as stages in social evolution: feudalism replaced by capitalism, which Marx argued would be replaced by socialism.

## Founding the field

Thorstein Veblen, often called the father of evolutionary economics, coined the term and argued that no universal "human nature" explains economic behaviour. He held that social institutions undergo selection and that economics should embrace Darwinian theory. His followers soon dropped the evolutionary framing; later figures such as Joseph Schumpeter and Friedrich Hayek used "evolution" loosely, meaning change rather than Darwinian selection.

Armen Alchian (1950) argued that under uncertainty and incomplete information, firms adapt to their environment rather than maximise profit, an early step toward formal models.

## The Nelson–Winter turning point

The 1982 publication of *An Evolutionary Theory of Economic Change* by Richard Nelson and Sidney Winter transformed the field. Drawing on Alchian and on Cyert and March's behavioural theory of the firm, they built a framework in which firms operate using organisational routines (regular, repeatable patterns of behaviour). Routines are evaluated and altered within a selection environment, applying natural selection to business behaviour. Nicolai Foss summarised the resulting focus as "the transformation of already existing structures and the emergence and possible spread of novelties." Economies came to be viewed as complex systems of non-linear, chaotic interactions, and Herbert Simon's bounded rationality became the standard behavioural assumption.

By the 1990s Geoffrey Hodgson described an international "invisible college" of evolutionary economists united by opposition to static, equilibrium-based mainstream analysis.

## Extensions

Paul Rubin used evolutionary analysis to explain decision-making anomalies such as zero-sum thinking and anti-trade preferences, treating the utility function (an economist's model of how people rank preferences) as a fitness function. Around 2004, Andrew Lo proposed the adaptive market hypothesis, treating financial market participants as "species" shaped by their environment. It allows efficient equilibria but predicts other states emerge under selection pressure; it has been tested on Islamic-market calendar effects and Bitcoin.

Oded Galor's unified growth theory frames human economic history as one continuous process. In a Malthusian epoch, where income stays near subsistence, technological progress raises population, which accelerates innovation by generating more ideas. Eventually, technology demands an educated workforce; families invest in fewer, better-educated children, triggering a demographic transition (a shift from high birth and death rates to low ones) and sustained growth. The process raises living standards, improves the position of the working class, contrary to Marx's prediction of immiseration, and advances gender equality.

## Criticism

Philip Mirowski (1983) questioned whether Nelson and Winter's framework was genuinely evolutionary in Veblen's sense, since it could absorb neoclassical orthodoxy. Some Veblenian institutionalists call it a "protective modification" of neoclassical economics. Proponents acknowledge the field is heterogeneous, with no convergence on a single integrated approach.

## Related fields

Evolutionary psychology applies Darwinian reasoning to cognition and behaviour, treating traits as adaptations or by-products. Evolutionary game theory, formalised by John Maynard Smith and George Price in 1973, models Darwinian competition as contests between strategies and has been adopted by economists to explain cooperation and strategic interaction in evolving populations.
