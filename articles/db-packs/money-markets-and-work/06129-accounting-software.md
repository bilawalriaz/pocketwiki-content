# Accounting software

Accounting software is a computer program that maintains a company's account books: recording transactions, tracking account balances, and turning raw entries into reports. Beyond bookkeeping, it can manage budgets, handle multiple currencies, run payroll, track customer relationships, and produce financial statements. Work to put these functions on computers goes back to the earliest days of electronic data processing, and the category has expanded from basic ledgers to real-time financial processing.

## Core modules

Most accounting software is built as a set of modules, each handling one area of accounting. The most common core modules are:

- **Accounts receivable** — records money received from customers.
- **Accounts payable** — records bills the company owes and payments made.
- **General ledger** — the company's "books," where all entries eventually land.
- **Billing** — produces invoices to clients.
- **Stock/inventory** — tracks goods on hand.
- **Purchase order** — orders inventory from suppliers.
- **Sales order** — records customer orders for inventory.
- **Bookkeeping** — records collections and payments.
- **Financial close management** — verifies and adjusts balances at the end of an accounting period.

Non-core modules extend the same idea: payroll (salaries, wages, taxes), expense reporting, timesheets, reconciliation (comparing two sides of a transaction for consistency), debt collection, electronic payment processing, bank feed integration, document attachment, and approval workflows. Vendors often rename these functions, but the underlying duties are similar across products.

## Types of accounting software

**Personal accounting** is simple, inexpensive software for individuals. It handles basic payable-style transactions, budgets, and simple reconciliation, and is widely used to prepare and file tax returns in formats accepted by national authorities such as the United States Internal Revenue Service (IRS). TurboTax is a common example.

**Low-end business software** is inexpensive and covers general accounting needs, often for small domestic firms. Many of these products are "single-entry" rather than double-entry, may not be compliant with Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) or International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) set by bodies such as the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB), and sometimes lack adequate security or audit trails.

**Mid-market software** can serve multiple national accounting standards, handle multiple currencies, and link to management information systems or project accounting add-ons. It typically rests on industry-standard databases and reporting tools and exposes configuration or extension points such as a software development kit (SDK).

**High-end software** for large organizations was historically tied to mainframe computers. By the 1970s and 1980s, vendors selling packages for the IBM mainframe market included Management Science America, McCormack & Dodge, Walker Interactive Products, Informatics General, and Cullinet Software. The most complex packages became part of broader enterprise resource planning (ERP) suites, with implementations often running longer than six months and requiring heavy integration, configuration, and customization. Free, web-based open-source high-end alternatives have also appeared.

**Hybrid software** targets companies at multiple growth stages. As small firms open new locations or hire remote workers, they need features (advanced customization, scalable databases, multi-location access) once reserved for larger buyers, often delivered through software-as-a-service (SaaS) or other internet-accessible applications.

## SaaS and cloud accounting

SaaS accounting replaced the large upfront license fee with a monthly recurring charge, pushed by faster computers and internet connections, and has been adopted so widely that legacy vendors now offer their own online versions.

Cloud accounting, introduced in 2011, goes further: financial data lives on a centralized computer but can be reached from any internet-connected device. Compared with software tied to one machine, cloud accounting removes worries about hardware maintenance and upgrades, can lower total cost, and supports access from multiple locations. Its main barrier is the security of data held by a third party.

## Data privacy and security

Cloud data sits with an outside provider rather than on the user's own machine, which creates three recurring sources of distrust: unauthorized access, unpredictability in how the data is handled, and nonconformity with the user's expectations. Security controls in cloud and traditional IT environments are broadly similar, but the legal landscape differs because data may be stored across jurisdictions with different rules, leaving the user with less direct control than local storage provides.
