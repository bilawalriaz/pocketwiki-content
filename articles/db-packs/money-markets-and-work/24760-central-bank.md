# Central bank

A central bank is a public institution that holds the monopoly right to issue a country's monetary base, the cash and reserves that anchor the banking system. Unlike commercial banks, central banks are not driven by profit on lending. Their job is to manage the money supply, set the policy interest rate, supervise or regulate commercial banks, serve as the "lender of last resort" in a crisis, and hold the state's foreign-exchange and gold reserves. Almost every sovereign state has one, and several monetary unions share a supranational central bank, such as the European Central Bank, established in 1998.

## Origins

The earliest institutions now regarded as central banks were municipal public banks, not national ones. The Taula de canvi in Barcelona, founded in 1401, is the oldest documented example. The Bank of Amsterdam (1609) and the Hamburger Bank (1619) widened the model across north-western Europe. The first national central bank is usually identified as Sweden's Riksbank, founded in Stockholm in 1664. The Bank of England was chartered in 1694 to raise war loans for the government, with sole English note-issuing rights. The Bank Charter Act of 1844 fixed a ratio between its gold reserves and its note issue, and it absorbed the role of lender of last resort after 1873, following Walter Bagehot's argument in *Lombard Street* that a central bank should lend freely against good collateral during a credit crunch.

The United States ended its First and Second Banks in the 1830s and only created the Federal Reserve System in 1913. About two-thirds of sovereign states lacked a central bank in 1900, and two waves of new foundations followed: after World War I under League of Nations guidance, and after World War II as decolonised states set up their own institutions. By the early 21st century, most countries had a publicly owned national central bank.

## How a central bank operates

Central banks create base money by buying assets such as government bonds, foreign currency, or gold, and crediting the seller's account at the central bank. They shrink the base by selling assets or letting bonds mature without replacement. In normal times the principal lever is the administered interest rate. Raising it makes commercial bank borrowing more expensive, which feeds through to mortgage and loan rates, slowing spending and inflation; cutting it has the opposite effect.

When the policy rate is pinned near zero, central banks turn to unconventional tools. Quantitative easing enlarges the balance sheet through large-scale asset purchases. Forward guidance commits the bank to keep rates low for a stated period. Credit easing targets specific markets by purchasing private assets. Central banks also supervise commercial banks, oversee payment and clearing systems, issue coins and notes, and act as the government's own bank. Profit earned on note-issuing power is seigniorage; the Federal Reserve and the ECB remit most of their interest income to their treasuries.

## Mandates and goals

The dominant mandate is price stability. Most central banks now target annual inflation close to 2%, so that a low, stable rate keeps expectations anchored. Many mandates add employment, economic growth, financial stability, or interest-rate stability as secondary goals. These can conflict: rate cuts that stimulate growth may fuel inflation, while aggressive inflation-fighting can deepen a recession. Some central banks, notably the People's Bank of China, have begun folding climate-related objectives into collateral rules and asset purchases, and over 70 are members of the Network for Greening the Financial System, founded in 2017, though critics such as Jens Weidmann argue climate policy falls outside a central bank's remit.

## Independence

Most developed-country central banks are institutionally independent from political control. The argument, formalised by Alesina and Summers in 1993, is that elected officials face incentives to print money before elections, eroding credibility and raising inflation. Independent banks with fixed, non-renewable governors' terms are less easily captured. The Deutsche Bundesbank became the first fully independent central bank in 1951, and the Bank of England was given operational independence in 1997.

Independence has several layers: institutional (protection from political instruction), goal (the right to set targets), operational (choice of instruments), personal (security of tenure for the governor), financial (no obligation to finance the government), and legal (capacity to act in court). Even "independent" banks remain accountable to parliaments through hearings, published minutes, and audited accounts. Populist governments can erode de facto independence; the dismissal of National Bank of Moldova governor Octavian Armașu in December 2023 is a recent illustration.

## Scale

In 2016, 75% of all central-bank assets were held by four centres: China, the United States, Japan, and the eurozone. The top five balance sheets were the Federal Reserve ($8,349 billion), the Bank of Japan ($6,621 billion), the ECB ($6,590 billion), the People's Bank of China ($3,450 billion), and the Deutsche Bundesbank ($3,160 billion). Collectively, central banks held over 33,000 metric tons of gold in 2018, roughly a fifth of all gold ever mined, and continue to purchase around 500 tonnes a year. Several major central banks, including the People's Bank of China, are also developing central-bank digital currencies to extend the same monopoly on base money into electronic form.

## Criticism

Central banks have drawn opposition from across the political spectrum. Thomas Jefferson warned that "banking establishments are more dangerous than standing armies," and Andrew Jackson destroyed the Second Bank of the United States, calling it a "money power." Austrian-school economists from Friedrich Hayek to Murray Rothbard argue that central-bank credit expansion distorts interest rates, misallocates capital, and drives the business cycle, a view that fed the 2008 surge of public scepticism and renewed interest in free banking, full-reserve banking, and currency boards.
