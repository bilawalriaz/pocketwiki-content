# Balance of trade

The balance of trade is the difference between the monetary value of a country's exports and its imports of goods over a given period. By the official IMF definition it covers goods only, though some definitions fold in services. Because exports and imports are flows measured over time, the figure can be positive, negative, or zero without implying equilibrium between the two sides.

When exports exceed imports, the country runs a **trade surplus**, also called a positive balance. When imports exceed exports, it runs a **trade deficit**. As of 2016, roughly 60 of 200 countries posted surpluses. The largest surpluses were held by China (about $823 billion), Germany ($226 billion), and Singapore ($154 billion); the largest deficits by the United States (about $1.15 trillion), the United Kingdom ($271 billion), and India ($241 billion).

## Where it sits in the accounts

The balance of trade is one component of the **current account**, which also includes investment income from a country's net international investment position and international aid. A current-account surplus adds to the country's net international asset position; a deficit subtracts from it. There is also a deeper identity: the trade balance equals the gap between what a country produces and what it spends on goods, since any output not consumed domestically must be exported, and any spending not matched by domestic output must be imported.

A measurement problem shadows every official figure. When statisticians add up reported exports and imports for all countries, global exports come in about 1 percent higher than global imports, so the world appears to run a positive balance with itself. That cannot literally be true, since every transaction is one country's export and another's matching import. The gap is mostly attributed to money laundering, tax evasion, and smuggling, and unusually appears mainly between developed countries with otherwise trusted statistics.

## What shifts the balance

The main drivers are relative production costs (land, labor, capital, taxes) in the exporting versus importing economy; the cost and availability of raw materials and intermediate inputs; currency exchange rate movements; tariffs, quotas, and non-tariff barriers such as environmental, health, or safety standards; the supply of foreign exchange available to pay for imports; and how responsive domestic supply is to price changes.

The balance also moves with the **business cycle**. In export-led economies, booms shift the balance toward more exports. In demand-led economies such as the United States and Australia, the same expansion pulls in more imports and pushes the balance toward deficit.

There is a distinction between the **monetary balance** (measured in dollars) and the **physical balance** (measured in tonnes of raw materials, also called Total Material Consumption). Developed countries import large quantities of raw materials, process them, and export finished goods, so their physical deficit is typically larger than their monetary deficit suggests.

## Historical roots and the mercantilist view

Mercantilist policy in early modern Europe held that a surplus was good and a deficit harmful, shaping colonial trade: colonies sent raw materials and cash crops to Europe and received processed goods in return. The earliest English statement appeared in 1549: "We must always take heed that we buy no more from strangers than we sell them, for so should we impoverish ourselves and enrich them." Thomas Mun's 1630 *England's Treasure by Foreign Trade* systematized the position. Adam Smith in 1776 dismissed it: trade between two places is not a contest in which one side wins what the other loses.

## Keynes, Friedman, and the modern debate

At the 1944 Bretton Woods conference, John Maynard Keynes proposed the **Keynes Plan**, whose core principle was that surplus and deficit countries share blame for imbalances and should both act to restore balance, with an International Clearing Union settling debts through a new accounting unit called the bancor. The plan was rejected, mainly because the United States would not accept equal treatment as a creditor.

Monetarists reached a different conclusion. Frédéric Bastiat argued by reductio ad absurdum that a deficit can signal a successful economy: a trader exporting 50 francs of wine, selling it abroad for 70, and using the proceeds to import coal worth 90 makes a 40-franc profit, even though customs records a 20-franc trade deficit. Milton Friedman held that deficits tend to correct themselves, since high exports push a currency up, dampening further exports, while imports do the opposite. Since the United States abandoned fixed exchange rates in 1971, its cumulative current-account deficit reached $7.75 trillion by 2010, matched by definition by an inflow of foreign investment. David Hume had reached a similar conclusion earlier: gold hoarded by an export-surplus country raises domestic prices, eroding competitiveness until the balance corrects.

Most modern economists reject the idea that bilateral deficits are inherently bad, and a 2018 NBER paper studying 151 countries from 1963 to 2014 found that tariffs do little to shift the trade balance. The IMF warns that deficits can still cause balance-of-payments problems and foreign-exchange shortages. Joseph Stiglitz has argued that surplus countries impose a "negative externality" on partners by suppressing global demand, and Ben Bernanke has said Germany's large surplus redirects demand away from its eurozone neighbors. Carla Norrlöf lists three benefits the United States draws from its deficit: consuming more than it produces, importing efficiently produced intermediate goods, and using its large import market as leverage in trade talks. Countries with high savings rates, such as Japan and Germany, tend to run surpluses, while those with low savings rates, especially the United States, tend to run deficits.

## Effect on GDP and distinction from the balance of payments

Net exports enter the expenditure-based calculation of GDP directly, so a surplus adds to it and a deficit subtracts from it. The balance of trade is narrower than the **balance of payments**: the trade balance covers only visible goods, while the balance of payments adds services, investment income, transfers, and capital flows.
