# Employment

Employment is a contractual relationship in which an employer pays an employee for assigned work. The employer may be a corporation, not-for-profit, cooperative, or other entity; the employee contributes labour and skill in return for compensation. In modern OECD economies this wage labour arrangement is the dominant form of work, and the work product generally becomes the employer's property.

## Pay and Benefits

Employees are paid by hourly wages, piecework (per unit produced), an annual salary, commissions (common in sales and real estate), or gratuities. Some sectors add bonuses for meeting targets, or stock options that align the worker's interest with company performance.

Common non-wage benefits are health, dental, and life insurance, housing, disability coverage, retirement pensions, paid vacation and sick leave, childcare, and tuition reimbursement. Many governments require employer contributions to social security or pension schemes. Under the faithless servant doctrine, recognised in several US states including New York, an employee who acts disloyally must forfeit compensation earned during the disloyal period.

## Employee vs. Independent Contractor

Whether a worker is an employee or an independent contractor is a central legal question. Employees receive benefits, tax withholding, workers' insurance, and legal protections that contractors do not.

An independent contractor agrees on a finished product, controls the means of achieving it, serves the public at large rather than a single client, supplies their own tools, and signs a written agreement specifying contractor status. Under US law the default assumption is that a worker is an employee; courts apply the ABC test, a three-part legal standard for worker classification, to decide. Indicators of employee status include regular pay, set hours, employer-supplied tools, close supervision, acting on the employer's behalf, and working for only one employer at a time.

Intellectual property follows the same line: an employer owns inventions by an employee "hired to invent" even without a written assignment, while a company commissioning work from a contractor owns the copyright only with a written "work made for hire" agreement or assignment.

## Finding Work and Ending It

Workers and employers connect through newspaper classified ads, online job boards, recruitment agencies paid by the employer, "Help Wanted" signs, and job interviews. After hiring, employers invest in training to equip workers and improve retention.

Most employment is at-will, terminable by either party, often with a notice period. Tenure protects some university professors, civil servants, and orchestra musicians from dismissal at will. Other endings include layoffs, severance arrangements, and employment bonds that penalise departure.

## Voice and Organisation

Employees may form unions to collectively bargain over wages, benefits, and working conditions. In the United States, unions are legally recognised as worker representatives and centre activity on collective bargaining, dispute resolution, and political lobbying; most belong to the AFL-CIO or the Strategic Organizing Center. Unjust termination complaints in non-union US workplaces go to the Department of Labor; in Canada, Ontario uses its Ministry of Labour and Quebec its Commission des normes du travail.

## Working Poor and Age

Employment does not guarantee escaping poverty. The International Labour Organisation estimates that as many as 40% of workers worldwide earn less than $2 per day. Oxfam and sociologist Mark Robert Rank argue that the US economy increasingly fails to provide jobs that adequately support families, and sociologist Matthew Desmond calls US wages among the lowest in the industrialised world. A 1% rise in productivity was associated with a 0.07% reduction in employment growth in the 1960s, but a 0.54% reduction by the 2000s, meaning productivity gains no longer translate into new jobs as they once did.

Young workers face roughly double the occupational injury rate of older workers, because they are concentrated in high-risk industries such as agriculture, restaurants, waste management, and mining, and have less driving experience. US law under the Fair Labor Standards Act restricts those under 18 from certain dangerous jobs. Older workers may continue working for income or purpose but sometimes face age discrimination and may shift to less physical roles.

## Four Models

Scholars frame the employment relationship in four ways, each implying different views of unions, regulation, and management. Mainstream economics sees it as a mutually beneficial transaction between self-interested equals in a free market. Human resource management (unitarism) sees it as a long-term partnership of shared interests, where conflict reflects poor management. Pluralist industrial relations sees a bargained exchange among stakeholders with both shared and competing goals and unequal bargaining power. Critical industrial relations sees an unequal power relationship embedded in systemic social inequality.

## Critiques and Alternatives

Critics use the term wage slavery to draw parallels between selling labour and chattel slavery; Cicero made similar comparisons, and Marx analysed wage labour in *Wage Labour and Capital*. Philosopher John Dewey argued that until "industrial feudalism" yields to "industrial democracy", politics will remain the shadow cast by big business. Management scholar Jeffrey Pfeffer estimates that toxic working conditions, job insecurity, and long hours in the United States cause around 120,000 excess deaths annually, making the workplace the fifth leading cause of death in the country.

Alternatives to formal employment include self-employment (as sole proprietor, independent contractor, or partner), volunteering, post-secondary education, and social assistance. Indentured servants and slaves are not considered employed because they receive no wages.

## Globalization

Economist Eddy Lee's 1996 analysis identifies four effects of globalisation on employment: import competition and offshoring shrink manufacturing jobs and suppress wages for unskilled workers in industrialised countries; economic liberalisation in developing countries produces job losses that outstrip new hiring; a global labour market pressures wages and conditions downward in a race to the bottom; and capital mobility weakens the state's ability to regulate. About 70% of workers in industrialised countries now work in services, most of which are non-tradable, pushing demand toward higher skills and reshaping what employment looks like.
