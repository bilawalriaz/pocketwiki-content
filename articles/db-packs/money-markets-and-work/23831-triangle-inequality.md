# Triangle inequality

The triangle inequality is the rule that for any three points, the direct distance between two of them can never exceed the route through the third. In a triangle with side lengths $a$, $b$, $c$ this becomes $c \le a + b$, together with the two symmetric statements. Equality holds only in a degenerate triangle whose three vertices lie on a line, where one side equals the sum of the other two. A non-degenerate triangle exists precisely when all three strict inequalities hold; if one holds with equality, the triangle is degenerate.

For a proper triangle, $a + b > c$, $b + c > a$, $c + a > b$, equivalently $|a - b| < c < a + b$, and the longest side is less than the semiperimeter. The area, given by Heron's formula $4 \cdot \text{area} = \sqrt{(a+b+c)(-a+b+c)(a-b+c)(a+b-c)}$, is a real positive number if and only if these inequalities hold. The converse is also true: any three positive numbers satisfying them can be realised as the sides of a triangle with positive area.

The same shape of statement governs vectors in a normed space: $\|u + v\| \le \|u\| + \|v\|$ for all vectors $u$, $v$. In a Euclidean, or more generally strictly convex, space equality holds exactly when $u$ and $v$ lie along the same ray, so one is a non-negative scalar multiple of the other and the triangle $u, v, u+v$ is degenerate. This is subadditivity, and any function proposed as a norm must satisfy it. The $\ell^1$ (Manhattan) norm is a counterexample to the "if and only if" part: $u = (1, 0)$ and $v = (0, 1)$ give a non-degenerate triangle yet $\|u + v\|_1 = 2 = \|u\|_1 + \|v\|_1$.

The statement $d(A, C) \le d(A, B) + d(B, C)$ is one of the three axioms of a metric space. Most of the structure of metric spaces, including the fact that every convergent sequence is Cauchy, follows from it: if both $x_n$ and $x_m$ lie within $\varepsilon/2$ of a limit $x$, then $d(x_n, x_m) < \varepsilon$. Common examples include the absolute value on the real line, the taxicab norm $\|v\|_1 = \sum |v_i|$, the Euclidean norm $\|v\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum |v_i|^2}$, and the general $p$-norm $\|v\|_p = (\sum |v_i|^p)^{1/p}$ for $p \ge 1$ (Minkowski's inequality). The Euclidean case follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to $\langle u+v, u+v \rangle$.

A useful companion is the reverse triangle inequality: $|\|u\| - \|v\|| \le \|u - v\|$, or in geometric language, any side of a triangle is at least the difference of the other two. It gives lower bounds rather than upper ones, and it makes every norm and every distance function Lipschitz continuous with constant 1. The two inequalities are equivalent: each follows from the other by writing $u$ as $(u-v)+v$ (or its mirror) and applying the original bound.

The polygon generalisation, provable by induction, says any polygonal path is at least as long as the straight line between its endpoints, so each side of a polygon is shorter than the sum of the rest. Since arc length is the supremum of polygonal approximations, any curve is at least as long as the straight-line distance between its endpoints, which is why a straight line is the shortest path between two points in Euclidean space. The same shape recurs in higher dimensions: in a simplex, the hypervolume of any facet is at most the sum of the hypervolumes of the other facets, a result stronger than the ordinary triangle inequality in some cases, such as forbidding four points with distances $AB = BC = CA = 26$ and $AZ = BZ = CZ = 14$.

In Minkowski space, where the metric is not positive-definite, the inequality reverses for two future-timelike vectors, giving $\|u + v\| \ge \|u\| + \|v\|$; the same reversed form holds for past-timelike or null vectors, while a space-like plane obeys the usual triangle inequality.
