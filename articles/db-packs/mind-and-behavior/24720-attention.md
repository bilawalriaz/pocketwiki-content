# Attention

Attention is the concentration of awareness on some task or stimulus while largely excluding others. It is not a single faculty but an umbrella for several related processes: selective attention (favoring some inputs over others), sustained attention (maintaining focus over time), divided attention (sharing resources across tasks), and orienting (shifting focus in space or time). These processes depend on distributed networks in the frontal and parietal cortex and subcortical structures, and they are tightly linked to working memory and executive control.

A central finding is that attention is severely limited. Wilhelm Wundt, who introduced the systematic study of attention, showed that only about three to six items can be held in the focus of consciousness at once, and that an item needs roughly a tenth of a second of exposure to enter that focus. Decades of work on subitizing, the rapid apprehension of small numerosities, confirmed the upper bound. The scope expands with development, from roughly two items at six months of age to five or more by age five. More items in the focus yield more possible combinations (3 items produce 6 pairings, 4 produce 24, 6 produce 720), so capacity trades against combinatorial richness.

The brain performs this selection through two interacting systems. Bottom-up, or exogenous, attention is automatic and stimulus-driven: a sudden flash, motion, or onset captures focus and may trigger a reflexive saccade, an abrupt jump of the eyes toward the stimulus. Bottom-up selection is guided by a saliency map, a neural representation that marks the most conspicuous location, built in primary visual cortex (V1) and relayed through the superior colliculus, a midbrain structure that orients the eyes and head. Top-down, or endogenous, attention is voluntary and goal-driven, directed to locations or features that match current intentions. Top-down control relies on the frontal cortex and basal ganglia and is strongly affected by cognitive load; bottom-up capture is comparatively resistant to load. External cues are not purely exogenous, however; they capture attention mainly when behaviorally relevant. Brief peripheral cues speed responses, but if the cue precedes a target by more than about 300 ms the benefit reverses, a phenomenon called inhibition of return, in which attention resists revisiting the just-cued location.

Michael Posner's framework partitions attention into three interacting networks: alerting (achieving readiness, modulated by norepinephrine), orienting (directing focus to a stimulus), and executive attention (recruited during conflict, anchored in the anterior cingulate cortex). Eric Knudsen identified four underlying components: working memory, competitive selection (often implemented by lateral inhibition, in which active neurons suppress their neighbors), top-down sensitivity control, and salience filters. A characteristic EEG signature of focused attention is elevated gamma-band activity, neural oscillations in the 40–60 Hz range.

Attention can also be classified by what it does. Focused attention is the basic response to a specific stimulus. Sustained attention, or vigilance, is maintaining focus over long periods; performance typically declines over time in the vigilance decrement. Selective attention is filtering relevant from irrelevant input, the mechanism behind the cocktail party effect, the ability to follow one conversation amid many. Alternating attention is flexibly switching between tasks with different demands. Divided attention is multitasking, and almost always degrades performance. Dual-task studies, especially of driving while phoning, show more errors, harder braking, and lane drift, with little difference between hands-free and handheld phones, indicating that the bottleneck is cognitive rather than motor. Kahneman's single-pool resource model captures this: one finite pool of effort is divided among tasks, and similar modalities (two verbal streams, for instance) interfere more than dissimilar ones. Automaticity changes the picture: once a skill is overlearned past 100% accuracy, as in trained Morse code operators who transcribe while conversing, it consumes far fewer attentional resources.

Several metaphors structure research on attention. Broadbent's early filter model held that unattended inputs are blocked before meaning is extracted. Treisman's attenuation model softened this, proposing that unattended inputs are merely weakened rather than blocked, which is why one's own name can break through. Deutsch and Deutsch pushed selection further back, to the response stage, claiming all inputs are processed semantically. Nilli Lavie's perceptual load theory reconciled these positions: under high load, relevant stimuli exhaust capacity and produce early selection; under low load, spare capacity spills onto irrelevant stimuli and produces late-selection effects. The spotlight metaphor treats attention as a movable beam with a high-resolution focus and a coarse fringe; the zoom-lens variant lets that beam widen (lower resolution over more area) or narrow (higher resolution over less), with a minimum focus of about one degree of visual angle. Feature integration theory adds that basic features such as color and orientation are registered automatically and in parallel across the visual field, but binding them into objects requires focused attention.

Attention also varies with culture. In many Indigenous communities in the Americas, children learn by observing and pitching in to ongoing group activities, a pattern called simultaneous attention, sustaining focus on multiple events at once rather than alternating between them. This produces especially keen observational skills compared with middle-class European-American contexts, where children more often shift between discrete, adult-directed activities. Social attention adds another dimension: people preferentially process self-related information such as their own face or name, and they also attend to others' gaze, posture, and intentions, with imbalances in this self-to-other balance implicated in autism spectrum disorder and Williams syndrome.

Limited capacity, selective filtering, and top-down control together explain why attention is both essential and fragile: it lets a brain with finite resources act effectively on the most important information, and it is the bottleneck that makes multitasking costly, distraction dangerous, and sustained focus on uninteresting material difficult.
