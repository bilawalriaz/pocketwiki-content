# Brain connectivity estimators

Brain connectivity estimators are mathematical tools that turn recorded brain activity into patterns of links between regions, neurons, or neural assemblies. The goal is not just correlation but a usable picture of how signals flow, ideally with direction, strength, and frequency. Three concepts sit underneath every estimator: structural connectivity (physical wiring, often traced with Diffusion Weighted Imaging), functional connectivity (statistical dependence between distant signals), and effective connectivity (directed influence from one neural system on another). The literature often calls causal or directed connectivity "functional"; the cleaner view treats functional as undirected dependence and effective as directed influence estimated from time series such as EEG (electroencephalography, voltage recorded at the scalp), LFP (local field potential, voltage recorded inside the brain), spike trains, or fMRI (functional magnetic resonance imaging, a blood-flow proxy for neural activity).

Estimators split along two axes. The first is linear versus non-linear: correlation and coherence are linear; mutual information, transfer entropy (a non-linear measure of how much one signal's past reduces uncertainty about another), generalised synchronisation, and phase synchronisation are non-linear. The second is bivariate versus multivariate: bivariate methods look at pairs of signals, multivariate methods model the whole set at once. Non-linear methods need long stationary recordings, are noise-sensitive, and, except for transfer entropy, do not give direction. Surrogate-data tests on EEG and LFP (tests that shuffle or phase-randomise the data to check whether a non-linear property is genuinely present) have repeatedly found that genuine non-linearity is the exception, and that linear methods already perform well on non-linear signals. Non-linear estimators should therefore be used only when non-linearity has actually been demonstrated.

The bivariate-versus-multivariate distinction is the central methodological choice. Bivariate measures always register a flow whenever two channels have a delay, even if the delay only reflects distance from a single source. With two or three simultaneous sources the resulting diagram collapses into dense, disorganised structure that looks almost random, which may explain reports of "random" brain connectivity in some studies. Multivariate estimators, by fitting all channels jointly, suppress these spurious flows and recover the deterministic structure that fMRI, inverse solutions (algorithms that estimate which brain sources produced the recorded scalp signals), and intracortical recordings independently show exists.

The most developed multivariate framework is Granger causality: a series Y "Granger-causes" X if past values of Y improve the prediction of X beyond what X's own past provides. Granger himself noted that this is only meaningful when the channel set is complete. The practical implementation is the multivariate autoregressive (MVAR) model, in which each sample X(t) is expressed as a weighted sum of p past samples from k channels plus noise. Model order p is chosen by information-theoretic criteria such as AIC (Akaike Information Criterion, a score balancing fit quality against model complexity). Transforming to the frequency domain yields a transfer matrix H(f) that is non-symmetric, so causal direction can be read from it. Three estimators live inside this framework.

The Granger Causality Index (GCI) is defined in the time domain as the log ratio of residual variance of an n-channel model to that of an (n−1)-channel model with the candidate source removed. It lies between 0 and 1 and is mainly used when the timing of influence matters more than its spectral content. For brain signals the spectral content usually matters, because a task can increase propagation in one frequency band and decrease it in another.

The Directed Transfer Function (DTF), introduced by Kaminski and Blinowska, gives the normalised ratio of inflow from channel j into channel i at frequency f relative to all inflows into i. It reveals where the sinks are and, in its non-normalised form (NDTF), scales with coupling strength. Because DTF propagates through cascades, a chain 1→2→3 also shows a flow 1→3; the direct DTF (dDTF) multiplies a modified DTF by partial coherence (a frequency-domain measure of the association between two signals after removing the influence of the others) to expose only direct links. The distinction between direct and indirect flows matters for implanted electrodes but is less critical for scalp EEG. DTF also extends to point processes such as spike trains, and to spike-train–LFP interactions.

Partial Directed Coherence (PDC), introduced by Baccala and Sameshima, normalises by the outflow from source channel j instead of the inflow into target i, so it emphasises sources rather than sinks and is restricted to direct flows. Its normalisation has a quirk: adding more variables that are influenced by the same source dilutes PDC, so a single strong output looks larger than the same intensity split across several outputs.

Brain activity is non-stationary, so static MVAR fits miss dynamics. The standard remedy is a short sliding window, fit independently on each trial, with correlation matrices averaged across trials and bootstrap resampling (recomputing the statistic on randomly resampled trials to estimate its variability) used to obtain error bars on the resulting Short-Time DTF (SDTF). Window length is always a trade-off: shorter windows track change but reduce fit quality. The number of samples in a window (k channels × N_T points) should exceed the number of model parameters (k²p) by roughly an order of magnitude, which forces experimenters to repeat trials.

Applications concentrate where directed information flow is the natural object: localising epileptic foci, tracking EEG propagation across sleep stages (sources drift frontally as sleep deepens and settle over the corpus callosum in deep sleep, consistent with subcortical drive to cortex), comparing real and imagined finger movement (a short gamma burst from the primary motor cortex during real movement, replaced by later, cross-talk-heavy activity between motor and supplementary motor areas during imagery), Continuous Attention Test performance (engagement of prefrontal and frontal regions with active inhibition by pre-SMA and right inferior frontal cortex), and clinical work on schizophrenia, depression, stroke, and tumours using atlas- or DWI-based parcellation.

A practical caution: DTF and PDC were once claimed to be insensitive to volume conduction (the smearing of a single neural source across multiple scalp electrodes), but Brunner et al. (2016) and Van De Steen et al. (2016) showed this is not generally true. Scalp-level DTF and PDC can still be contaminated, so source reconstruction or equivalent preprocessing should precede connectivity estimation, and the older insensitivity claim should be treated as superseded.
