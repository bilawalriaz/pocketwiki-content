# Artificial general intelligence

Artificial general intelligence (AGI) is a hypothetical AI that matches or surpasses human capability across virtually all cognitive tasks, generalising knowledge and solving novel problems without task-specific reprogramming. Beyond it, artificial superintelligence (ASI) would exceed the best human performance in every domain. Narrow AI (ANI) is confined to well-defined tasks such as speech recognition. AGI is a stated goal of OpenAI, Google, xAI, and Meta; a 2020 survey found 72 active AGI projects across 37 countries.

## Definitions and a levels framework

The term was used in 1997 by Mark Gubrud and popularised around 2002 by Shane Legg and Ben Goertzel. Aliases include strong AI, full AI, and human-level AI. In 2000, Marcus Hutter proposed AIXI, a mathematical formalism defining intelligence as an agent's ability to achieve goals across a wide range of environments.

In 1980, philosopher John Searle distinguished a weak AI hypothesis (machines can act as if they think) from a strong AI hypothesis (machines can genuinely have a mind and consciousness). Mainstream AI research cares about behaviour, not inner experience, so for most researchers "strong AI" and "AGI" are separate questions.

In 2023, Google DeepMind proposed five performance levels: emerging, competent, expert, virtuoso, and superhuman. A competent AGI outperforms 50% of skilled adults on non-physical tasks; a superhuman AGI (equivalent to ASI) surpasses 100%. They classify large language models like ChatGPT and LLaMA 2 as emerging AGI, comparable to unskilled humans. They also define five autonomy levels, from tool (fully human-controlled) to agent (fully autonomous).

## Required capabilities

Researchers generally agree an AGI must reason, use strategy, solve puzzles, and make judgements under uncertainty; represent knowledge, including common sense; plan; learn; communicate in natural language; and integrate these skills toward any goal. Many also add imagination, autonomy, and physical capabilities such as sensing, acting, and detecting hazards. A problem is called "AI-complete" if solving it is believed to require AGI, because no purpose-specific algorithm can do it alone.

## Tests proposed for human-level AGI

| Test | What it requires |
|---|---|
| Turing test (1950) | Convince a human judge it is human in text conversation |
| Ikea test | A robot autonomously assembles flat-pack furniture from parts and instructions |
| Coffee test (Steve Wozniak) | Enter an unfamiliar home and make coffee |
| Suleyman's test | Turn US$100,000 into US$1,000,000 |
| Video-game test | Learn and succeed at a wide range of games unknown to its developers |

In 2014, the chatbot Eugene Goostman reportedly fooled 33% of judges, though the result was widely disputed. In 2025, a pre-registered study found GPT-4.5 judged human in 73% of five-minute conversations, surpassing real confederates at 67%. Robot progress on the coffee and Ikea tests has been demonstrated by task-specific systems, including MIT's 2013 multi-robot table assembly and Figure AI's 2024 Keurig operation learned from video.

## A century of optimism and disappointment

Modern AI research began in the mid-1950s. Herbert Simon predicted in 1965 that machines would do any work a man can do within twenty years; Marvin Minsky said in 1967 the problem of creating AI would be substantially solved within a generation. By the early 1970s the difficulty was clear, and funding shifted to narrower "applied AI". Japan's Fifth Generation Computer Project in the 1980s revived AGI ambitions with a ten-year timeline, then failed. By the 1990s, AI researchers avoided the term "human level" for fear of being called dreamers.

In 2012, AlexNet won ImageNet with a 15.3% top-5 error rate, launching the deep learning era. In 2020, GPT-3 demonstrated broad task ability without specific training. In 2022, DeepMind's Gato handled more than 600 different tasks. In 2023, Microsoft researchers argued GPT-4 could be viewed as an early, still incomplete AGI, and a separate study found GPT-4 outperformed 99% of humans on the Torrance tests of creative thinking.

A 2025 review of surveys from the previous 15 years reported most experts agreed AGI will arrive before 2100, with recent surveys clustering around 2040. In December 2025, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman said "AGI kinda went whooshing by" with less societal impact than expected, urging the field to define superintelligence instead.

## Brain emulation as an alternative path

Whole brain emulation scans and maps a biological brain in detail, then runs a faithful simulation on a computer. The human brain has roughly 10¹¹ neurons and an estimated 10¹⁴ to 5×10¹⁴ synapses in adulthood, performing around 10¹⁴ synaptic updates per second. Ray Kurzweil estimated 10¹⁶ operations per second would match the brain and predicted sufficient hardware between 2015 and 2025; 10¹⁶ was reached in 2011 and 10¹⁸ in 2022.

Critics note that artificial neuron models are far simpler than biological ones, and that glial cells and molecular detail may add several orders of magnitude of required computation. Embodied cognition, the theory that meaning depends on a physical body that interacts with the world, argues a full human-like simulation may require a robotic body.

## Why the debate is hard to settle

There is no agreed definition of intelligence, and questions about consciousness, goal-setting, and whether intelligence emerges from scale remain contested. Four polls from 2012–2013 put the median expert estimate for 50% confidence in AGI at 2040–2050, with a mean of 2081 and 16.5% answering "never" at 90% confidence. A separate analysis of 95 predictions from 1950–2012 found a bias toward predicting arrival within 15–25 years of the prediction, a pattern that has repeatedly failed.

Sceptics such as Yann LeCun argue AGIs will have no desire to dominate, and that fears of existential risk serve as regulatory capture. Others, including Paul Allen, Alan Winfield, and Stevan Harnad, hold that current computing is as far from human-level AI as spaceflight is from faster-than-light travel, and that top-down symbolic approaches may never ground meaning in embodied experience. In 2024, Geoffrey Hinton shortened his own estimate to 5–20 years and stressed the attendant existential risks.

## Risks and rewards

In 2023, the CEOs of Google DeepMind, OpenAI, and Anthropic signed a statement that mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside pandemics and nuclear war. OpenAI researchers estimated 80% of the US workforce could have at least 10% of work tasks affected by LLMs, with 19% seeing at least 50% affected, most exposed in white-collar roles. Proponents point to improved productivity, high-quality medical diagnostics, personalised education, and rational disaster prevention; critics emphasise mass unemployment, entrenchment of existing values, and the loss of human purpose.
