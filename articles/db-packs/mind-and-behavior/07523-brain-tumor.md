# Brain tumor

A brain tumor is an uncontrolled growth of cells forming a mass inside the skull. Tumors are classified as **malignant** (cancerous, invading tissue) or **benign** (non-cancerous, but still dangerous in the closed cranial space). They are further split into **primary** tumors, which arise from brain or meningeal tissue, and **secondary** (metastatic) tumors, which have spread from cancers elsewhere in the body. Secondary tumors are roughly four times more common than primary ones, and about half originate in the lung.

Because the brain is encased in bone, even a benign growth harms by compressing tissue and raising pressure. The **blood–brain barrier**, a tightly sealed layer of cells lining brain blood vessels, normally keeps toxins and most drugs out. This barrier is what makes brain tumors both hard to treat and hard to image, since diagnostic tracers used elsewhere in the body often cannot reach them.

## Causes and risk factors

For most patients no cause is identified. The clearest environmental risk is **ionizing radiation**: about 4% of brain cancers in the general population are attributed to CT-scan radiation, and the relative risk rises by roughly 0.8 per 100 grays of exposure. Inherited syndromes including neurofibromatosis, tuberous sclerosis, and von Hippel–Lindau disease substantially raise risk, and mutations in tumor-suppressor genes such as *TP53* are implicated in some tumors.
