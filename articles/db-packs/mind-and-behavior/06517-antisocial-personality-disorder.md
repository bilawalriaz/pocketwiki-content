# Antisocial personality disorder

Antisocial personality disorder (ASPD) is a mental condition defined by a long-term pattern of ignoring other people's rights and well-being. It belongs to DSM-5 Cluster B, sits in ICD-11 under the unified personality disorder category with the trait specifier "Dissociality," and usually appears in childhood or early adolescence, peaking in the late teens and twenties. Lifetime prevalence in the general population is 1–4% (about 6% in men, 2% in women); in prisons it reaches roughly 47% of male inmates and 21% of female inmates.

## Core features

DSM-5 diagnosis requires the person to be at least 18, to have shown conduct disorder before age 15, and to display at least three of seven adult traits: failure to obey laws, deceitfulness, impulsivity, irritability and aggression, reckless disregard for safety, consistent irresponsibility, and lack of remorse. Two underlying personality domains, antagonism and disinhibition, drive most of the picture. Common features include chronic boredom, shallow emotions, contempt toward others, difficulty forming stable relationships, and a tendency to manipulate or exploit people through charm, intimidation, or both. Many also have intact social cognition (the ability to read others' intentions) but use it to deceive rather than connect, and they often take sexual and physical risks, including multiple partners, unprotected sex, and substance misuse. ASPD is the personality disorder most strongly linked to addiction.

## Causes

ASPD arises from a mix of genetic and environmental factors. Twin and family studies show moderate heritability. Specific gene variants have been linked to it: low-activity MAOA (the "MAOA-L" allele) and the short "S" allele of the serotonin transporter gene SLC6A4, especially when combined with early adversity. Neurobiologically, lower serotonin activity (measured by the metabolite 5-HIAA) is associated with impulsivity and aggression, and structural brain imaging shows reduced gray matter in the prefrontal cortex (especially ventromedial and orbitofrontal regions), the amygdala, and the insula, areas that govern decision-making, impulse control, and emotional learning. Higher testosterone, partly counteracted by cortisol, is associated with more violent behavior. Childhood hyperactivity, conduct disorder, lower IQ, reading problems, animal cruelty, and fire-setting predict ASPD in adulthood.

## Development and diagnosis

Conduct disorder, the childhood precursor, involves repeated aggression, theft, vandalism, and rule-breaking, with a childhood-onset form before age 10 and an adolescent-onset form after 10. About 25–40% of youths with conduct disorder go on to receive an ASPD diagnosis; children without conduct problems by 15 almost never develop ASPD. Differential diagnosis includes psychopathy, ADHD, narcissistic, borderline, and bipolar disorders, schizophrenia, substance use disorder, and simple criminal behavior. Oppositional defiant disorder is distinct because it lacks serious rights violations. The DSM-5 also offers an Alternative Model that defines ASPD through impairments in personality functioning plus traits of antagonism and disinhibition, optionally with a "with psychopathic features" specifier marked by low fear and high boldness.

## ASPD and psychopathy

ASPD overlaps with but is not identical to psychopathy. Both share antisocial behavior, shallow affect, and lack of remorse, but psychopathy adds a strong affective-interpersonal style: fearlessness, grandiosity, and cold charm measured by tools like the PCL-R. ASPD captures two to three times more prisoners than psychopathy does, but PCL-R scores are better at predicting reoffending and violence.

## Course, prognosis, and comorbidities

Symptoms usually peak between the late teens and early thirties, then often fade after 40, a pattern called "antisocial burnout." Remission is uncommon: only 27–31% of patients improve significantly, and core traits like manipulativeness often persist. Comorbidities are the rule: people with ASPD are about 13 times more likely to develop a substance use disorder, 4 times more likely to have major depression, and have elevated rates of anxiety disorders (up to 50%), PTSD, and suicide.

## Treatment

ASPD is among the hardest personality disorders to treat because patients often lack motivation, may feign remorse, and tend to drop out. No medication is approved specifically for it, though antipsychotics, antidepressants, and mood stabilizers can help with aggression, impulsivity, or co-occurring conditions. Psychotherapies showing some promise include multisystemic therapy for adolescents, schema therapy, and mentalization-based treatment for probationers. Structured, supervised residential or forensic programs work better than outpatient therapy, and treatment is often delivered only when mandated by courts.
