# Evolutionary educational psychology

Evolutionary educational psychology studies how evolved folk knowledge and its accompanying biases shape academic learning in settings the brain was not built for, including schools and modern workplaces. Its starting point is that natural selection shaped the human mind to handle recurring survival problems, not literacy, algebra, or industrial skills.

## Three folk domains

Natural selection produced minds motivated to gain the resources that historically covaried with survival and reproduction. Those resources fall into three categories, each matched by a domain-specific cognitive system:

- **Social** → folk psychology: reading intentions, alliances, status, and deception.
- **Biological** → folk biology: categorising living things and predicting animal behaviour.
- **Physical** → folk physics: understanding objects, forces, space, and immediate cause and effect.

Attentional, perceptual, and cognitive systems, including inferential and attributional biases, evolved to process information in these three domains. Each system handles restricted classes of input. The folk-biology system does not reason about molecules, and the folk-physics system does not reason about electrons.

## Constrained primary systems, fluid novelty-handling

Although the brain is plastic, primary folk abilities are inherently constrained. The information patterns they handle were consistent across generations and within individual lifetimes during human evolution, so the systems are tuned to ancient regularities rather than modern ones.

A second evolved capacity copes with variation. Systems that mentally simulate possible future social, ecological, or climatic conditions, and allow rehearsal of responses, support general fluid intelligence (gF), including everyday reasoning and problem solving. Fluid intelligence is the mechanism for handling novelty outside the three folk domains.

An evolved openness to experience, related to a personality dimension, biases individuals to seek novelty across the developmental period and lifespan.

## Children are primed for folk learning

Children are biologically motivated to engage in social play and exploration of the environment and objects, the activities that recreate the ecologies of human evolution. The accompanying experiences interact with skeletal folk systems and flesh them out, adapting them to the local social group and ecology. This learning is self-initiated and largely implicit.

## Cultural accumulation outpaces folk systems

Knowledge that helps a group control its ecology or social dynamics is retained across generations as cultural artefacts, such as books and tools, and as learning traditions, such as apprenticeships. Across generations the store grows, while folk systems inherited by children remain largely unchanged. A widening gap opens between what children are built to learn and what societies need them to know.

For most people, the breadth and complexity of this accumulated secondary knowledge exceeds any built-in bias to learn evolutionarily novel material.

## Schools close the gap

Schools emerge in societies where accumulated knowledge has outstripped what folk learning alone can transmit. Their function is to organise children's activities so they acquire biologically secondary competencies that close the gap between folk knowledge and the occupational and social demands of the society.

Secondary competencies are built from primary folk systems and fluid intelligence, but assembled by explicit instruction, practice, and cultural scaffolding rather than self-directed exploration. The more a secondary competency diverges from its supporting primary system, the more explicit instruction it requires.

## Creation versus learning

Whether the traits that enable creation of secondary knowledge overlap with those that enable learning of it is a separate question. Research on creative and productive individuals suggests that the full constellation of cognitive, motivational, and personality traits underlying discovery is rare and not reproducible on a large scale. Education aimed at having every child rediscover knowledge is therefore unrealistic; most learning is transmission of discoveries made by a few.

## Motivational conflict

Children's inherent bias is to seek activities that adapt folk systems to local conditions, mainly social play and physical exploration. Schooling demands activities that yield secondary learning. This motivational conflict is a recurring source of difficulty in formal education.
