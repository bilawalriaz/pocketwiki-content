# Intelligence

Intelligence is the capacity for learning, reasoning, problem-solving, and adapting to new situations. It is one of the most studied and most contested constructs in psychology and cognitive science, because researchers disagree on how to define it, how to measure it, and whether it exists outside the human mind.

## A contested definition

The word "intelligence" descends from Latin roots meaning "to comprehend or perceive." In medieval scholastic philosophy it was tied to the "active intellect," an inner faculty of understanding. Early modern philosophers such as Francis Bacon and Thomas Hobbes preferred the term "understanding" instead, and "intelligence" faded from English philosophy for centuries before being revived by modern psychology.

The most widely cited working definition comes from a 1995 consensus report by the Board of Scientific Affairs of the American Psychological Association, *Intelligence: Knowns and Unknowns*. It says individuals differ in their ability to understand complex ideas, adapt to the environment, learn from experience, and engage in various forms of reasoning. No single definition commands universal agreement, and a person's intellectual performance can shift across occasions and domains.

## Measuring human intelligence

Human intelligence draws on memory, learning, concept formation, reasoning, pattern recognition, and problem-solving. It is distinct from learning itself, which is the act of retaining information; intelligence is broader, encompassing how that information is used.

Attempts to quantify it produced Intelligence Quotient (IQ) tests, first developed in the early twentieth century to screen children for intellectual disability. An influential framework is Charles Spearman's General Intelligence, or the *g factor*, a single construct summarising the consistent positive correlations between scores on different cognitive tasks. Most psychologists agree IQ captures some aspects of intelligence, especially those linked to academic success, but many doubt it as a complete measure.

A related distinction separates **fluid intelligence** (reasoning and problem-solving in novel situations) from **crystallized intelligence** (accumulated knowledge and skills).

IQ is also debated for what it might explain beyond the individual. Heritability, the share of population variation in IQ attributable to genetic differences, is itself contested, though the scientific consensus holds that genetics do not explain average IQ differences between racial groups.

## Multiple forms of intelligence

Because IQ leaves much of everyday ability unmeasured, psychologists have proposed additional forms:

- **Emotional intelligence** — accurately perceiving, conveying, and managing emotions in oneself and others.
- **Social intelligence** — reading social cues and motivations and navigating interpersonal situations.
- **Moral intelligence** — understanding right from wrong and acting accordingly.
- **Book smart versus street smart** — a folk contrast between academic knowledge and practical experience.

## Animal intelligence

Researchers study non-human animals both to map their cognitive abilities and to test theories of intelligence more broadly. Work focuses on problem-solving, numerical and verbal reasoning, and innovation. A *g* factor appears in other species too: in primates it explains about 47% of individual variance on cognitive measures, and in mice between 55% and 60%, figures that bracket the 40–50% typically found in humans. Notable subjects include chimpanzees, bonobos (including Kanzi, a bonobo known for language use), other great apes, dolphins, elephants, and, to some extent, parrots, rats, and ravens. Cephalopods, especially octopuses, are valued as a comparative case because their evolutionary lineage is distant from vertebrates.

## Plant and artificial intelligence

Whether plants are intelligent is controversial. Proponents note that plants sense their environments, learn from experience, and communicate chemically. Critics reply that intelligence requires persistent memories and learning rather than automated responses, and that calling those responses "intelligence" dilutes the term beyond usefulness.

Artificial intelligence is defined by Kaplan and Haenlein as "a system's ability to correctly interpret external data, to learn from such data, and to use those learnings to achieve specific goals and tasks through flexible adaptation." AI is usually evaluated on specific benchmarks, such as a chess rating or a language exam, which measure narrow skill rather than general ability. Some scholars instead define intelligence as **optimization power**, the capacity to steer outcomes toward preferred goals, or as an agent's ability to achieve goals across a wide range of environments. Today's AI systems exceed humans on many narrow tasks but lack general intelligence, the capacity to learn a huge range of tasks the way humans do.
