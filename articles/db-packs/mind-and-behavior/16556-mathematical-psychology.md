# Mathematical psychology

Mathematical psychology builds formal models of perception, cognition, learning, and motor behaviour, then tests those models against quantitative data, usually reaction times or choice patterns, collected under controlled conditions. Unlike psychometrics, which compares people on stable traits, mathematical psychology models the internal processes of an "average" individual performing a task. Work groups into five areas: learning and memory, perception and psychophysics, choice and decision making, language and thinking, and measurement and scaling.

## Origins in measurement and sensation

The earliest mathematical laws came from 19th-century Germany. Ernst Weber (1795–1878) formulated Weber's law, the first mathematical law of the mind: the just-noticeable difference between two stimuli is a constant proportion of stimulus magnitude. Gustav Fechner (1801–1887) extended this into Fechner's law, expressing perceived intensity as a logarithmic function of physical intensity. Stevens' power law later replaced the logarithm with a power function and remains a standard psychophysical scaling model.

Two parallel 19th-century lines made reaction-time research possible. Astronomers noticed systematic differences between observers timing stellar transits; Friedrich Bessel (1782–1846) formalised these as personal equations to correct observations. Hermann von Helmholtz independently measured reaction times to estimate nerve conduction speed and proposed the Young–Helmholtz theory of colour vision. F. C. Donders and J. J. de Jaager then recognised that reaction time could quantify the duration of elementary mental operations, founding mental chronometry. In England, Francis Galton pioneered the study of individual differences using the bivariate normal distribution and measurement-error models, laying groundwork for psychometrics and intelligence testing (continued by James McKeen Cattell and Alfred Binet).

## 20th-century institutional formation

The German and English traditions converged in the United States through factor analysis (Charles Spearman), analysis of variance, and structural equation modelling (Sewall Wright). The Psychometric Society was founded in 1935 and *Psychometrika* launched in 1936.

Modern mathematical psychology emerged from a wartime meeting of experimental psychologists, mathematicians, engineers, physicists, and economists working on signal processing, information theory, linear systems and filter theory, game theory, stochastic processes, and mathematical logic. Behaviourism had dominated American psychology but largely avoided formal theorising; the wartime influx changed that.

Two papers in *Psychological Review* established mathematical learning theory: Bush and Mosteller's 1951 paper introduced the linear-operator approach, and Estes's 1950 paper launched stimulus sampling theory. By the end of the 1950s the number of mathematical psychologists had grown more than tenfold, clustered at Indiana, Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Stanford. Three 1950s advances were Luce's theory of choice, Tanner and Swets's signal detection theory for human stimulus detection, and Miller's information-processing approach. The field consolidated around the *Journal of Mathematical Psychology*, whose first issue appeared in January 1964 following an initiative by R. C. Atkinson, R. R. Bush, W. K. Estes, R. D. Luce, and P. Suppes.

## Core models by domain

In sensation and perception, psychophysics relies on the Weber–Fechner law, Ekman's law, Stevens' power law, and Thurstone's law of comparative judgment. Signal detection theory, borrowed from radar engineering, models discrimination as a tradeoff between hits and false alarms governed by a noisy internal response plus a decision criterion. Choice and simple-decision research uses accumulator, diffusion, race, random-walk, renewal, and cascade models, plus decision field theory and the sequential probability ratio test. Memory scanning and visual search use push-down stack and serial exhaustive search models. Learning draws on linear operator models and stochastic learning theory, and classical conditioning on the Rescorla–Wagner rule. Measurement theory rests on the theory of conjoint measurement. Cognitive modelling is dominated by production-rule systems such as ACT-R and by connectionist (neural network) architectures.

Across these domains the field has shifted from deterministic functional equations, analogous to classical physics, toward stochastic models that treat behaviour as inherently noisy.

## Applications and developmental extension

Mathematical models now appear in clinical, social, educational, and music psychology. Developmental psychology uses three frameworks: symbolic models, connectionist neural networks, and dynamical systems models. Nonlinear dynamical systems have been applied to affect, second-language acquisition, and locomotion, showing how interacting components produce emergent developmental change.

Key outlets are the *Journal of Mathematical Psychology*, *Computational Brain & Behavior*, and the *British Journal of Mathematical and Statistical Psychology*. The field meets annually through the Society for Mathematical Psychology (US), the European Mathematical Psychology Group, and the Australasian Mathematical Psychology conference.
