# Brain injury

A brain injury is the destruction or degeneration of brain cells from any cause: external trauma, stroke, infection, tumour, poison, metabolic failure, or inherited disease. The most common form is traumatic brain injury (TBI), caused by a physical blow or jolt to the head. Because the brain controls movement, sensation, language, emotion, and identity, damage anywhere in it produces effects that match the function of the lost region.

## How injuries are classified

Two axes run through almost everything else. By **location**, a focal injury damages one area (a bruise, clot, or stroke); a diffuse injury damages many areas at once, usually from shearing forces that tear nerve fibres across the brain. Severe focal injuries kill more often (about 40% mortality) than severe diffuse ones (about 25%), but diffuse injuries more often leave survivors with long-term cognitive and behavioural deficits.

By **timing**, a primary injury is the mechanical damage at the moment of impact. A secondary injury is the cascade that follows: swelling, reduced blood flow, seizures, or rising pressure inside the rigid skull. Emergency treatment mainly tries to prevent secondary injury.

Severity is rated on the Glasgow Coma Scale, which scores eye opening, speech, and movement from 3 to 15: 13–15 is mild, 9–12 moderate, 3–8 severe. A mild TBI from a blow to the head is a concussion.

## Mechanisms of damage

| Cause | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Blunt impact** (falls, crashes, assault) | Brain hits the skull at the impact site (coup) and the opposite side (contrecoup); contrecoup is usually worse. |
| **Rotational acceleration** | White and grey matter have different densities, so shearing forces tear axons: diffuse axonal injury. |
| **Penetrating trauma** | The object breaches skull and dura; the most lethal TBI form, with 70–90% dying before hospital. |
| **Blast** | Primary shock wave, secondary shrapnel, tertiary impact from being thrown, and quaternary injuries such as burns. |
| **Haemorrhagic stroke** | A vessel ruptures; blood compresses tissue and raises pressure. |
| **Ischaemic stroke** | A clot blocks a vessel; the tissue it supplied dies. |
| **Tumour** | Primary or metastatic, compressing or infiltrating tissue; most brain tumours are metastatic. |
| **Infection** | Bacteria, viruses, or fungi inflame brain tissue: encephalitis, meningitis, or rhinocerebral mucormycosis. |
| **Neurodegeneration** | Alzheimer's (amyloid plaques and tangles), Parkinson's (loss of dopamine neurons from Lewy bodies), Huntington's (autosomal dominant, onset 30–50, causing chorea and dementia), multiple sclerosis (autoimmune loss of myelin), ALS (loss of motor neurons). |
| **Toxins and deficiency** | Mercury damages the cerebellum and visual cortex; lead disrupts development and can cause fatal encephalopathy; thiamine deficiency causes Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome. |
| **Medical treatment** | Chemotherapy and radiation can damage neural stem cells and myelin ("chemo brain"). |

## Symptoms and complications

Mild injury causes headache, confusion, dizziness, fatigue, sleep disturbance, mood change, and slowed thinking. Moderate and severe injury adds prolonged loss of consciousness, worsening headache, vomiting, seizures, pupil dilation, weakness, poor coordination, and slurred speech. Young children cannot describe these feelings, so changes in eating, sleep, mood, and interests are the early clues.

Long-term effects depend on the region injured. A small lesion in a critical spot can wipe out one function; large lesions cause broader symptoms.

- Wernicke's area (posterior superior temporal gyrus): word retrieval problems and poor comprehension.
- Broca's area (posterior inferior frontal gyrus): halting, non-fluent speech, omitted grammar, alexia and agraphia.
- Fusiform gyrus: prosopagnosia, the inability to recognise faces.
- Amygdala: loss of fear responses and altered emotional processing elsewhere.
- Visual cortex V1: blindsight; V4: cerebral colour blindness; MT/V5: loss of motion perception.
- Parietal lobes: agnosia and amorphosynthesis (neglect of one side of the body).

Brain damage raises the long-term risk of seizures, Parkinson's disease, dementia, depression, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia, and can shift personality toward higher neuroticism, lower extraversion, and lower conscientiousness.

The most extreme outcomes are coma, persistent vegetative state (wakefulness without awareness), and brain death, which requires absent brain activity 24 hours after resuscitation, absent brainstem reflexes, and inability to breathe when carbon dioxide rises.

## Diagnosis and treatment

CT shows bleeding, skull fracture, and swelling quickly; MRI is more sensitive for diffuse axonal injury, brainstem lesions, and small injuries. Management begins with securing oxygen and stable blood pressure. A ventricular catheter monitors intracranial pressure, and patients with a Glasgow score below 8 are intubated. Corticosteroids and mannitol reduce swelling. Epidural and subdural haematomas that raise pressure must be surgically evacuated, sometimes by decompressive craniectomy. Penetrating wounds are debrided and the dura repaired.

Recovery depends on neuroplasticity, the brain's ability to form new connections, relearn skills, and shift functions to undamaged regions. Neuroregeneration is much weaker in the brain and spinal cord than in peripheral nerves. Older adults recover more slowly because of reduced physiological reserve. In children, an injury to a still-developing region such as the frontal lobe may not show its full effect until the executive functions that depend on it fail to mature in late adolescence.

## Epidemiology

In the United States, an average of about 1.69 million people per year were hospitalised for TBI between 2002 and 2006, and roughly 51,500 died. About 70–95% of TBIs are mild. Falls, traffic accidents, impacts from objects, and assault are the leading civilian causes. TBIs are more common and more severe in males. Children aged 0–4, adolescents 15–19, and adults over 75 carry the highest rates. Between 56% and 72% of people hospitalised with a TBI test positive for alcohol, and a person who has had one TBI is 2.3 to 3 times more likely to sustain a second.

## How the modern understanding was built

The Edwin Smith Papyrus of 16th-century BC Egypt first linked brain damage to specific symptoms: neck stiffness from meningeal injury, paralysis from cerebral lesions, and aphasia from temporal damage. The Greek physicians Hippocrates, Celsus, and Galen noted that head trauma often caused loss of consciousness.

The localisation of mental functions to particular brain regions was established in the 19th century. In 1848, Phineas Gage survived an iron rod through his frontal lobe, becoming the most famous case of personality change after brain damage. In the 1860s, Paul Broca tied speech production to the left frontal lobe through patients who could utter only a few words. In 1874, Carl Wernicke identified a different left-hemisphere region responsible for language comprehension, proving that the brain divides language labour between areas. In 1928, Harrison Martland described "punch drunk" boxers, and in 1940 the term chronic traumatic encephalopathy replaced it, recognising that head trauma can cause delayed neurodegeneration.
