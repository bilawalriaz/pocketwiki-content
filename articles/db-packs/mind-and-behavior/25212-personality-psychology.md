# Personality psychology

Personality psychology studies the stable patterns of thought, feeling, motivation, and behaviour that distinguish one person from another. "Personality" derives from the Latin *persona*, meaning mask, and refers to the organised, characteristic ways an individual engages with the world across situations.

Two complementary research approaches structure the field. The **nomothetic** approach seeks general laws applicable to many people, such as the trait of extraversion. The **idiographic** approach tries to understand the unique configuration of a single individual. Modern personality science blends both, using large-sample statistics to discover general traits and detailed case methods to capture personal meaning.

## Philosophical questions every theory must answer

Personality theorists divide on five recurring questions:

- **Freedom versus determinism**: are people agents of their own behaviour, or driven by unconscious, environmental, or biological forces?
- **Heredity versus environment**: are traits mainly genetic or mainly learned? Twin studies show most traits are jointly determined, with identical twins showing roughly 0.50 trait correlations versus about 0.20 for fraternal twins.
- **Uniqueness versus universality**: do people share core psychological principles, or is each person qualitatively distinct?
- **Active versus reactive**: do humans initiate behaviour, or do stimuli shape them?
- **Optimistic versus pessimistic**: can people deliberately change their own personalities, or are they largely fixed?

## Major theoretical traditions

**Dispositional (trait) theory** measures where people fall on stable dimensions. The most empirically supported model is the **Big Five**: openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism. These traits show substantial heritability and emerge consistently through factor analysis of self-report data. Type theories sort people into discrete categories. Carl Jung's *Psychological Types* introduced perceiving (sensing vs intuition) and judging (thinking vs feeling) functions, later expanded by Isabel Briggs Myers and Katharine Cook Briggs into the **Myers–Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI)**, which has been characterised as pseudoscientific. Other typologies include Friedman and Rosenman's Type A–B model, linked to coronary risk, the Enneagram of nine types, and Holland's RIASEC vocational types.

**Psychoanalytic theory** begins with Sigmund Freud, who divided personality into the **id** (pleasure-seeking), **ego** (reality-oriented mediator), and **superego** (moral conscience). Freud argued that adult personality is largely fixed by age five through five psychosexual stages, with unconscious conflicts driving behaviour. Alfred Adler added that birth order shapes personality.

**Behaviourist theories** explain personality as learned habits. B. F. Skinner's three-term contingency, antecedent–behaviour–consequence, frames operant conditioning. Ivan Pavlov's classical conditioning established how stimulus associations form.

**Social-cognitive theories** add mental processes to behaviourism. Albert Bandura showed that children imitate aggression they observe, a process called observational learning. George Kelly's personal-construct theory treats people as naive scientists who use personal frameworks to anticipate events.

**Humanistic theories** emphasise free will and growth. Carl Rogers centred therapy on the client's subjective frame of reference. Abraham Maslow studied self-actualising people, identifying traits such as problem-centred awareness, acceptance, and peak experiences of unity and meaning.

**Biopsychological theories** trace personality to the brain and genome. The famous 1848 case of Phineas Gage, whose personality reportedly changed after an iron rod destroyed his frontal cortex, though modern analysis suggests the changes were exaggerated, launched the field. Modern personality neuroscience uses fMRI as the dominant imaging technique. Twin studies show that genetic variation sets a range of possibilities, while the environment selects which genes are expressed.

**Evolutionary theory** interprets traits as adaptations. Natural selection favours dispositions supporting survival and reproduction, such as conscientiousness, sociability, emotional stability, and dominance. Extreme variants of these traits may shade continuously into personality disorders.

## Assessment methods

Personality is measured two ways. **Projective tests**, such as the Rorschach inkblot test and the Thematic Apperception Test, assume unconscious motives and use ambiguous stimuli to elicit projection. **Objective tests** use self-report questionnaires and generally show higher validity and reliability, though critics cite the Forer effect to warn that vague descriptions can feel personally accurate. Major objective tools include the MBTI and Big-Five inventories.
