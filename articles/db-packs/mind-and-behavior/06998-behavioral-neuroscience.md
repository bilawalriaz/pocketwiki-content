# Behavioral neuroscience

Behavioral neuroscience, also called biological psychology, biopsychology, or psychobiology, is the branch of neuroscience that studies the biological substrates of behavior. It applies biology to the physiological, genetic, and developmental mechanisms of behavior in humans and other animals, derived from the older label physiological psychology. A defining assumption is that the nervous system is the organ of behavior, so any biological variable that influences behavior must act through it. The field allies closely with comparative psychology, ethology, evolutionary biology, and neurobiology, and methodologically with neuropsychology, which studies humans with nervous system damage rather than experimentally induced manipulation.

## Roots and historical shape

The modern discipline grew from eighteenth- and nineteenth-century physiology, which first established that living organisms could yield reliable experimental data through the work of Claude Bernard, Charles Bell, and William Harvey. William James argued in *The Principles of Psychology* (1890) that psychology must be grounded in biology. Knight Dunlap introduced the term *psychobiology* in *An Outline of Psychobiology* (1914), defining the field as the interconnection of mental and physiological functions, a framing that still describes behavioral neuroscience today.

Two enduring debates shaped the field. The mind-body problem, framed by monism (one substance) and dualism (mind and body as separate), was first argued by Plato, who placed mental life in the brain, and Aristotle, who treated the brain as a coolant for the heart's emotions. The localization-versus-equipotentiality debate asked whether specific mental functions reside in specific brain regions; Wilder Penfield's mapping of the cortex in epileptic patients, and the case of Phineas Gage, whose personality changed after an iron rod damaged his frontal lobe, established that brain regions have specialized behavioral roles.

In 1983, the APA split the *Journal of Comparative and Physiological Psychology* into *Behavioral Neuroscience* and the *Journal of Comparative Psychology*, formalizing the field as the contemporary descendant of physiological psychology. The Society for Neuroscience held its first conference in 1971, consolidating neuroanatomy, neurochemistry, neuroendocrinology, and related fields into one interdisciplinary discipline.

## What counts as a behavioral neuroscience experiment

A study qualifies when either the independent variable is biological or a dependent variable is biological. The nervous system is altered, or an aspect of it is measured, in relation to behavior.

Decreasing neural function. Lesions destroy or disable a brain region to observe behavioral change, placed using stereotactic coordinates (3D reference points for targeting internal brain structures). Methods include surgical removal, electrolytic shock, neurotoxin infusion (excitotoxic lesions), cooling, anesthetics such as tetrodotoxin, transcranial magnetic stimulation (which disrupts cortical activity transiently), chemogenetic tools like DREADDs (Designer Receptors Exclusively Activated by Designer Drugs) that silence neurons in response to synthetic ligands, and optogenetic inhibition (using light to switch neurons off) with light-activated proteins such as halorhodopsins.

Enhancing neural function. Electrical stimulation, pharmacological antagonists (for example, the NMDA receptor blocker AP5, which blocks long-term potentiation, the long-lasting strengthening of synapses implicated in learning, and fear conditioning in rodents), excitatory DREADDs, and optogenetic excitation using channelrhodopsin-2 all increase neural activity. Optogenetics operates on a millisecond timescale, allowing precise temporal control.

Measuring neural activity. Methods include single-unit and multielectrode recording, calcium imaging (using fluorescent dyes that brighten when neurons fire and calcium floods in) with fluorescent dyes or genetically encoded indicators, voltage-sensitive dyes, fMRI (which detects blood-flow changes tied to regional activity), PET (which uses radioisotopes to reveal chemical and pathological differences), EEG and event-related potentials, electrocorticography (invasive but higher resolution), and magnetoencephalography.

Genetic and behavioral quantification. QTL (quantitative trait locus) mapping, selective breeding, and knockout or transgenic mice link genes to behavior. Markerless pose estimation (DeepLabCut, Anipose) uses computer vision to track animal movement frame-by-frame without physical markers, enabling machine-learning classification of behaviors such as walking, grooming, or courtship. Computational models of neurons and circuits generate and test predictions.

## Trade-offs of each method

Permanent lesions risk damaging surrounding tissue, so observed changes may reflect collateral injury rather than the targeted region. Most genetic manipulations are permanent, though newer inducible systems allow gene expression to be switched on or off by diet. Pharmacological blockade is temporary, clearing as the drug is metabolized.

## Topic areas and contributions

Most experimental work uses non-human animals, particularly rats, mice, and monkeys, based on the assumption that biological and behavioral similarities permit cross-species extrapolation. Core topics include sensation and perception, motivated behavior (hunger, thirst, sex), movement control, learning and memory, sleep and biological rhythms, and emotion. Increasingly precise non-invasive human methods extend the field into language, reasoning, decision making, and consciousness.

Behavioral neuroscience has contributed to the understanding of Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, Alzheimer's disease, clinical depression, schizophrenia, autism, anxiety, drug addiction, and alcoholism, supplying animal models that reveal underlying mechanisms and therapeutic targets. Solomon Carter Fuller (1872–1953), the first African American psychiatrist, worked alongside Alois Alzheimer from 1904 and published the first review of Alzheimer's disease in 1912; the American Psychiatric Association later established the Solomon Carter Fuller Award in his honor.

Among Nobel Prizewinners, Eric Kandel (2000) worked out the molecular basis of memory, while John O'Keefe, May-Britt Moser, and Edvard Moser (2014) discovered the brain's place and grid cell systems for spatial navigation, illustrating how behavior traced to specific neural circuits can yield general principles of brain function.
