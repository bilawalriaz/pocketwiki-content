# Evolutionary psychology of parenting

Parenting strategies are shaped by evolved psychological adaptations that balance the certainty of genetic relatedness against the costs of care. Because women gestate and give birth, maternity is certain. Because men cannot gestate, they face **paternal uncertainty**—the inability to be sure a child is genetically theirs. This asymmetry in minimum reproductive investment drives much of the sex-specific parenting behaviour observed across cultures.

**Maternal role and certainty of relatedness.** Parental investment theory holds that mothers are predisposed to invest heavily in offspring because the genetic link is assured. Polyandry (a woman taking multiple husbands) is rare in most societies for the same reason: a single acknowledged father reduces paternity disputes and secures his help with the child. The biological and social machinery supporting this began when ancestors developed upright bipedal locomotion. Carrying infants in the arms rather than on the back promoted closer mother–infant contact, while easier defence of one partner on the ground favoured monogamous pair-bonds. Natural selection then reinforced neural circuitry for attachment, because offspring of bonded parents were likelier to survive.

Women are attuned to infants' facial expressions, especially negative emotion, which supports secure attachment. The **tend-and-befriend** hypothesis proposes that under stress, mothers nurture offspring and recruit social networks that provide collective protection. Maternal grandmothers invest more than paternal grandmothers, and maternal aunts more than uncles, because each additional biological link raises the chance that effort aids a non-relative. Menopause is interpreted as an adaptation that lets older women redirect care toward existing descendants rather than producing new ones.

**Paternal role and uncertainty.** Because any child a man fathers may not be his, selection favours lower baseline investment than in mothers, a tendency the source terms **paternal insecurity**. Human fathers nevertheless invest heavily compared with most mammals, including close primates. They allocate more resources to children who resemble or smell like them, behaviours absent in species where paternity is unknowable. When a child is born, men's testosterone falls, reducing the likelihood of abuse, infidelity, and divorce during the vulnerable early period—hormonal changes that protect the father's genetic stake.

Paternal investment is also social. Long-term monogamy benefits children's development, which raises the chance those children will reproduce, propagating the father's genes. Evolutionary accounts treat abandonment as a conditional strategy shaped by environment and experience, not an inevitable outcome. After divorce, men not granted custody often disengage; a new partner can redirect effort toward new offspring, optimising gene spread at the expense of earlier children.

**Influence on offspring's mating.** Trivers' parental investment theory predicts that the parent who invests more controls mate choice more tightly, to avoid squandering that effort on a partner unlikely to reproduce. Historically, parents of young women wielded this power as resource providers or through intimidation, enhancing their own **inclusive fitness** (an individual's own reproductive success plus that of relatives carrying shared genes).

David Buss links a father's presence in early childhood to the sexual strategy a child later adopts. An absent or unreliable father correlates with insecure attachment, earlier sexual maturation, earlier sexual activity, and frequent partner switching—an **r-strategy** favouring many low-investment offspring. A present father correlates with secure attachment, delayed sexual activity, and fewer, heavily invested children—a **K-strategy** emphasising quality over quantity. The source claims modern conditions select against these inherited patterns, though it offers no evidence for the claim.
