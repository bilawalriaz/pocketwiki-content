# Imprinting (psychology)

Imprinting is a rapid form of learning that occurs during a narrow window of early life, then shapes behaviour for the long term. Unlike ordinary learning, which can happen at almost any age, imprinting depends on a developmental phase: an animal must encounter the relevant stimulus while its nervous system is still primed to absorb it. Once that window closes, the preference is largely fixed. The term originally described how young animals internalise the features of a caregiver, predator, or food, and has since been extended to mate choice, emotional memory, and even software preferences.

## Filial imprinting

In filial imprinting, young animals attach socially to a caregiver, usually the mother. It is most visible in nidifugous (precocial) birds such as ducklings and goslings, which can walk and follow shortly after hatching. The young bird accepts as its parent the first suitable moving stimulus it sees, whether or not it is alive or its own species.

Konrad Lorenz popularised the phenomenon in the twentieth century using greylag geese. He identified a critical period of about 13 to 16 hours after hatching: goslings exposed during that window imprinted on his wading boots and followed him everywhere. They would also imprint on a box carried around a model train. The same trick trains captive-hatched migratory birds to follow a route: handlers in distinctive clothing, sometimes flying ultralights or hang-gliders, lead Canada geese, Siberian cranes, or eagles along a path the birds then follow on their own. The Italian pilot Angelo d'Arrigo hatched chicks under the wing of his glider and flew with them across the Sahara and over the Mediterranean. Some raptor species, including the peregrine falcon, can imprint on a location such as a cliff or bridge, biasing where they later choose to breed. Filial imprinting has also been used experimentally to show that domestic chicks only days old can perform rudimentary counting: when imprinting on sets of plastic balls, they reliably move toward the larger hidden group.

## Sexual imprinting

In sexual imprinting, a young animal learns the traits of an acceptable mate. Male zebra finches tend to prefer females who resemble the bird that reared them. If that bird is a human, the adult may direct sexual behaviour toward people: London Zoo's female giant panda Chi Chi refused the male panda An An and presented herself to a zookeeper, and the human-imprinted kākāpō Sirocco attempted to mate with zoologist Mark Carwardine. In falconry, human-imprinted birds called "imprints" require artificial insemination, since the male will only copulate with a human handler and the female will only accept what feels like another bird. Sexual imprinting on inanimate objects is one proposed explanation for human fetishism, such as shoe fetishism, though the link from specific childhood objects remains speculative.

## Limbic imprinting

Limbic imprinting concerns humans and other higher mammals. Their long-term memory of emotional significance sits in the limbic system, deep in the brain. Early experiences, including prenatal ones, partly irreversibly remodel the neural structure of this system, producing an "emotional map" that combines stored impressions with deep beliefs and values and quietly steers conscious thought and behaviour. Sigmund Freud anticipated this idea in his 1895 *Project for a Scientific Psychology*, describing how the brain stores experiences as a "permanent change after an event," a function he later assigned to the superego. Traumatic imprints stored in the limbic system are hard to alter, because therapeutic change requires bringing their causal background into conscious awareness, a process associated with activity in the frontal lobe.

## Westermarck effect

The Westermarck effect is a reverse form: two people who live in close domestic proximity during the first years of either one's life become desensitised to later sexual attraction to each other. Finnish anthropologist Edvard Westermarck described it in 1891. Evidence comes from Israeli kibbutzim, where children raised together in age-based peer groups almost never married each other later, and from the Chinese *shim-pua* custom and biological families. The effect operates mainly in the first six years of life. When early proximity is absent, as with siblings separated at birth, the opposite can occur: strong adult sexual attraction between genetic relatives, sometimes called genetic sexual attraction and seen in some cousin couples. The effect is thought to have evolved to suppress inbreeding. Westermarck used it to claim that the human incest taboo emerges naturally from innate attitudes, directly contradicting Freud, who held that an innate family lust must be controlled by cultural taboos.

## Baby duck syndrome

In human–computer interaction, baby duck syndrome applies the same metaphor: users tend to imprint on the first system they learn and then judge every later system by how closely it resembles that original, which can make it harder to switch tools or train students on new interfaces.
