# Evolutionary developmental psychology

Evolutionary developmental psychology (EDP) is a research paradigm that applies natural selection to understand how human behavior and cognition develop. It studies the genetic, environmental, and epigenetic (gene–environment interaction) mechanisms that build social and cognitive competencies, and how these competencies are tuned to local conditions.

EDP's central claim distinguishes it from mainstream evolutionary psychology (EP): natural selection can favor individual differences, not just species-typical traits, through "adaptive developmental plasticity." From this view, human development follows alternative life-history strategies shaped by environmental variability, rather than one fixed pattern.

## How EDP differs from evolutionary psychology

EDP emerged from EP but diverges in three ways. First, EDP centers on adaptations of ontogeny (the developmental trajectory of an individual from conception onward) rather than adaptations of adulthood. Second, it treats proximate "how" questions (mechanisms of development) as equal in importance to ultimate "why" questions (selective reasons), drawing on Tinbergen's four categories of explanation. Third, it embraces a developmental systems approach: function at the genetic level affects organization at adjacent levels, and gene–environment interactions are continuous and bidirectional.

Mainstream EP figures including Tooby, Cosmides, Barrett, and Lieberman have rebutted this framing. They argue EP does take development seriously, that developmental systems theory produces no testable predictions, and that both fields share the goal of uncovering species-typical cognitive architecture.

## Core theoretical assumptions

EDP rests on claims drawn from Bjorklund, Blasi, and Ellis's chapter in *The Handbook of Evolutionary Psychology*:

- All evolutionarily influenced adult traits develop, so ontogeny must be studied, not just adult function.
- Traits emerge through continuous, bidirectional gene–environment interactions over time.
- Infants and children are prepared by natural selection to process some information more readily than others.
- Development is constrained by genetic, environmental, and cultural factors.
- Children show high developmental plasticity and adaptive sensitivity to context.
- An extended childhood exists to learn the complexities of human social communities.
- Many childhood traits are preparations for adulthood (deferred adaptations).
- Other childhood traits serve an adaptive function only at that life stage (ontogenetic adaptations), with no adult purpose.

## Three kinds of developmental adaptation

**Deferred adaptations** are childhood traits selected because they prepare individuals for adult roles. Sex differences in children's play illustrate this: boys engage in more rough-and-tumble play, while girls engage in more parenting-themed fantasy play, cross-culturally, plausibly rehearsing adult roles that recurred across evolutionary history.

**Ontogenetic adaptations** serve a function only during a specific developmental window and are then discarded. The placenta is a physiological example, useful before birth and shed afterward. David F. Bjorklund argues that infant imitation of facial gestures, which peaks and then shifts function, may be a psychological ontogenetic adaptation.

**Conditional adaptations** detect environmental cues and redirect developmental pathways, producing the alternative life-history strategies EDP emphasizes. A child raised in a harsh or unpredictable environment, with exposure to violence, unstable caregiving, or frequent disruption, is more likely to develop a "fast" life-history strategy: earlier sexual maturation, unstable pair bonds, greater impulsivity, and reduced cooperation, with current reproduction prioritized over future reproduction.

## Related research areas

**Childhood and cultural learning.** The social brain hypothesis holds that increasing group size drove the evolution of human intelligence, and that this in turn extended the juvenile period. Humans expanded childhood and adolescence as distinct stages to support sophisticated social learning. Over-imitation, copying even apparently unnecessary actions of a model, emerges by about age three in humans and appears largely absent in chimpanzees. It is thought to support high-fidelity cultural transmission, a prerequisite for cumulative culture.

**Cooperation and prosociality.** Michael Tomasello argues that cooperation and prosociality are evolved human traits, citing helping behavior in 18- to 24-month-old infants. Studies comparing preschool children with both human-raised and semi-free-ranging chimpanzees find that children consistently display greater prosociality.

**Life history strategies.** A life-history strategy is a chain of resource-allocation trade-offs, including how much effort goes into growth versus reproduction. Biologists have long described between-species variation along a fast–slow continuum, and EDP researchers apply the same framework within species, treating early environmental inputs as key shapers of where an individual falls on that continuum.
