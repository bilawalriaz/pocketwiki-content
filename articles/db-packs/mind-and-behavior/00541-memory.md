# Memory

Memory is the mind's system for encoding, storing, and retrieving information so that past experience shapes future behavior. Without it, language, relationships, and personal identity could not develop. The process is fallible: pain, inattention, brain damage (especially to the hippocampus), and decay can corrupt it, and remembering itself can rewrite what is remembered.

## Architecture: From Sensation to Long-Term Storage

Incoming information passes through three stages. **Sensory memory** briefly holds raw perceptual input — visual (iconic), auditory (echoic), or touch (haptic) — for less than a second. Sperling's 1963 partial-report experiments showed iconic memory can hold roughly 12 items, but they decay within hundreds of milliseconds. Attention transfers some input into **short-term memory**, which retains a small number of items for seconds to a minute. Miller's 1956 "Magical Number 7±2" set the classical capacity, though modern estimates cluster around 4–5 items. Grouping items into meaningful units — chunking — expands effective capacity. Short-term memory relies mainly on acoustic coding: Conrad showed in 1964 that acoustically similar letters are easily confused.

Through **consolidation**, selected short-term traces become **long-term memory**, which has vast, potentially lifelong capacity and stores information semantically. Baddeley demonstrated this in 1966: delayed recall suffers more for semantically similar words. Consolidation requires the hippocampus, essential for forming new long-term memories but not the final storage site. At the molecular level it depends on gene transcription, new protein synthesis, and epigenetic changes such as DNA methylation and demethylation.

## Working Memory: An Active Workspace

In 1974, Baddeley and Hitch replaced the unitary short-term store with **working memory**, a system for actively maintaining and manipulating information. It contains a **central executive** that directs attention, a **phonological loop** that rehearses verbal material, a **visuospatial sketchpad** for visual and spatial content, and — added by Baddeley in 2000 — an **episodic buffer** that binds information from different domains into coherent episodes and links to long-term memory. Working memory explains why combining a verbal and a visual task is easier than combining two verbal ones, and it underpins reasoning and learning.

## Two Major Divisions

Long-term memory splits into **declarative (explicit)** memory, which supports conscious recall, and **non-declarative (implicit)** memory, which does not. Declarative memory divides into **semantic memory** (context-free facts) and **episodic memory** (personally experienced events with a specific what, when, and where). Non-declarative memory includes **procedural memory** for skills such as riding a bike, dependent on the cerebellum and basal ganglia, and **priming**, in which prior exposure biases later responses without awareness.

A separate axis distinguishes **retrospective memory** (recalling the past) from **prospective memory** (remembering to do something in the future), introduced by Meacham in 1975. Future intentions often rely on external cues such as lists or alarms.

## Neural Basis

A small set of structures carries distinct roles. The **hippocampus** binds spatial and declarative information and drives consolidation. The **amygdala** strengthens emotionally significant memories; damaging it abolishes that enhancement. The **prefrontal cortex** supports working memory, source memory, temporal order, and prospective memory. The **cerebellum and basal ganglia** handle procedural learning. Synapses change strength through **long-term potentiation (LTP)** and **long-term depression**, the cellular basis of learning. Final **engrams** are distributed across neocortical circuits.

Consolidation has two phases: fast **synaptic consolidation** in the medial temporal lobe, and slower **systems consolidation** that gradually frees memories from the hippocampus over months or years. When a memory is retrieved it becomes temporarily labile and must be **reconsolidated** — a window in which it can be altered, and in which traumatic memories might potentially be weakened in therapy. Patient H.M. (Henry Molaison) appeared to confirm the hippocampus's role when most of it was removed to treat epilepsy, but post-mortem revealed residual tissue, complicating that conclusion.

## Why Memory Fails and Can Be Distorted

Memory is reconstructive, not reproductive. Loftus and Palmer showed in 1974 that post-event wording changes recall: people asked how fast cars were going when they "smashed" estimated higher speeds and were more likely to falsely remember broken glass than those asked about cars that "hit." This **misinformation effect** shows that suggestive questions can implant detail.

**Interference** weakens memory in two directions: new learning can overwrite older memories (retroactive), or earlier learning can impair new encoding (proactive). Stress hormones such as glucocorticoids impair hippocampal encoding — a cold-pressor stress reduced free recall by about 30% — and chronic stress can damage CA1 neurons through glutamate excitotoxicity. **Sleep** consolidates memories through hippocampal–neocortical replay during slow-wave sleep, so deprivation weakens retention and can produce false memories.

## Influencing Factors and Improvement

Spacing study sessions exploits the **spacing effect**, producing better retention than cramming. The **method of loci** ties items to familiar spatial locations; the **Zeigarnik effect** shows uncompleted tasks are recalled better than completed ones. Regular sleep, exercise, intellectual activity, and stress reduction support memory. Aging impairs frontal-dependent tasks and sharply reduces memory-related gene expression after 70, partly through oxidative DNA damage. Disorders such as Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, and **Korsakoff's syndrome** (from thiamine deficiency) degrade memory through different routes.

## Memory Beyond Animals

Plants have no neurons yet show memory-like behavior. A **Venus flytrap** closes only when two trigger hairs are stimulated within about 30 seconds; the plant sums cytoplasmic calcium across events until an action-potential threshold is reached. **Mimosa pudica** habituates to being dropped after roughly 60 trials and remembers the habituation a month later, though shaking restores its response, ruling out simple fatigue. Whether these processes are homologous to animal memory or merely convergent physiology remains contested.
