# Memory B cell

A memory B cell (MBC) is a long-lived B lymphocyte that remembers a specific antigen so that, on re-exposure, the body mounts a faster and stronger antibody response than it did during the first infection.

## What it is and where it comes from

B lymphocytes patrol the body looking for antigens. When a naïve follicular B cell first meets its matching antigen in a secondary lymphoid organ such as the spleen or a lymph node, it binds the antigen, internalises and breaks it down, and displays the resulting peptides on class II major histocompatibility complexes (MHCII) on its surface. A follicular helper T cell (T_FH) that recognises the same peptide binds, delivers CD40L and cytokines, and the B cell proliferates at the border between the B-cell follicle and the T-cell zone.

The activated B cell then enters a germinal centre inside the follicle. There it undergoes somatic hypermutation, random point mutations in the genes that encode the antigen-binding part of its B cell receptor (BCR). B cells whose mutated receptors bind the antigen more tightly receive survival signals from T_FH cells; those whose receptors bind weakly, or which become self-reactive, die by apoptosis. This selection, called affinity maturation, steadily raises the average fit between receptor and antigen. Alongside it, class switch recombination changes the immunoglobulin heavy-chain gene so the cell switches from IgM to IgG, IgA, or IgE while keeping antigen specificity.

Inside the germinal centre, B cells follow one of three fates, dictated by which transcription factors they express. Most become short-lived plasma cells, antibody factories that pour out immunoglobulin but soon die. Some continue to proliferate and mutate. The remainder become memory B cells, exit the germinal centre, and relocate to the periphery, including the blood, Peyer's patches in the gut, and the tonsils, sites where future antigen is most likely to appear. The exact signal that pushes a germinal-centre B cell toward the memory fate is still unresolved. One hypothesis is that cells with somewhat lower antigen affinity default to memory while the highest-affinity cells default to plasma cells.

A separate route skips the germinal centre. IgM⁺ memory B cells that have not class-switched exist, and a minor subset called B1 cells in the peritoneal cavity can become memory-like cells without any T-cell help, producing IgM against bacterial polysaccharides.

## Primary versus secondary response

During the primary response, plasma cells clear the first wave of infection, while the few B cells that become memory B cells persist in a quiescent, non-dividing state. On re-exposure to the same antigen, memory B cells recognise it through their BCRs, which carry the same specificity and usually a higher affinity than the parent B cell's, proliferate rapidly, and differentiate into antibody-secreting plasma cells. This conversion is far faster than differentiation by naïve B cells, which is the basis for an accelerated and robust secondary immune response. Memory B cells that do not immediately become plasma cells can re-enter germinal centres for further class switching and somatic hypermutation.

## Subsets and surface markers

Memory B cells are most often identified by CD27, although CD27-negative memory-like cells appear in HIV, lupus, and rheumatoid arthritis, where they tend to be exhausted or autoreactive. Several surface proteins divide memory B cells into functional subsets whose marker profile predicts their fate on reactivation.

| Marker profile | Behaviour on reactivation |
|---|---|
| IgM⁺ (tonsil, Peyer's patch, lymph node) | More likely to re-enter germinal centre for further mutation |
| IgG⁺ (expresses CD80, PD-L2, CD73) | More likely to differentiate into plasma cells |
| IgE⁺ | Very rare in healthy people; IgE-bearing B cells usually become plasma cells |
| IgD-only | Very rare; concentrated in tonsils |

T-bet⁺ memory B cells are a further subset tied to class switching and to responses against intracellular bacteria and viruses. CCR6, a chemokine receptor, marks B cells destined for the memory fate and is required for memory B cells to migrate toward CCL20 and into sites of antigen re-exposure. CCR6-deficient mice mount normal primary responses but fail on rechallenge, which shows that CCR6 is essential for recalling memory B cells to their cognate antigen.

## Lifespan

Memory B cells can persist for decades without continued antigen exposure or T-cell contact, which is why immunity to once-circulating pathogens such as smallpox can still be measured in vaccinated people many years later. The mechanism is thought to involve elevated expression of anti-apoptosis genes. In mice the actual lifetime is shorter than was once assumed: tracking bromodeoxyuridine-labelled cells in a transgenic model gave a half-life of roughly 8–10 weeks and a stable count for 8–20 weeks after immunisation, and memory B cells lived at least nine times as long as naïve follicular B cells. The relative longevity holds even when absolute numbers turn over more quickly than in humans.

## Vaccination and clinical relevance

Vaccines work by exploiting this compartment: a non-pathogenic antigen triggers a primary response that generates long-lived plasma cells and memory B cells, so that real infection is met with an accelerated secondary response and is cleared before it causes disease. Booster shots refresh and broaden the pool. Higher counts of memory B cells in the blood have been linked to a lower relapse risk in non-small-cell lung cancer patients treated with perioperative chemoimmunotherapy, and the smallpox-specific memory B cell pool has been detectable for decades after DryVax vaccination in the absence of restimulation.
