# Body image (neuroscience)

Body image is the conscious mental picture a person forms of their own body, layered with feelings, attitudes, and beliefs about how it looks. Austrian neuropsychiatrist Paul Schilder coined the term in his 1935 book *The Image and Appearance of the Human Body* and defined it as "the picture of our own body we form in our mind, that is to say the way in which the body appears to ourselves." Current research uses the phrase for a conscious representation of the body that mixes perceptual input, emotion, and cognition.

Body image must be separated from a related idea to make sense. The body schema is the unconscious, sensorimotor representation of the body that the nervous system uses to guide action: reaching, walking, grasping without watching the hand. Keizer and colleagues (2013) describe it as "an unconscious, sensorimotor, representation of the body that is invoked in action." Body image is what a person can think and talk about; body schema is what they use to move. Recent work on multisensory integration of body signals suggests the split is convenient but oversimplified, and probably no longer useful as a sharp scientific boundary.

Body image is shaped by what the brain does with sensory information. The extrastriate body area (EBA) and fusiform body area (FBA) in the visual cortex underpin the perception of bodies, including one's own. The insula contributes the sense of self and self-awareness that colours how a person feels and thinks about their body, feeding the affective and cognitive layers of body image. When these systems misfire, the conscious picture of the body drifts from reality.

That drift shows up in clinical conditions. Body image disturbance is common in eating disorders and in several psychiatric and neurological disorders, including anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge eating disorder, psychotic spectrum disorders, body dysmorphic disorder, and body integrity dysphoria. In anorexia nervosa researchers separate the disturbance into three components. Perception, served by the EBA and FBA, is impaired, which is why patients misjudge their own shape and size. Affect covers how satisfied or dissatisfied someone feels, and cognition covers what they believe about their body; both draw on the insula. The allocentric lock theory adds a memory point: patients with eating disorders seem unable to update an older stored view of their body, so even when their body changes their conscious report stays anchored to the prior picture. In body dysmorphic disorder attention locks onto perceived flaws invisible to others, and in body integrity dysphoria a person feels that a healthy limb no longer belongs to them, often linked to abnormal structure across several body-perception regions.

Social input pushes body image in the same direction. Upward social comparisons, especially to idealized media images, lower body satisfaction, and women are more prone to these appearance comparisons than men. The identity disruption model proposes a route: early adverse experiences such as abuse or neglect fragment identity development, leaving low self-concept clarity and a stronger habit of comparing the self to others, which then feeds body dissatisfaction.

Researchers measure the gap between how heavy people feel and how heavy they actually are using the FAI (feel-minus-actual inconsistency) index, introduced by Zaccagni in 2014. Scores run from −3 to +3; negative values mean people underestimate their weight, positive values mean they overestimate it, and zero means an accurate read. In the original study women scored positive and men scored negative, and later work adapted the index to fat mass measured by bioelectrical impedance analysis, producing the FAI-FAT version.
