# Information behavior

Information behavior is the subfield of information science that studies how people search for, encounter, and use information, and the social and psychological reasons they do so. Thomas D. Wilson coined the term in 1981 and later defined the field as "the totality of human behavior in relation to sources and channels of information." He chose behavior over "information needs" because needs are not directly observable, but behavior is.

Researchers treat information behavior as an umbrella concept with nested layers:

- **Information need**: the recognition of a gap in one's knowledge. Understanding it requires knowing why the person seeks information, what purpose it will serve, and how they intend to use it.
- **Information-seeking behavior**: purposive action to satisfy a goal, such as querying, browsing, or asking another person.
- **Information-searching behavior**: the narrower interaction between the seeker and an information system.
- **Information use**: what the seeker does with what they find, including passively adopting the knowledge.

Seeking is best analysed as a session embedded in context, not as a single query. It can be explicit (solving a known problem) or exploratory (learning what a service contains).

Two cross-cutting concepts recur. **Information poverty**, introduced by Elfreda Chatman in 1987, is not a shortage of facts but a worldview in which outsiders' information is distrusted because it does not match lived experience. The related idea of a **small world** describes a group with shared concerns and agreed-upon sources whose members will not look outside unless something forces them to.

## Metatheories

A metatheory is a set of assumptions guiding how a phenomenon is theorised. Information behavior draws on three:

- **Cognitivist**: rooted in psychology; treats thought as the driver of seeking and use. Nigel Ford's 2004 model focuses on the intellectual processes of searching.
- **Constructionist**: rooted in social constructionism; holds that behavior is shaped by social discourse. Michel Foucault is the most cited thinker. One constructionist study examined a knitting group at a public library, using a collectivist theory that denies universal patterns and instead asks how discourse communities jointly construct their information needs and sources.
- **Constructivist**: individuals actively construct their understanding within their social world, but reality is built in the mind rather than handed down by society. Kuhlthau's information search process is a constructivist model.

## Selected theories and models

- **Navigators and explorers**. Experienced searchers (navigators) revisit domains and follow tight, sequential paths; novices (explorers) visit many domains and branch often.
- **Sensemaking** (Brenda Dervin). People construct meaning across gaps in their understanding, using their own language to interpret ambiguous situations.
- **Anomalous state of knowledge** (Nicholas Belkin). People search because they recognise their own knowledge is flawed or incomplete; the anomaly itself drives the search.
- **Information foraging** (Card, Chi, Pirolli). Borrowed from animal foraging; seekers follow "information scents" like links, summaries, and images to estimate proximity to a target. It explains *why* people search as they do, not the mechanics.
- **Everyday life information seeking** (Reijo Savolainen, 1995). Built on way of life and life domain, modelling information seeking as a routine part of daily practice.
- **McKenzie's two-dimensional model**. Four modes (active seeking, active scanning, non-directed monitoring, by proxy) cross two phases (connecting and interacting), accounting for both deliberate searching and serendipitous encounters.

Carol Kuhlthau's **Information Search Process** (1991) is a six-stage model developed from high school students and later extended to workplaces. Each stage tracks four aspects: thoughts, feelings, actions, and strategies.

| Stage | Task | Dominant feeling |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Task initiation | Apprehension, uncertainty |
| 2 | Topic selection | Confusion, brief elation after choosing |
| 3 | Pre-focus exploration | Doubt, frustration with inconsistent material |
| 4 | Focus formulation | Optimism, sense of insight |
| 5 | Information collection | Confidence, interest |
| 6 | Search closure | Relief, satisfaction, or disappointment |

A replicated finding is that uncertainty is not a side effect but a driver: many searches are abandoned when uncertainty peaks, and relief signals closure.

David Ellis identified eight recurring activities in academic information seeking: starting, chaining, browsing, differentiating, monitoring, extracting, verifying, and ending. Choo, Detlor, and Turnbull later mapped these onto web searching.

A persistent methodological concern is that the field imports theories from sociology, psychology, communication, organisational behavior, and computer science, so individual studies are hard to compare, and review work now restricts itself to specific contexts.
