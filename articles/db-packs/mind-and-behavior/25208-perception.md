# Perception

Perception is the brain's active process of turning raw sensory signals into a usable interpretation of the world. The same retinal image can produce different percepts depending on expectation, attention, learning, and memory. The Necker cube and Rubin vase are textbook examples: one stimulus, two valid interpretations.

## The basic pipeline

Perception begins with a **distal stimulus** (the object itself), which sends energy to the senses. Sensory receptors convert that energy into neural activity through **transduction**, producing a **proximal stimulus** (raw neural firing). The brain then reconstructs a mental image, the **percept**. A ringing telephone illustrates this: the phone's ring is distal; sound waves hitting the ear and the resulting neural firing are proximal; "someone is calling" is the percept. Different kinds of sensation (sight, sound, taste, touch, smell) are called **sensory modalities**.

## Why perception is active

Sensory information is almost always incomplete. A coin tilted toward you projects an ellipse onto the retina, yet you see a circle. The brain compensates through **perceptual constancy**: shape, color, size, brightness, and roughness stay stable across different inputs. **Phonemic restoration** shows this in hearing: Warren replaced a phoneme with a cough and listeners could not tell which sound was missing.

**Gestalt grouping laws** reveal how the brain organizes fragments into wholes. Close items are grouped (proximity), similar items are grouped (similarity), gaps are filled (closure), overlapping forms are split along smooth paths (good continuation), and things moving together are seen as one object (common fate).

**Multistable perception** is when one stimulus yields competing percepts that flip over time, again visible in the Necker cube. **Contrast effects** bend judgements: lukewarm water feels hot after cold water; a normal-sized person looks small next to a basketball player.

## How the senses work

Vision begins when light strikes the retina's rods, cones, and intrinsically photosensitive ganglion cells, encoding intensity, color, and position. About 15 types of information travel via the optic nerve.

Hearing detects pressure waves between 20 Hz and 20,000 Hz. The outer ear collects sound, the middle ear matches impedance, and the inner ear transduces vibrations into neural signals routed to the temporal lobe. Real-world sound from multiple sources arrives superimposed, so the brain must separate them.

Haptic perception combines touch and proprioception to identify objects by feel. Taste uses roughly 10,000 taste buds detecting sweet, salty, sour, bitter, and umami. Smell works when odor molecules bind to one of about 347 receptor types in the nose, then shapes flavor perception.

Other modalities include vestibular sense (balance), proprioception (body position), interoception (internal states like hunger and pain), chronoception (time perception, governed by the suprachiasmatic nucleus for circadian rhythm and dopamine pathways for interval timing), and the sense of agency, the feeling of having caused one's actions. Libet's experiments showed neurological signs of a decision appear roughly half a second before the conscious feeling of choosing.

## Theoretical camps

**Direct perception** (J. J. Gibson) argues the environment already contains all the information an animal needs. Light carries stable **invariants**, lawful projections of object properties, so the visual system can pick them up directly. Perception and action form a closed loop.

**Constructivist theories** hold the opposite: sensation is too impoverished to specify the world, so the brain enriches it with hypotheses, memory, and expectations. Perception is unconscious science, always testing guesses. Modern versions like **predictive coding** model the brain as continuously predicting sensory input and updating only when predictions fail.

**Evolutionary psychology** treats perception as an adaptation for guiding action, not for producing knowledge. Depth perception evolved for collision avoidance, not for measuring distance. Sensory systems are metabolically expensive, so they evolve only when they boost fitness, explaining modularity: damage to a specific area can destroy face recognition alone (**prosopagnosia**), implying a dedicated face-reading module.

**Anne Treisman's feature integration theory** describes a two-stage process: a fast, unconscious **preattentive** stage breaks objects into separate features (color, shape, motion), then focused attention binds them into unified percepts. Without attention, features miscombine into **illusory conjunctions**.

Experience refines perception through practice. **Perceptual sets** (expectations) bias interpretation: told to expect animal words, subjects read "sael" as "seal"; told to expect boat words, they read "sail." Motivation stacks on top: subjects preferred to perceive the letter B when paired with a pleasant drink, and the number 13 when paired with a bad one. Top-down beliefs and bottom-up data feed back on each other continuously.

The science of perception began with **Weber's law** (the just-noticeable difference is proportional to stimulus intensity) and **Fechner's law** (quantifying perceived intensity from physical intensity), the oldest quantitative laws in psychology. From them grew psychophysics, sensory neuroscience, and the Gestalt school. Philosophers from Descartes to Berkeley to Kant have asked whether perceived qualities belong to the world or only to the mind, a question that remains open and shapes how experiments are designed.
