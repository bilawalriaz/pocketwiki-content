# Cognitive architecture

A cognitive architecture is a formal theory of the fixed structures that produce intelligent behaviour, together with a computational instantiation of that theory. It plays two roles: a hypothesis about how natural minds are organised, and a blueprint for artificial intelligent agents. Allen Newell framed the project in 1990 as a "unified theory of cognition" that explains intelligent behaviour across diverse, complex environments.

## Origins

The intellectual roots reach into early memory and learning research. Herbert A. Simon pointed to Ed Feigenbaum's 1960 thesis EPAM as a possible "architecture for cognition" because it committed to a specific design for both human memory and human learning. John R. Anderson began studying human memory in the early 1970s, and his 1973 thesis with Gordon H. Bower set out a theory of human associative memory. Anderson extended this work into a broader theory of long-term memory and thinking, initially called ACT, and in 1983 published *The Architecture of Cognition*. The software built to run such theories came to be called cognitive architectures too, and the label now serves as a design template for intelligent agents, most often modelled on human cognition but applicable to other intelligent systems. Cognitive architectures form a subset of general agent architectures, with "architecture" signalling concern for structural as well as behavioural properties.

## How they differ

Architectures are usually classified along several design axes. The processing style can be symbolic (rule-based, with explicit representations, as in ACT-R or Soar), subsymbolic or connectionist (relying on emergent properties of simple processing units, as in neural networks), or hybrid (combining both, as in CLARION). The control structure can be centralised around a single processor-like core, or decentralised and distributed, the route popularised as parallel distributed processing and connectionism from the mid-1980s. The overall organisation can be holistic or modular. A deeper philosophical divide separates traditional top-down AI, where the programmer's intelligence is encoded explicitly, from biologically-inspired bottom-up approaches, where complex behaviour emerges from many simple rules or nodes interacting.

## Notable examples

A few examples illustrate the range. ACT-R, developed at Carnegie Mellon University by John R. Anderson, is a rule-based symbolic architecture grounded in cognitive psychology. Soar, developed under Allen Newell and John Laird at Carnegie Mellon and the University of Michigan, instantiates Newell's unified theory of cognition. CMAC, proposed by James Albus in 1975, is a cerebellar neural model used in robotic control and reinforcement learning. DeepMind combined Q-learning with a recurrent neural network to play video games in a human-like way, and built a neural network that accesses an external memory in the manner of a Turing machine. Spaun, by Chris Eliasmith at the University of Waterloo, is a large network of spiking neurons that coordinates cognitive tasks through "semantic pointers" in a compressed neural vector space. Sparse distributed memory, proposed by Pentti Kanerva at NASA Ames, stores large patterns and retrieves them by partial match. These systems range from symbolic software frameworks to large-scale neural simulations of brain function.
