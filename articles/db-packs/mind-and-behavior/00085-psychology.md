# Psychology

Psychology is the scientific study of mind and behavior, including thoughts, feelings, motives, and unconscious processes. It crosses the natural and social sciences and aims to improve mental health, education, work, and social life. The word comes from Greek *psyche* (soul) and *-logia* (study); the Latin form *psychiologia* first appeared around 1510–1520, coined by Croatian humanist Marko Marulić.

## Mind Versus Behavior

For most of its history, psychology has disagreed about what to study and how. William James defined it in 1890 as "the science of mental life." John B. Watson countered in 1913 that psychology should be a purely objective natural science whose goal is "the prediction and control of behavior." This split between inner experience and outward action shaped nearly every later debate, including the cognitive revolution of the 1950s, which rejected the behaviorist ban on mental explanations. Most working psychologists now combine both.

## Becoming a Laboratory Science

For centuries, mental phenomena were treated as philosophical or spiritual questions. Two advances turned psychology into a laboratory science. Gustav Fechner's 1860 *Elements of Psychophysics* showed that perception can be measured; his Weber–Fechner law states that perceived intensity varies with the logarithm of stimulus strength. Wilhelm Wundt established the psychological laboratory at Leipzig University in 1879, the conventional founding of experimental psychology, aiming to break mental processes into basic components.

The discipline spread through Wundt's students: G. Stanley Hall at Johns Hopkins, Hugo Münsterberg at Harvard, Yujiro Motora at Tokyo, and Narendra Nath Sen Gupta at Calcutta in 1905. Edward Titchener advanced *structuralism*, classifying mental elements through introspection, while James, John Dewey, and Harvey Carr advanced *functionalism*, the Darwinian view that behavior is explained by its usefulness.

## Major Schools

Behaviorism holds that most behavior is learned through conditioning: *classical conditioning*, in which Pavlov paired a neutral stimulus with a meaningful one until the neutral one triggered a response, and *operant conditioning*, in which Skinner showed that behavior changes through reinforcement and punishment. Noam Chomsky's 1959 critique argued that conditioning cannot explain how children acquire language, helping trigger the cognitive revolution.

Psychoanalysis, originating with Freud in the 1890s, analyzes the unconscious mind. The philosopher of science Karl Popper argued it is not *falsifiable*, meaning no possible observation could prove it wrong, which most scientists consider a serious flaw. Humanistic psychology, emerging in the 1950s, stresses free will and self-actualization; Abraham Maslow proposed a hierarchy of needs and Carl Rogers developed client-centered therapy. Cognitive psychology treats the mind as an information processor handling perception, attention, memory, and problem solving. Evolutionary psychology sees psychological traits as adaptations to ancestral problems. Neuropsychology links brain function to behavior. Social psychology studies how social environments shape thoughts, feelings, and behavior.

## Research Methods

Experiments with random assignment allow strong causal claims; quasi-experiments study pre-existing groups when true experiments are impossible. Surveys measure attitudes and traits. Observational studies come in cross-sectional (one snapshot) and longitudinal (over time) forms. Brain measurement includes EEG, which records voltage changes across the scalp, and functional neuroimaging, which tracks blood flow. Twin and adoption studies in behavioral genetics separate genetic from environmental influence. Qualitative methods, including interviews and participant observation, capture meaning that numbers miss. Standard statistical tools include the Pearson correlation coefficient, analysis of variance, and structural equation modeling.

## Tests, Therapy, and Application

Psychological testing dates back to Chinese civil service exams around 2200 BCE. Modern mental testing began with Francis Galton; Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon introduced the concept of *mental age* in 1905–1911, and Lewis Terman converted it into the *intelligence quotient* (IQ). Eugenicists misused these tests to justify forced sterilization, upheld by the U.S. Supreme Court in *Buck v. Bell* (1927).

Clinical psychology began in the U.S. with Lightner Witmer's clinic in 1896. The dominant modern treatment is cognitive-behavioral therapy, developed in the 1970s by Albert Ellis and Aaron Beck, which combines behaviorist and cognitive techniques. The Hawthorne effect, found in industrial-organizational research, shows that people change their behavior when they know they are being observed, a basic limitation for any study.

## Contemporary Problems

A replication crisis has emerged: systematic efforts have found that as many as two-thirds of highly publicized psychology findings failed to replicate. Samples suffer from a WEIRD bias, meaning they come from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic societies, which hold only 12% of the world's population, so most findings may not generalize. Jacob Cohen warned in 1994 that psychologists routinely mistake statistical significance for practical importance. Thomas Kuhn's 1962 analysis suggested psychology lacks a single overarching theory, putting it in a pre-paradigm state compared to physics or chemistry.

## Ethics and People

The APA's five ethical principles are beneficence and nonmaleficence, fidelity and responsibility, integrity, justice, and respect for people's rights and dignity. The Nuremberg Code and the Declaration of Helsinki set international research-ethics standards after Nazi abuses. University ethics committees must approve research on humans or animals; Harry Harlow's isolation experiments on rhesus monkeys drew lasting condemnation.

Women now make up about 78% of U.S. psychology undergraduates and 71% of graduate students, though pay and senior-position gaps remain. Martha Bernal became the first Latina psychology PhD in 1962, and Marigold Linton the first Native American woman in 1969. In 1954, Kenneth and Mamie Phipps Clark's doll studies helped show that racial segregation damaged Black children's self-image, contributing to *Brown v. Board of Education*.
