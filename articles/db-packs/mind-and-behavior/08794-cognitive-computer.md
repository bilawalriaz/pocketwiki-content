# Cognitive computer

A cognitive computer is a machine that hardwires artificial intelligence and learning algorithms into an integrated circuit, producing hardware that mimics the brain instead of executing a program step by step. The field is also called neuromorphic engineering, and its chips are called neuromorphic or cognitive chips.

The goal is to escape the **von Neumann bottleneck**: in a conventional computer, a processor and a separate memory exchange data over a single bus, wasting time and energy. A cognitive computer folds memory, computation, and communication into the same circuitry, so each small unit can both store and process locally, like a biological neuron.

The defining building block is a **spiking neuron model**: a small circuit that accumulates inputs, fires a brief electrical pulse when a threshold is crossed, then resets. Thousands to millions of these are wired into a **network-on-chip**, communicating through event-driven, asynchronous packet switching rather than a global clock. The result is highly parallel and dramatically more energy-efficient than running the same neural network on a general-purpose CPU or GPU.

## Major chips and systems

**IBM TrueNorth (2014).** A CMOS (complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor) neuromorphic chip with 4,096 neurosynaptic cores, 256 simulated neurons per core, and 256 synapses per neuron, totalling just over one million neurons and 268 million programmable synapses, on 5.4 billion transistors. Each core handles memory, compute, and communication. IBM reported power use of about 70 milliwatts and a power density roughly 1/10,000 that of conventional microprocessors. The design uses a simplified integrate-and-fire neuron model, no global clock, and unary number representation (numbers encoded as counts of pulses rather than binary digits). A DARPA SyNAPSE board linked 16 TrueNorth chips. The lack of backward compatibility with existing toolchains risked vendor lock-in.

**IBM NorthPole (2023).** A proof-of-concept inference chip that intertwines compute and on-chip memory to eliminate the von Neumann bottleneck. It has 256 cores, 224 MB of RAM, runs at 25 to 425 MHz, and performs 2,048 operations per core per cycle at 8-bit precision (8,192 at 2-bit). Compared with conventional GPUs on the same 12-nm fabrication process, it runs ResNet-50 or Yolo-v4 image recognition about 22 times faster while using 25 times less energy and 5 times less space, and is roughly 4,000 times faster than TrueNorth. It cannot yet run models as large as GPT-4 because of memory and accuracy limits.

**Intel Loihi (2017) and Loihi 2 / Pohoiki Beach (2021).** Loihi uses Intel's 14-nm process and packs 128 clusters of 1,024 artificial neurons each, totalling 131,072 simulated neurons and about 130 million synapses, far short of the human brain's roughly 800 trillion synapses and also behind TrueNorth. Intel claims Loihi is roughly 1,000 times more energy-efficient than general-purpose systems for training neural networks, and can perform both training and inference on the same silicon without a cloud connection. Loihi is available to over 40 academic research groups as a USB-form-factor board. Research demonstrations include simultaneous localization and mapping (Rutgers, 2019) and hazardous-materials recognition by smell (Intel and Cornell, 2020). Loihi 2, branded Pohoiki Beach, has 64 cores with faster speeds, higher inter-chip bandwidth, and improved programmability.

**Intel Hala Point (2024).** A six-rack-unit chassis holding 1,152 Loihi 2 processors on the Intel 3 process node, with more than 2,300 embedded x86 processors for housekeeping. It supports up to 1.15 billion neurons and 128 billion synapses across 140,544 neuromorphic cores, consumes 2,600 watts, and delivers up to 20 petaops, with efficiency above 15 trillion 8-bit operations per second per watt on conventional deep neural networks. Intel calls it the world's largest neuromorphic system, claims it can process its 1.15 billion neurons about 20 times faster than a human brain, and asserts it can support large language models, though the LLM claim is unverified.

**Darwin3 (2023).** A neuromorphic chip from Zhejiang University and Alibaba with a novel instruction set and on-chip learning, whose reported neuron and synapse figures exceed Hala Point's.

**SpiNNaker (University of Manchester).** A massively parallel manycore supercomputer architecture designed to simulate spiking neural networks at large scale, rather than a neuromorphic chip in the hardwired sense.

## Limits and tradeoffs

The rise of large language models built on Transformer technology has made energy efficiency a central concern, because training and running these models on GPUs consumes enormous power. Cognitive computers use far less energy, but their spiking neuron models and learning rules such as spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP, a rule that strengthens or weakens a synapse based on the relative timing of spikes) still cannot match the accuracy of **backpropagation** (the standard gradient-based learning algorithm used to train deep neural networks). As a result, methods that translate trained artificial neural networks into spiking equivalents, including quantization-aware training (QAT), post-training quantization (PQT), and progressive quantization, are becoming popular despite their own limitations.

Critics have also pointed to the gap between demonstrations and deployment: IBM's Watson, a room-scale cognitive system, drew early fanfare but struggled in practice, with the Oncology Expert Advisor cancer project abandoned in 2016 after it failed to use patient data and could not reliably interpret doctors' notes and patient histories.
