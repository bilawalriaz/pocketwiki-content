# Avoidant personality disorder

Avoidant personality disorder (AvPD), also called anxious personality disorder, is a chronic personality disorder defined by social inhibition, persistent feelings of inadequacy, and hypersensitivity to rejection or negative evaluation. The central feature is motivational conflict: people with AvPD want close relationships but avoid them because they expect ridicule, humiliation, or being found inferior. They describe themselves as uneasy, lonely, and unwanted, and they tend to choose isolated jobs that require little contact with others.

A core distinction separates AvPD from *social anxiety disorder* (SAD). Social anxiety disorder is the fear of social circumstances; AvPD is better described as an aversion to intimacy in relationships. The two share criteria, and some researchers view them as different conceptualisations of the same problem, with AvPD as the more severe, pervasive form. People with AvPD show more depression and greater functional impairment than those with generalised social phobia alone, though not poorer social skills. They also meet the general criteria for a personality disorder, which SAD does not require.

AvPD sits in Cluster C ("anxious or fearful") of the DSM-5 categorical system, alongside dependent and obsessive–compulsive personality disorders. A diagnosis requires a pervasive pattern of social inhibition, feelings of inadequacy, and hypersensitivity to negative evaluation, with at least four of seven diagnostic criteria met. The DSM-5 Alternative Model (Section III) reframes AvPD around impairment in personality functioning, especially in identity and self-direction, plus at least three of four traits, one of which must be anxiousness. The other three are withdrawal, anhedonia, and intimacy avoidance, collectively capturing fearfulness combined with emotional inhibition.

ICD-11 takes a different path. It drops named personality-disorder categories in favour of a severity rating (mild, moderate, severe) based on impact on relationships, sense of self, and risk, with trait qualifiers as optional specifiers. AvPD maps onto the ICD-11 trait domains Negative Affectivity and Detachment, with an additional link to Anankastia (excessive caution aimed at avoiding negative outcomes).

Causes are not fully established. Research links AvPD to a heritable temperament of behavioural inhibition (being shy, fearful, and withdrawn in new situations), to childhood emotional neglect, and to repeated peer rejection or bullying. The relative weight of genetics, temperament, and environment is unclear, and AvPD can also appear without any history of abuse or neglect. Some researchers add that high sensory-processing sensitivity combined with adverse childhood experiences may raise risk.

The disorder is chronic. Symptoms often appear in childhood, can intensify through adolescence and early adulthood, and tend to persist, though treatment and time can produce partial improvement. Functional consequences include lower educational attainment, greater reliance on disability benefits, and lower rates of cohabitation and marriage. Population prevalence is reported around 1.5–2.5%, while clinical samples range widely (one outpatient study found 14.7%). Occurrence in men and women is roughly equal, with some evidence of higher prevalence in women.

Comorbidities are common. Social anxiety disorder is especially frequent, with panic disorder and social phobia up to eight or nine times more likely in those with AvPD. Depressive disorders, substance use disorders, and bipolar disorder also occur at elevated rates. Co-occurring personality disorders most often include borderline, dependent, paranoid, schizoid, and schizotypal types.

Differential diagnosis relies on the motive behind shared features. *Dependent* PD differs in its drive toward closeness versus AvPD's withdrawal. *Schizoid* and *schizotypal* PDs differ because people with AvPD want relationships and feel lonely from isolation, while those with schizoid patterns are passively disinterested. From *paranoid* PD, the reluctance to confide in AvPD stems from fear of embarrassment, not fear of malicious intent.

Treatment research on AvPD is limited, and much of it is extrapolated from social anxiety disorder. Approaches include social-skills training, cognitive therapy, graded exposure to increase social contact, and group therapy for practising interaction. Pharmacotherapy has not been directly studied for AvPD but is used for comorbid conditions, especially social anxiety disorder. A central practical challenge is the therapeutic alliance itself: people with AvPD often avoid sessions if they begin to distrust the therapist or fear rejection, so building and keeping trust is a prerequisite for the work of challenging the exaggerated negative self-beliefs the disorder maintains. Millon's subtypes (phobic, conflicted, hypersensitive, and self-deserting avoidant) describe blended clinical pictures, and Alden and Capreol's cold-avoidant and exploitable-avoidant subtypes capture variants with restricted positive emotion or difficulty resisting coercion.
