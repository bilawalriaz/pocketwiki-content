# Animal cognition

Animal cognition is the study of the mental capacities of non-human animals: how they perceive, attend to, remember, categorize, navigate, reason, count, and in some cases appear to be aware of themselves and others. The field grew from comparative psychology and is shaped by ethology, behavioral ecology, and evolutionary psychology; the alternative name *cognitive ethology* captures this mix.

## How the field took its shape

For most of the 20th century, animal behavior was explained without invoking mental states. Thorndike's puzzle-box experiments and Pavlov's conditioned reflexes showed that apparently intelligent acts could be explained by simple associations. Watson and later Skinner's radical behaviorism pushed this further, treating even "private events" like mental images as unnecessary. During this period, careful laboratory work distinguished classical (Pavlovian) conditioning from instrumental (operant) conditioning.

Two threads kept cognition alive. Köhler showed chimpanzees solving novel problems in sudden, "aha" rearrangements rather than trial-and-error, suggesting insight. Tolman proposed internal *cognitive maps* that animals use to find their way. Around 1960, the cognitive revolution made it acceptable to infer unobservable processes; Hebb's view that "mind" names processes that control complex behavior became foundational. Animals are now treated as "goal-seeking agents that acquire, store, retrieve, and internally process information at many levels of cognitive complexity."

A standing caution comes from Morgan's Canon: do not interpret an action as evidence of a higher psychological process if a simpler one explains it equally well. This guards against anthropomorphism, reading human feelings into animal behavior, which remains a main methodological risk.

## Core cognitive processes

Perception and attention. Animals extract information from sensory organs in ways that often resemble human perception and sometimes exceed it: bat and dolphin echolocation, fish motion detection through skin receptors, and ultraviolet vision in some birds. Attention is a limited resource that selects relevant information, can be divided among stimuli (pigeons learn tone-plus-light compound discriminations), and can be primed by recent experience. Tinbergen's "searching image," in which birds become better at detecting an insect type they have just repeatedly encountered, is a classic example.

Concepts and categories. Animals group things by appearance, function, and abstract relation. Pigeons trained by Herrnstein pecked photographs containing humans and rejected those without, even with novel photos they had never seen. Bees can form concepts of "above" and "below." In matching-to-sample tasks, many species learn to pick the stimulus that matches a previously seen one; with extensive training, pigeons and reliably monkeys and chimpanzees transfer this to novel stimuli, suggesting a grasp of "same" and "different" rather than memorized pairings.

Memory. The same divisions used for human memory (short-term/working vs. long-term/reference) apply to animals. Delayed matching-to-sample, radial-arm mazes, Morris water mazes, and novel-object recognition tests probe these systems. Spatial memory is especially well studied: Clark's nutcrackers and certain jays and squirrels remember thousands of cache locations, and the hippocampus is implicated as a physiological substrate. Honeybees combine a transient working memory with a long-term reference memory specific to a feeder, and a single learning trial can produce memory lasting days, with three trials producing a lifetime memory.

Spatial cognition. Animals use several navigation strategies, often in combination. A visible goal is a *beacon*. Nearby objects serve as *landmarks*. *Dead reckoning* (path integration) lets a desert ant that has wandered in a twisted foraging path head straight home, correcting for displacement. Some animals build *cognitive maps*, taking novel short-cuts between known sites rather than retracing familiar routes. Some jumping spiders take detours to prey, suggesting flexible route planning.

Tool use and problem solving. Tool use is no longer treated as uniquely human. Chimpanzees in Fongoli sharpen sticks into spears for hunting. A New Caledonian crow named Betty spontaneously bent straight wire into a hook to lift a bucket of meat, then repeated the trick. The parrot Alex learned more than 50 object labels, several colors and shapes, and the concepts "same" and "different," undermining the view that parrots merely mimic. Several wrasse species use rocks as anvils to crack bivalves. Octopuses open jars and carry coconut shells for shelter. Asian elephants, like Köhler's apes, have moved a box into position to reach suspended food.

Reasoning, insight, and numeracy. Köhler introduced *insight* as an experimental concept: a sudden reorganization of behavior rather than gradual learning. A range of species show numerical abilities: rhesus monkeys order 1–9 items, ants of some species count up to 20 and add and subtract within 5, honeybees and angelfish discriminate quantities, and salamanders, bears, dolphins, and parrots have passed "more vs. fewer" tests.

Self-awareness, theory of mind, and metacognition. In Gallup's mirror test, an animal that uses a mirror to investigate a mark on its own body is taken as showing self-recognition; great apes, bottlenose dolphins, an Asian elephant, and the European magpie have passed, while monkeys generally have not. Ravens adjust cache protection based on whether a competitor can actually see them, suggesting they understand "seeing" as a mental state. Great apes, dolphins, and rhesus monkeys can opt out of difficult trials with an "I don't know" response, indicating they monitor their own knowledge. A 2012 Cambridge conference declaration stated that the neurological substrates of consciousness are not unique to humans.

## Limits and open debates

Counter-evidence is genuine. Morgan and Thorndike argued that apparently clever behavior often reduces to trial-and-error or instinct. Povinelli and Eddy found that young chimpanzees begged from experimenters regardless of whether those experimenters could see them. Nissani's elephants kept tossing aside a lid even after it no longer blocked food, suggesting limits to causal grasp.

Biological constraints matter. *Instinctive drift* is the tendency of trained animals to revert to species-typical behaviors, as when a raccoon rubbed coins instead of depositing them. Larger-brained species generally show more learning-dependent behavior. The encephalization quotient (EQ) formalizes brain size relative to body size; Jerison's late-1960s formula fits mammalian data.

The "scala naturae" view that species stack on a single ladder is contested; cognitive skills are adaptations to specific ecological niches, and no species is "the smartest" overall. Fish show reversal learning, novel obstacle avoidance, and oxytocin-mediated social awareness, complicating any simple hierarchy. Whether a general intelligence factor (*g*) exists in non-human animals is debated: one meta-analysis reports an average inter-task correlation of only 0.18, suggesting the evidence for a single *g* outside humans is weak.

Failures on artificial tasks may reflect captivity, stress, low ecological validity, or task structure rather than absence of a capacity. Wild Mexican jays solved an extractive foraging problem faster than captive conspecifics. A claim of absence should be read as a claim about performance under specific conditions rather than a definitive statement about a species' mind.
