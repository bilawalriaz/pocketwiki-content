# Cognitive neuroscience

Cognitive neuroscience studies how the brain's biological machinery produces cognition. It investigates which neural circuits give rise to mental processes such as memory, attention, language, and decision-making, and sits at the intersection of neuroscience, psychology, cognitive science, and computational modeling. A *neuron* is a nerve cell that sends electrical signals; a *neural circuit* is a connected group of neurons whose joint activity produces a behavior or mental state.

The field rests on a premise: if a mental function depends on a particular part of the brain, then damaging, stimulating, or recording from that region should predictably change the function. For most of modern history this premise was contested between two views. The *localizationist view* holds that specific functions map onto specific regions. The opposing *aggregate field view*, that all areas contribute equally to all behavior, was defended by Pierre Flourens, who removed brain tissue from pigeons and saw broad deficits. Modern cognitive neuroscience is built on localization, but with the added nuance that most functions rely on networks of regions rather than single spots.

## Evidence that built the field

Four lines of evidence established that specific regions support specific functions.

*Brain lesions.* In 1848, Phineas Gage survived an iron rod through his prefrontal cortex, with lasting changes to personality and decision-making. In 1861, Paul Broca tied damage to the posterior inferior frontal gyrus (now Broca's area) to an inability to speak. In 1874, Carl Wernicke linked the left posterior superior temporal gyrus to language comprehension. Damage to these areas produces *aphasia*, a language disorder that can include incoherent "word salad" speech.

*Electrical stimulation.* In 1870, Eduard Hitzig and Gustav Fritsch applied current to a dog's cortex and produced muscle contractions in different body parts depending on the spot, showing that the motor cortex is mapped onto the body.

*Cellular structure.* In the 1870s, Camillo Golgi's silver staining revealed nerve cells. Santiago Ramón y Cajal improved the method and argued in 1887 that neurons are discrete, independent cells that transmit signals in one direction, the *neuron doctrine*. Golgi and Cajal shared the 1906 Nobel Prize.

*Brain mapping.* In 1909, Korbinian Brodmann divided the cortex into 52 regions (*Brodmann areas*) by structural differences, later tied to distinct functions. The cortex is commonly divided into the frontal, parietal, occipital, temporal, and insular lobes, separated by landmarks such as the lateral (Sylvian) fissure and the longitudinal fissure.

## Tools that made the field possible

Hans Berger's 1924 electroencephalogram (EEG) added non-invasive measurement of brain electrical activity, including the alpha rhythm. Angelo Mosso's 1878 observation that brain pulsations rose locally during mental calculation established the link between blood flow and neural activity. That principle later powered positron emission tomography (PET), with a clinical prototype in 1953 and the first research PET scanner in 1973, and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), building on Paul Lauterbur's 1971 MRI invention, which has dominated cognitive neuroscience since the 1990s. Other modern methods include magnetoencephalography (MEG, 1968), transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS, 1985), optogenetics, single-unit recording in animals, eye tracking, and near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS).

The term "cognitive neuroscience" was coined in the late 1970s by Michael Gazzaniga and George Miller, who integrated cognitive science with neuropsychology and neuroscience. Cognitive science had emerged from a 1956 MIT meeting featuring Miller, Noam Chomsky, and Newell and Simon, and was named in Ulric Neisser's 1967 *Cognitive Psychology*. David Marr's three levels of analysis (computational, algorithmic, physical) became a standard framework. Behaviorism had dominated early 20th-century psychology by treating the mind as inaccessible; the *cognitive revolution* rejected that, opening the door to linking mind and brain.

## Typical research methods

Studies typically combine experimental paradigms from psychophysics and cognitive psychology, functional neuroimaging (fMRI, PET) to localise active regions, electrophysiology (EEG, MEG, single-unit recording) to capture timing, TMS or optogenetics to perturb specific circuits, cognitive genomics and behavioral genetics to link genes to cognition, and lesion studies in patients, which still serve as a cornerstone because brain damage acts as a natural experiment.

## Recent directions and link to AI

Recent work has moved beyond asking where a function lives toward how regions interact. Studies now combine multiple technologies, use naturalistic stimuli such as films, and rely on computational modeling. Newer tools include shadow imaging, which combines light microscopy with fluorescence labeling of interstitial fluid to visualize brain tissue in fine detail, and faster fMRI sequences such as DIANA, which images every 5 milliseconds.

The closest external payoff is artificial intelligence. Convolutional neural networks were inspired by the visual cortex, and deep learning broadly mirrors hierarchical processing in the brain. The 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, awarded to John O'Keefe, May-Britt Moser, and Edvard Moser for discovering the brain's grid and place cells, shows how findings about biological circuits continue to inform machine intelligence.
