# Behavioural sciences

Behavioural science is the interdisciplinary study of what organisms do, why they do it, and how to change it. Though the term technically covers all living organisms, it is almost always applied to humans, using animals only when invasive methods or cross-species comparisons are needed. The field sits between psychology, cognitive science, neuroscience, behavioural genetics, and the social sciences, drawing methods from each to link observed actions to their cognitive, neural, and social causes.

## Core disciplines and methods

Behavioural science brings together branches of psychology, neuroscience, behavioural biology, behavioural economics, and portions of sociology, criminology, and political science. Researchers combine psychological experiments, neuroimaging, genetic data, self-reports, cross-cultural comparisons, and longitudinal or correlational designs to identify the frequency, mechanisms, causes, and consequences of specific behaviours. Applied work usually narrows this to cognitive psychology, social psychology, and behavioural economics, pulling in fields like health psychology when needed.

The methodological toolkit has changed in step with technology. Early twentieth-century work relied on controlled conditioning experiments, such as B.F. Skinner's operant conditioning chamber (the "Skinner box"), which automated the measurement of learning and reinforcement in animals, and Ivan Pavlov's classical conditioning protocols in dogs. Because human brain function could not be recorded directly, researchers used invasive techniques in animals, including targeted lesions, intracranial electrodes for single-unit recording, and microdialysis to sample neurotransmitter concentrations, establishing causal links between brain structures, neurochemistry, and behaviour.

From the late twentieth century onward, non-invasive human imaging took over: fMRI measures blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signals to localise active brain regions during tasks; EEG records scalp electrical activity with millisecond resolution; MEG detects the magnetic fields produced by neural currents. Computational methods such as reinforcement learning models, Bayesian decision frameworks, and machine learning on large behavioural datasets now complement these tools, allowing researchers to formalise theories and predict decision patterns at scale.

## Applications

Applied behavioural science exploits known cognitive biases, heuristics, and decision quirks to design interventions. Consumer behaviour research examines how purchasing decisions are made and how they can be influenced. Organisational behaviour applies the same insights inside firms, studying what motivates employees and how managers can lead more effectively. Health interventions use levers such as loss aversion, framing, defaults, and nudges to steer choices, and policy work has used these techniques widely, including during the COVID-19 pandemic response. Other applied branches include operations research and media psychology.

## Key figures

- B.F. Skinner: pioneer of operant conditioning and schedules of reinforcement.
- Ivan Pavlov: discovered classical conditioning in dogs.
- John B. Watson: early proponent of controlled behavioural experiments.
- Albert Bandura: social learning theory, observational learning, self-efficacy.
- Eric Kandel: memory and synaptic plasticity.
- Michael Gazzaniga: split-brain research and the cognitive neuroscience of consciousness.
- Robert Sapolsky: stress physiology, primate behaviour, and neuroendocrinology.

Behavioural science's distinctive value lies in tracing behaviour continuously from neural mechanisms to real-world decisions, then turning that understanding into interventions that measurably shift how people act.
