# Borderline personality disorder

Borderline personality disorder (BPD) is a long-term pattern of unstable relationships, a distorted sense of self, and intense emotions driven by difficulty regulating feelings. Symptoms typically begin in adolescence, are diagnosed in early adulthood, and persist across situations. Onset can be triggered by events others consider ordinary. Roughly 1.6% of people qualify at any given time, and about 5.9% meet criteria at some point in life; in clinical settings, women are diagnosed about three times as often as men, though community studies show no real gender difference.

The central feature is *emotional dysregulation*: high sensitivity to emotional cues, intense reactions, rapid mood shifts, a bias toward negative feelings, high affective empathy but low cognitive empathy, and a slow return to calm. People rely on maladaptive strategies, including self-harm, rumination, and avoidance, because they cannot deploy healthier ones. This dysregulation is thought to arise from an imbalance between the limbic system (especially the amygdala) and the prefrontal cortex: brain imaging shows increased activity in circuits generating emotional pain and decreased activity in circuits that suppress it. Volume reductions in the amygdala, hippocampus, and orbitofrontal and anterior cingulate cortex have been observed.

Interpersonal relationships are marked by *splitting*, a black-and-white oscillation between idealizing and devaluing others, and an internal counterpart where the self flips between feeling wronged and feeling worthless. Family members may swing between over-involvement and detachment. Impulsive acts, including substance use, binge eating, unsafe sex, and self-injury, provide brief relief from emotional pain, followed by shame that intensifies the original pain and drives the next impulse, a cycle that becomes automatic. Between 50% and 80% of people with BPD self-harm, usually by cutting; roughly 70% do so without intending to die, using it instead to express anger, self-punish, or interrupt dissociative numbness. Up to 10% die by suicide, a rate far above the general population; men with BPD die by suicide more than twice as often as women with BPD (about 18% vs 8%).

A fragile sense of self produces identity uncertainty, chronic emptiness, and chameleon-like mirroring of whoever is nearby. Dissociation and concentration problems are common. About 20–50% of patients experience psychotic symptoms, including hallucinations and delusions, and these appear phenomenologically similar to those in schizophrenia, suggesting shared neurobiology rather than a separate "pseudo-psychosis."

Causes are multifactorial, with no consensus. Heritability is estimated at 37–69%; a Swedish study found 46% heritability with an 11.5-fold risk in identical twins. Adverse childhood experiences, especially abuse, neglect, and emotional invalidation, strongly correlate with later BPD. Marsha Linehan's biosocial model proposes that BPD emerges when a biologically vulnerable child meets an environment that punishes or dismisses emotional expression.

Diagnosis (DSM-5) requires five of nine criteria covering instability of relationships, self-image, affect, and impulsivity. The ICD-11 instead uses a dimensional severity scale with a "borderline pattern" qualifier. BPD is commonly misdiagnosed as bipolar disorder; bipolar mood episodes last at least two weeks and are unresponsive to environment, whereas BPD mood shifts are brief and reactive.

Psychotherapy is the primary treatment, especially dialectical behavior therapy (DBT), schema therapy, mentalization-based treatment, and transference-focused psychotherapy. DBT trains skills in distress tolerance, emotional regulation, interpersonal effectiveness, and mindfulness. Medications do not treat BPD itself but address comorbidities; some evidence supports topiramate for anger and aripiprazole for hostility. Short-term hospitalization offers no advantage over community care. Remission rates reach 50–70% within five years and roughly 50% within ten, with 86% of patients attaining at least a four-year remission, though a 30% ten-year relapse risk means improvement, not cure. Stigma within the mental health field, including pejorative labels like "manipulative" and "treatment resistant," remains a major barrier to people receiving the care that produces these outcomes.
