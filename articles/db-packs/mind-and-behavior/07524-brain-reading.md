# Brain-reading

Brain-reading, also called thought identification, infers a person's mental content from non-invasive measurements of brain activity, most often fMRI (which tracks blood-flow changes linked to neural activity) or EEG (which records electrical activity through the scalp). Each stimulus, image, word, or intention produces a distinct pattern across many voxels (the three-dimensional pixels of a brain scan), and machine-learning decoders can be trained to map those patterns back onto what the person experienced.

Two analytical goals dominate the field. **Classification** assigns a brain-activity pattern to one of a set of categories, for instance deciding which of ten images a volunteer is thinking about. **Reconstruction** tries to rebuild a literal picture of what was seen, heard, or imagined. Early reconstruction used voxels from primary visual areas V1, V2, and V3 to recover simple geometric shapes. By 2009, Thomas Naselaris and Jack Gallant used a Bayesian framework to reconstruct recognisable natural images: subjects first viewed 1,750 black-and-white photographs while a model learned the mapping from voxels to image features, then the system guessed 120 new images well enough to be structurally and semantically similar. In 2011, Shinji Nishimoto reconstructed short video clips by searching a large library of YouTube footage for material whose voxel patterns most closely matched a volunteer's brain activity. In 2023, Yu Takagi and Nishimoto applied the generative model Stable Diffusion to fMRI data, producing much higher-resolution image reconstructions, and a 2024 study extended the approach to purely imagined images with no visual stimulus at all. EEG-based image reconstruction was also reported in 2024.

A second strand decodes language. Statistical analysis of EEG has been claimed to recognise phonemes and, in 1999, simple visual words at 60–75% accuracy. In 2012, Brian Pasley's team at UC Berkeley reconstructed audible speech from electrodes near the superior temporal gyrus, the cortical region that processes sound meaning, though the output was noisy and had to be matched by waveform to the original. In 2015, Toshimasa Yamazaki's group at the Kyushu Institute of Technology used EEG over Broca's area, a speech-production region, to infer a rock–paper–scissors choice before the subject's hand moved. In 2023, the University of Texas at Austin fed non-invasive recordings into a GPT-1 language model, producing paraphrases that could usually place a listener at the right point in an audiobook even when the exact words were wrong. In a 2020 study using implanted electrodes and about 40 minutes of training per participant, an AI transcribed read-aloud sentences from a 50-sentence set with 97% accuracy.

Brain-reading also reaches into intention and decision-making. In 2008, John-Dylan Haynes's group predicted, with 60% accuracy, whether a subject would press a left or right button, and did so up to ten seconds before the subject reported having decided. Haynes has separately shown that fMRI can distinguish whether a person is mentally adding or subtracting, and whether they recognise a face, object, or crime scene.

Three practical constraints matter. First, the data are noisy and indirect: fMRI measures blood oxygenation rather than neural firing, and the mapping from blood flow to activity is incompletely understood, so exact reconstruction is impossible and every output is a best guess constrained by a statistical prior. Second, performance depends on individual calibration, because voxel geometry and functional anatomy vary between people. Third, lab accuracy does not translate cleanly to real-world settings. For lie detection, studies report up to 85% accuracy, but if only a small fraction of those tested are actually lying, false positives can exceed true positives, with roughly one in five innocent people misclassified. EEG-based "brain fingerprinting" attempts a related goal, using the P300 response, a positive electrical deflection about 300 ms after a recognised stimulus, to detect whether a person recognises information held only by the perpetrator of a crime.

The ethical questions are at least as significant as the technical ones. Most US legal scholars hold that involuntary brain scanning, like involuntary polygraphy, would breach the Fifth Amendment protection against self-incrimination, though some argue brain data are closer to physical evidence such as DNA. Outside the United States the technology has already entered courtrooms: in 2008 an Indian court convicted a woman of murder after an EEG was said to show familiarity with the crime. Ethicists warn of unreliable evidence being deployed prematurely, of unprecedented access to private thought, and of severe risks for patients who use brain–computer interfaces to speak, where devices may broadcast inner monologue rather than intended speech.
