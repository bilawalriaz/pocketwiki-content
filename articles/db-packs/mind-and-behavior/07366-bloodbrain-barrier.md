# Blood–brain barrier

The blood–brain barrier (BBB) is a selective interface of tightly joined capillary cells that controls which substances pass from the blood into the brain. Its job is to keep harmful or unnecessary molecules out of the central nervous system while still letting the brain receive the nutrients, ions, and signals it needs.

A *tight junction* is a molecular seal between neighbouring *endothelial cells* (the cells that line every blood vessel). The brain's capillaries use unusually strong tight junctions, and that is the basis of the barrier.

## What forms the barrier

Three cell types work together at each brain capillary. *Endothelial cells* form the wall, joined continuously by tight junctions made of transmembrane proteins such as occludin, claudins (notably claudin-5), and junctional adhesion molecules, all anchored inside the cell by scaffolding proteins like ZO-1. *Astrocyte* end-feet wrap the vessel and provide biochemical support. *Pericytes* sit embedded in the basement membrane around the capillary.

The BBB is distinct from the blood–cerebrospinal fluid barrier, which is built by the choroid plexus, and from the blood–retinal barrier in the eye. Not every brain vessel has BBB properties: the *circumventricular organs* (CVOs) instead have highly permeable capillaries.

## What crosses and what does not

Small, fat-soluble molecules diffuse freely: oxygen, carbon dioxide, hormones, and drugs under about 400 daltons in mass slip through the lipid membrane. Water-soluble molecules, large molecules, and most pathogens are blocked.

Essential nutrients that cannot diffuse are pulled across by specific transport proteins. Glucose, amino acids, ions, and organic anions enter through carriers. Larger molecules such as insulin and transferrin enter by *receptor-mediated transcytosis*, in which the cell engulfs them in a vesicle and releases them on the other side. The barrier also uses active efflux pumps, mainly P-glycoprotein, to push unwanted substances back into the blood.

Because antibodies and most immune cells cannot cross, the brain is partly insulated from peripheral immune activity. Blood-borne brain infections are therefore rare, but the ones that do occur are hard to treat: only certain antibiotics penetrate the barrier, so in some cases a drug must be injected directly into the cerebrospinal fluid.

## Exceptions: circumventricular organs and border zones

The CVOs sit next to the third and fourth ventricles and have permeable capillaries that serve two purposes. Sensory CVOs (area postrema, subfornical organ, vascular organ of the lamina terminalis) sample blood to detect circulating signals quickly. Secretory CVOs (median eminence, pineal gland, pituitary lobes) release brain-made hormones into the bloodstream. Together they form the main sites of bidirectional blood–brain communication for neuroendocrine function.

Between a typical brain capillary and a CVO capillary lie hybrid border zones of intermediate leakiness, found at the area postrema–nucleus tractus solitarii border and at the median eminence–hypothalamic arcuate nucleus border. These act as rapid transit regions where blood signals are handed off to nearby neurons.

## Development and disease

The barrier is functional at birth. P-glycoprotein is already present in embryonal endothelium, and newborn capillaries handle blood-borne solutes much like adult ones.

The barrier can be disrupted in Alzheimer's disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, epilepsy, hemorrhagic and ischemic stroke, and brain trauma, as well as in systemic liver failure. In brain tumours the barrier is often partly broken.

## Drug delivery

The barrier excludes about 100% of large-molecule therapeutics and more than 98% of small-molecule drugs, the central obstacle in treating brain disorders. Strategies to get through it include osmotic or chemical disruption (for example with bradykinin), localised high-intensity focused ultrasound, hijacking natural carriers such as glucose or amino acid transporters, and blocking efflux pumps like P-glycoprotein. Intranasal administration offers a non-invasive alternative, with drugs reaching the brain along olfactory or trigeminal nerve pathways, though it is less efficient than systemic routes.

## Origin of the concept

In 1898 an experiment showed that low doses of bile salts injected into blood had no behavioural effect, suggesting they never reached the brain. The term "blood–brain barrier" was first used around 1900 and is often attributed to Max Lewandowsky, though some historians credit the Russian–French scientist Lina Stern, whose non-English publications may have been overlooked. Around the same time, Paul Ehrlich found that aniline dyes stained every organ of an animal except the brain, and his student Edwin Goldmann showed in 1913 that injecting dye into cerebrospinal fluid stained the brain but not the rest of the body, confirming a sharp compartmentalisation between blood and brain.
