# Cognitive science

Cognitive science is the interdisciplinary study of the mind and its processes. It examines perception, memory, attention, reasoning, language, and emotion by drawing on psychology, neuroscience, linguistics, philosophy, computer science, and anthropology. A unifying assumption is that thinking can be understood as the manipulation of internal representations by computational procedures.

## Origins

The field's modern roots lie in the 1930s and 1940s, when Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts built the first artificial neural networks, computation models inspired by biological neurons. In the 1940s and 1950s, Gödel, Church, Shannon, Turing, and von Neumann developed the theory of computation and the digital computer, which gave cognitive science its central metaphor, the mind as a computer, and its main research tool.

The cognitive revolution of the 1950s replaced behaviorism, the view that psychology should study only observable stimulus–response links, with the study of internal mental states. A decisive moment came in 1959, when Noam Chomsky argued in a review of B. F. Skinner that language requires internal representational rules rather than learned response patterns. Christopher Longuet-Higgins coined the term "cognitive science" in 1973. The Cognitive Science Society was founded in 1979 at the University of California, San Diego, which established the first dedicated department in 1986. Vassar College granted the first undergraduate degree in the field in 1982.

## Levels of analysis

A central tenet is that no single level of explanation fully describes cognition. Studying a phone number being remembered by recording only behavior (recall accuracy) or only neurons (firing patterns) leaves a gap: behavior does not reveal the internal states that cause it, and neural firing does not explain the subjective experience of remembering. David Marr's three-level framework, proposed in 1982, bridges this gap by asking, for any cognitive process: what is the goal of the computation, what representations and algorithms realize that goal, and how is the algorithm physically implemented in neurons or silicon.

## Two modeling traditions

Cognitive science has long been split between two approaches to modeling the mind:

- Symbolic AI, dominant through the 1970s and early 1980s, represents knowledge as discrete symbols manipulated by rules. Researchers such as Marvin Minsky wrote programs in LISP to formalize human reasoning. By the late 1980s, its limits were clear: hand-coding all human knowledge as rules proved unworkable.
- Connectionism, the layered neural network approach associated with James McClelland and David Rumelhart, represents knowledge as patterns of activation across many simple processing units. It captures learning and perception well but is often too complex to interpret.

Neither approach is biologically realistic, since real neurons behave differently, and modern work increasingly combines them in hybrid systems. Some researchers also explore quantum cognition, which uses formal tools from quantum mechanics to model decision and judgment under uncertainty.

## Methods

Because the field is interdisciplinary, it borrows methods from many disciplines:

- Behavioral experiments measure reaction time, accuracy, and eye movements to infer what the mind is doing.
- Brain imaging links behavior to neural activity. EEG and MEG track electrical and magnetic activity with millisecond precision but poor spatial detail; fMRI and PET localize active brain regions with better spatial detail but slower timing.
- Computational modeling simulates cognitive processes using symbolic, subsymbolic (neural), or hybrid architectures.
- Neurobiological methods, including recording from single neurons, direct brain stimulation, animal models, and postmortem studies, connect cognition to its physical substrate.

## Major topics

**Attention** is the selection of which incoming information to process. In dichotic listening experiments, where a different message is played in each ear, listeners follow one message and remain unaware of the other.

**Memory** has short-term and long-term components, with the practical limit of long-term storage unknown. Declarative memory stores facts (semantic) and personal events (episodic); procedural memory stores skills such as riding a bicycle.

**Language** is studied through its subcomponents: phonetics, phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, and pragmatics. A central debate is whether grammatical knowledge is innate, as nativists argue, or emerges from general learning mechanisms applied to linguistic input.

**Learning and development** investigate how abilities such as walking and language appear so rapidly in early childhood, and how much of cognition is built in versus built from experience.

**Perception and action** examine how the senses construct a stable world from fragments (vision, hearing, touch, smell, taste) and how the brain guides movement.

**Consciousness** remains contentious. There is no agreed definition, and it is unclear whether it can be a scientific concept at all, a point captured by the "hard problem": why and how physical processes give rise to subjective experience.

**Embodied and 4E cognition** argues that the body, environment, and social context shape thought. The 4E framework groups these into embodied (cognition depends on the body), embedded (the environment is part of the cognitive system), enactive (cognition arises through action), and extended (tools and surroundings count as parts of the cognitive apparatus).

## Influence

Cognitive science has reshaped philosophy of mind and language, contributed to linguistics, and supplied models for behavioral finance, which studies how psychological biases affect economic decisions. In medicine, it has clarified how localized brain damage produces specific deficits such as dyslexia, anopsia, and hemispatial neglect.

A remaining puzzle is the binding problem: how the brain combines separate features such as color, shape, and motion, processed in different regions, into a single coherent percept. One proposed mechanism is binding-by-synchrony, the idea that neurons encoding the same object fire in synchrony, and this temporal alignment is how the brain tags features as belonging together.
