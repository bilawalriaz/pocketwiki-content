# Learning

Learning is the process of acquiring new understanding, knowledge, behavior, skills, values, attitudes, or preferences. It occurs in humans, other animals, some machines, and possibly certain plants. Some learning happens in a single event; most accumulates from repeated experience, and the resulting changes often last a lifetime. The field spans educational psychology, neuropsychology, cognitive science, and pedagogy, covering everything from simple habituation to complex play and social learning.

## Non-associative learning

The simplest form is non-associative learning: a relatively permanent change in response strength to a single repeated stimulus, excluding sensory adaptation, fatigue, or injury. **Habituation** diminishes an innate response to a repeated stimulus; songbirds stop reacting to a stuffed owl but still react to a new one. It is not the same as extinction, which involves association. Habituation appears in essentially every animal species and has been reported in *Mimosa pudica* (the sensitive plant) and the protozoan *Stentor coeruleus*. **Sensitization** is its opposite: progressive amplification of a response after repeated exposure, linked to both adaptive and maladaptive processes.

## Associative learning

Associative learning links two stimuli, or a behavior with its consequence. In **classical conditioning**, a neutral stimulus (a bell) is paired with a reflex-eliciting stimulus (meat powder, which causes salivation) until the bell alone produces salivation. The meat powder is the unconditioned stimulus, salivation the unconditioned response, and after pairings the bell becomes a conditioned stimulus producing a conditioned response. The same effect has been shown in honeybees and in garden pea plants.

**Operant conditioning** shapes behavior through reinforcement or punishment. Reinforcement should follow the behavior immediately; a gap produces "trace conditioning." Punishment can suppress behavior but only teaches avoidance; it cannot increase wanted behavior. Positive punishment adds something aversive, negative punishment removes something desirable, negative reinforcement removes an undesirable condition, and positive reinforcement adds a desirable one.

## Social and developmental forms

**Observational learning** occurs through watching others and needs no direct reinforcement, depending on social models. **Imprinting** is rapid, consequence-independent learning at a specific life stage, first described in birds by Konrad Lorenz in 1935. **Play** improves future performance despite costs in predator exposure, injury, and energy, implying substantial benefits. It teaches children social, emotional, cognitive, and language skills, and Vygotsky regarded it as the first setting where children grasp rules and symbols. Five overlapping play types—sensorimotor, roleplay (from about age three), rule-based, construction, and movement—intersect in typical play.

**Enculturation** is learning the values and behaviors of one's own culture, distinct from acculturation (adopting another culture's norms); examples include Mazahua collaborative practices. **Episodic learning** records a single event in episodic memory, which retains events in their experiential context. Semantic memory, by contrast, extracts timeless facts. People recognize episodic memories accurately even without intending to memorize, suggesting large storage capacity.

## Structured and technology-mediated learning

**Formal learning** is deliberate, teacher-directed, and goal-set by institutions, often credentialed. **Non-formal learning** is organized outside formal systems. **Informal learning** arises spontaneously from daily life, is self-directed and voluntary, and typically lacks accreditation. Systems can combine all three.

**Multimedia learning** combines auditory and visual stimuli, drawing on dual-coding theory. **E-learning** is computer-enhanced; **mobile learning** uses phones; **augmented learning** personalizes instruction as learners interact with their environment. For online learning, Moore identified three essential interactions—learner–learner, learner–instructor, and learner–content—with "transactional distance" bridged by structure and dialogue. **Incidental learning** is unplanned, a byproduct of another activity; on-campus students report it in about three times as many areas as online students, though the comparability of the two modalities remains under-studied.

## Transfer and domains

**Transfer of learning**—applying knowledge to a novel problem—is infrequent and most common when learners are cued, primed, and guided. Near transfer applies to a similar context, far transfer to a different one. Positive transfer helps a new task; negative transfer, where prior learning interferes, hinders it. Bloom's taxonomy groups objectives into three non-exclusive domains: cognitive (recall, analysis), psychomotor (physical skills), and affective (emotions, values).

## Factors that help or hinder learning

Effective techniques include spaced repetition (distributing study to counteract the forgetting curve that follows cramming), desirable difficulty, learning by teaching, self-explaining, and low-stakes testing. Intrinsic motivation sustains learning better than extrinsic rewards such as grades; praise helps, while fault-finding and embarrassment undermine it. Genetics influence ability, and malnutrition, fatigue, poor health, bad ventilation, poor lighting, and crowded or poorly arranged spaces all impede it.

At the molecular level, learning involves epigenetic changes such as DNA methylation and histone modifications. Children learn faster in part because rapid GABA boosting and abundant "silent synapses" support neuroplasticity, yet late middle-aged adults returning to university courses suggest that age differences may reflect time, support, environment, and attitudes rather than inherent capacity.

## Evolution and the plant question

Learning evolves when environments change within an animal's lifetime but not constantly; in fully stable or constantly shifting environments, non-learning evolves, and in *Drosophila melanogaster* learning can even reduce productivity. Gagliano's pea experiments suggest classical conditioning in plants: seedlings grew toward a light location predicted by a fan, possibly through auxin signaling that couples mechanosensory and photosensory pathways. Whether this counts as true learning is contested because researchers lack consistent definitions of "learning" and "cognition"; critics question the terminology rather than the data.

## Machine learning

Machine learning, a branch of artificial intelligence, builds systems that learn from data—for example, spam filters that classify messages. Most methods rely on probabilistic models associating inputs with output probabilities.
