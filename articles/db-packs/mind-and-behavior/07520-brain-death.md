# Brain death

Brain death is the permanent, irreversible loss of all brain function, including the brainstem. Because the brainstem controls breathing and consciousness, a brain-dead person on a ventilator shows no response, no reflexes, and cannot breathe unaided. They are legally and medically dead, even though machines can keep the heart beating and organs oxygenated.

## What it isn't

Brain death is not a coma and not a persistent vegetative state. In a coma or vegetative state, some brain activity persists and the person is alive. Brain death also differs from locked-in syndrome, in which the conscious mind is intact but the body is paralysed. Distinguishing these matters because only brain death qualifies as death.

Two main definitions exist worldwide. The **whole-brain** definition (United States) requires the cerebrum and brainstem to be dead, so spontaneous breathing is impossible. The **brainstem-death** definition (United Kingdom) focuses on the brainstem alone, because losing the brainstem stops breathing and consciousness. When only the cerebrum is dead but the brainstem works, the patient can still breathe on their own and is not legally dead.

## How it is diagnosed

Doctors first rule out reversible mimics: drug or alcohol overdose, sedation, hypothermia, and low blood sugar. Only when these are excluded does the exam proceed.

The core clinical test checks three things:

1. Unresponsive coma with no reaction to pain.
2. Absent brainstem reflexes, including fixed pupils, no corneal reflex (blink when the eye is touched), absent oculocephalic reflex (eyes do not move opposite to head turning), and no response to the caloric reflex test (cold water in the ear normally drives eye movement).
3. Apnoea test: no breathing effort when the ventilator is briefly removed and carbon dioxide rises in the blood, a strong chemical trigger to breathe.

Two independent physicians usually perform the exam. Confirmatory tests, such as an EEG (flat-line), a cerebral blood flow scan showing no intracranial circulation, or transcranial Doppler, are used when the exam is hard to interpret, for example in severe facial injury or in infants under one year. A flat EEG is helpful in the US but not in the UK, where any remaining activity above the brainstem is treated as irrelevant.

Family members sometimes see arm or torso movements in a brain-dead patient, the **Lazarus sign**. These come from spinal cord reflexes, not the brain, and can falsely suggest recovery.

## Causes and frequency

Causes include cardiac arrest, stroke, severe traumatic brain injury, prolonged lack of oxygen, and brain haemorrhage, all of which destroy brain tissue through ischaemia or direct injury. In the United States, brain death accounts for about 2% of adult and 5% of paediatric in-hospital deaths. A 2019 survey counted more than 3,000 paediatric brain deaths among over 15,000 children who died in US paediatric intensive care units.

## Legal and ethical history

Before ventilators, death meant the heart and breathing had stopped. Once medicine could keep the body running without any brain function, a new definition became urgent, especially because transplant programs needed a clear moment of death to recover healthy organs.

A 1968 Harvard Medical School report defined "irreversible coma." In 1981, a US presidential commission issued *Defining Death*, rejecting the idea that only the "higher" thinking parts of the brain must die and adopting a whole-brain standard. This became the **Uniform Determination of Death Act**, enacted in 39 US states. The UK shifted in 1995, when the Royal College of Physicians adopted brainstem death as the legal basis. In 2020, the World Brain Death Project published international guidelines to reduce inconsistencies between countries.

## Organ donation

Because a brain-dead body on a ventilator keeps its organs perfused with oxygenated blood, it is the ideal source for transplantable hearts, livers, kidneys, and lungs. The official time of death is the moment brain death was diagnosed, not when the heart later stops. Some countries (Spain, France, Portugal, the UK) treat everyone as a default organ donor unless they opt out; others, including most of the United States, rely on prior registration or family consent. A do-not-resuscitate order can prevent recovery of the heart and lungs, but other tissues often remain usable.
