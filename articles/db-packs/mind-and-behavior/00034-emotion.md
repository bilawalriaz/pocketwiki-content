# Emotion

An emotion is a physical and mental state produced by neurophysiological change, combining subjective experience, thought, bodily response, and outward behavior. There is no scientific consensus on a single definition, and emotion overlaps with mood, temperament, personality, and disposition. Mechanistically, an emotion is a positive or negative experience tied to a particular pattern of physiological activity, making it inherently multicomponent rather than purely mental.

## Etymology and Core Components

The word *emotion* was coined in 1543 by French humanist Étienne Dolet in a translation of Cicero's *Tusculanae Disputationes*, from Latin for "a movement from inside to outside." It entered English in 1579 and became a full theoretical term in the early 1800s, especially under Thomas Brown. Categories such as "anger" and "sadness" are not classified the same way across all cultures, though some researchers argue for universal bases. An inability to express or perceive emotion is termed *alexithymia*.

Scherer's Component Process Model identifies five elements of any emotional episode: cognitive appraisal (evaluating an event), bodily symptoms (physiological change), action tendencies (motivation to act), expression (face and voice), and subjective feelings. These are coordinated in sequence. Emotions can be brief occurrences (panic) or enduring dispositions (hostility), and they vary in intensity along a continuum, from mild concern to terror.

## Classification

Researchers divide on whether emotions are discrete or dimensional. Paul Ekman identified six basic emotions: anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, and surprise, treated as physiologically distinct. Cordaro and Keltner later extended the list to include amusement, awe, contentment, desire, embarrassment, pain, relief, and sympathy. Robert Plutchik's "wheel of emotions" groups eight primary affects into opposing pairs: joy versus sadness, anger versus fear, trust versus disgust, surprise versus anticipation. Jaak Panksepp carved out seven biologically inherited affective systems: SEEKING, FEAR, RAGE, LUST, CARE, PANIC/GRIEF, and PLAY.

A competing dimensional approach maps emotions onto valence (positive–negative) and arousal (energized–enervated). Using factor analysis, Cowen and Keltner identified 27 varieties of emotional experience, including awe, nostalgia, entrancement, and empathic pain.

## Historical and Somatic Theories

Theorizing about emotion's evolutionary roots dates to Charles Darwin's 1872 *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals*, which argued that expressions are universal across cultures and have homologs in other animals. In Hinduism, Bharata Muni enumerated the nine *rasas* in the *Nātyasāstra* (200 BC–200 AD), and in the early 11th century Ibn Sina theorized about emotion's influence on health. Stoic theory held that normal emotions are irrational impulses from incorrect appraisals.

Somatic theories treat bodily responses as central. The James–Lange theory (1884) proposed that perception of an exciting fact triggers a physiological response, which then produces emotion: "we feel sad because we cry." The Cannon–Bard theory countered that bodily responses are too slow, arguing physiological and conscious experience occur simultaneously. Schachter's two-factor theory described emotion as physiological arousal followed by cognitive appraisal of that arousal. A central open question is whether bodily states cause emotion or merely influence it.

## Neurobiology and Modern Debates

Emotion is organized in the limbic system, a group of brain structures including the hypothalamus, cingulate cortex, and hippocampi, and involves neurochemicals such as dopamine, noradrenaline, and serotonin. The amygdala coordinates responses to threat. James Papez (1937) and Paul D. MacLean (1952) established the limbic system's role, and LeDoux and Damasio advanced neurological work in the 1990s. Bud Craig (2003) distinguished "classical" emotions, triggered by external stimuli, from "homeostatic emotions" such as pain, hunger, and fatigue. Lisa Feldman Barrett argues emotions are socially constructed, "created" rather than triggered. Another live debate is whether cognitive appraisal is a genuine component of emotion or a separate interacting system.

## Disciplinary Views and Memory

Sociology treats emotion through cultural labels, physiological change, expressive movement, and appraisal. The history of emotions treats it as an essential analytical category, with research in transgenerational trauma suggesting some traumatic emotions can pass across generations. Computer science addresses emotion through affective computing, originating with Rosalind Picard's 1995 paper, designing systems that recognize and interpret human feeling. Emotion also shapes autobiographical memory: emotional events are encoded more strongly, reactivated more often, and remembered more vividly, feeding back into self-perception.
