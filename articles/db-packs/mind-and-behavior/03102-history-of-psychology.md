# History of psychology

Psychology is the scientific study of behavior and mental processes. Its history traces how philosophical questions about the mind were converted into experiments, methods, and competing schools, each defining what counted as evidence and what the proper subject matter of the field should be.

## From philosophy to a named discipline

Speculation about the mind is ancient. Plato proposed a tripartite soul of reason, spirit, and appetite; Aristotle theorised the psyche; Chinese and Indian texts described mental illness and the self; and medieval Muslim physicians such as al-Balkhi and Avicenna treated disorders of body and soul together. The word *psychologia* appears in print as early as 1525, but the modern term was popularised by the philosopher Christian Wolff in the 1730s, when psychology still meant the study of the soul in a philosophical register rather than a laboratory practice.

## Psychology becomes experimental

In the 19th century, physiology supplied the tools. Wilhelm Wundt, trained in Helmholtz's physiological laboratory, used lab instruments to study conscious experience and in 1879 founded the first psychology laboratory, in Leipzig, Germany. Sigmund Freud, working in Vienna at the same period, took a different path: case studies of patients led him to psychoanalysis, which locates the causes of behavior in unconscious desires and conflicts. In the United States, William James published *The Principles of Psychology* in 1890, and G. Stanley Hall founded the American Psychological Association in 1892, giving the new discipline its first major textbook and its first professional body.

## The first schools of thought

In the early 20th century, psychology split into rival schools defined by what each considered scientifically respectable.

- **Structuralism** (Edward Titchener) tried to break consciousness into basic elements through **introspection**, trained self-observation in which subjects report their own conscious experiences.
- **Functionalism** (James, John Dewey) asked what consciousness and behavior are *for*, framing mental life through Darwinian adaptation rather than anatomy.
- **Gestalt psychology** (Max Wertheimer, from a 1912 article on the *phi phenomenon*, the illusion of motion produced by flashing static images in sequence) held that perception is organised into meaningful wholes that cannot be reduced to their parts.
- **Behaviorism** (John B. Watson's 1913 manifesto *Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It*, later extended by B.F. Skinner) rejected the study of the inner mind and confined psychology to observable behavior and conditioning. Skinner's **operant conditioning** holds that behavior is shaped by its consequences: reinforcement strengthens it, punishment weakens it. By the 1920s behaviorism dominated American psychology.

## The cognitive revolution

Behaviorism's hold loosened after Noam Chomsky's 1959 review of Skinner's *Verbal Behavior*, which argued that language cannot be explained by stimulus and response alone and requires internal mental structures. The parallel rise of computer science offered a new metaphor, the mind as an information processor. These forces produced the **cognitive revolution** of the 1960s and 1970s, in which **cognitivism** restored memory, perception, and problem-solving to scientific respectability. **Humanistic psychology** had emerged in the 1930s as the "third force", a reaction to behaviorism and psychoanalysis that emphasised free will and the drive toward self-actualisation, the realisation of one's personal potential. Today's psychology is increasingly interdisciplinary, with **cognitive science** integrating neuroscience, linguistics, computer science, and philosophy to study the mind.

## Unresolved questions

The imageless-thought controversy pitted Wundt against the Würzburg School over whether higher thought could be studied by introspection at all. Structuralism and functionalism disagreed on whether psychology's job was to analyse the contents of consciousness or to explain its adaptive purpose. The nature-versus-nurture debate, anticipated by the 12th-century philosopher Ibn Tufail, recurs in every era in new form. The scientific status of psychoanalysis, and of concepts like the unconscious, remains contested. Integration of the biological, cognitive, social, and clinical approaches into one coherent science of mind and behavior is still an open project.
