# Memory card

A memory card is a removable electronic storage device, almost always built from flash memory, that fits a socket on a host device. Memory cards add portable storage to digital cameras, action cameras, drones, e-readers, handheld game consoles, and dashcams. Many modern phones have dropped expandable slots in favor of built-in storage, but cards remain standard elsewhere.

Flash memory, invented by Fujio Masuoka at Toshiba in 1980 and commercialized by Toshiba in 1987, retains data without power. That property distinguishes modern cards from earlier battery-backed SRAM cards and from rotating-disk alternatives.

Memory cards emerged in the 1980s as an alternative to floppy disk drives, consuming less power and volume in laptops, and some were sold as cheaper replacements for ROM game cartridges. Vendors produced many incompatible formats, including the Bee Card, Astron SoftCard, Sega Cards, NEC UltraLite cards, and the Mitsubishi Melcard. The Sega Card was developed as a cheaper alternative to full game cartridges. Some early PCMCIA cards held 1–5 MB and cost about US$100 per MB.

Standardization began with JEIDA, the Japan Electronic Industry Development Association, which started work on a memory card standard in 1985 and produced the JEIDA memory card in 1986. The Personal Computer Memory Card International Association (PCMCIA), founded in 1989, worked with JEIDA and adopted its 68-pin connector design. The PCMCIA Type I specification, released in 1990 and later renamed PC Card, unified the two standards. PC Card survives mainly in industrial applications and as a way to connect I/O devices such as modems.

In 1992, SanDisk introduced FlashDisk, a PCMCIA card and one of the first cards that retained its contents without a battery because it used flash memory. From 1994 onward, smaller formats appeared, beginning with CompactFlash, then SmartMedia and Miniature Card, driven by demand for compact cards in cell phones, PDAs, and compact digital cameras. The SD card was announced in 2000, envisioned as a single memory card format usable across many kinds of electronic devices and as a general expansion slot for adding new capabilities.

The market consolidated around SD. In 2001, SmartMedia held 50% of the digital camera market and CompactFlash dominated professional cameras. By 2005, SD and similar MMC cards had largely displaced SmartMedia, though Memory Stick variants and CompactFlash remained competitive. From 2010 onward, Sony and Olympus began offering devices with an additional SD slot, ending the format war in SD's favor. As of 2024, SD cards are the most common memory card format.

High prices shaped and then ended an interim generation. Around 2001, cards cost about US$3 per megabyte, which motivated miniaturized rotating-disk products such as the Microdrive. The Microdrive offered higher capacities than flash cards at the time and became obsolete once flash prices fell and capacities rose, by 2006.

Late in the 2010s, new high-speed formats appeared to handle 4K, 8K, and RAW video and high-speed continuous photography. CFexpress and Universal Flash Storage (UFS) cards entered the market; XQD, once positioned as a CompactFlash successor, has largely been replaced by CFexpress Type B, which keeps the same physical form factor but uses a faster PCIe interface.

Memory cards also had a parallel life in video game consoles. The Neo Geo AES, released in 1990 by SNK, was the first home console to accept a memory card, and its cards were interchangeable with Neo Geo MVS arcade cabinets, letting players move saves between home and arcade. Memory cards became standard once consoles moved to optical discs for game data, beginning with the TurboGrafx-CD and Sega-CD. Up through the sixth console generation, consoles used proprietary card formats; later systems adopted established formats such as FAT32. Modern home consoles store saves on hard disk drives and accept USB flash drives or cards via a reader, though most portable gaming systems still rely on custom memory cartridges because they draw little power, are physically small, and have no moving parts.

A memory card adapter lets one card type fit a slot designed for another. Passive adapters mainly change the physical connection with little or no electronics, as with a microSD-to-SD shell or a CompactFlash-to-IDE adapter, the latter working passively because CompactFlash already supports ATA-compatible operation. Active adapters contain electronics that translate between protocols, such as SD to ATA/ATAPI, CompactFlash to Serial ATA, or SD to USB card readers. Adapters can be chained, for example microSD to miniSD to SD to CompactFlash, or paired with older bridges like the FlashPath adapter that read an MMC card through a floppy disk drive.
