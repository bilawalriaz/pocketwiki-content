# Criticism of evolutionary psychology

Evolutionary psychology treats the mind as a set of adaptations produced by natural selection, analogous to physical traits. Critics challenge the field on scientific, methodological, and ethical grounds, while defenders argue that many attacks rest on misunderstandings or political bias.

## Scientific and methodological objections

**Massive modularity lacks biological support.** Evolutionary psychologists such as Leda Cosmides and John Tooby propose that the mind is built from many specialised "modules," innate task-specific circuits shaped by selection to handle ancestral problems. Critics counter that brain plasticity, the brain's ability to reorganise itself through experience, allows higher-order cortex to become functionally specialised through experience-dependent changes at synapses, rather than starting out as innately modular. Andrew Peters argues a brain can look modular after learning without being innately so. Critics also note the genome lacks room to encode that many dedicated circuits.

The flagship evidence is the Wason selection task, a logic puzzle where participants decide which cards to flip to test a rule. Cosmides and Tooby found people detect rule violations more easily when framed as cheating on a social contract, which they read as evidence for a cheater-detection module. Critics including Paul Davies argue the experiment only tested conditional reasoning, one of several forms of deduction, so it cannot rule out a general-purpose explanation. Critics add that the task's rules misrepresent real social exchange, since receiving a benefit without paying (as in gifts or charity) is not always cheating.

**The environment of evolutionary adaptedness is speculative.** Evolutionary psychology infers ancestral selection pressures from a hypothesised Pleistocene environment, the EEA. Critics, including David Buller, say too little is known about that period to specify which problems shaped our psychology, since mate attraction and foraging likely varied across populations. Defenders (Tooby, Cosmides, David Buss, Steven Pinker) reply that several features are reliably known, including pregnancies occurring only in women, small kin-based groups, and cooperative hunting, and that hypothesising specific selection pressures is testable through predictions about modern behaviour.

**Hypotheses are hard to test.** Critics accuse the field of generating "just-so stories," post-hoc adaptive explanations that fit the data by construction. Noam Chomsky captured the worry: "just about anything you find, you can make up some story for it." Defenders reply that evolutionary hypotheses make predictions about trait design. Pregnancy sickness, for example, should peak in the first trimester under a fetal-protection account, but should correlate with hormone levels under a byproduct account. Stewart-Williams argues that if evolutionary accounts cannot be falsified, neither can competing cultural accounts, since proving one refutes the other. Edward Hagen frames hypothesis choice as inference to the best explanation, where competing accounts are weighed on criteria such as explanatory breadth, parsimony, and coherence.

**Adaptive explanations are overused.** Evolution also produces traits through genetic drift (random changes in gene frequencies) and as byproducts of other adaptations. Stephen Jay Gould's "spandrel" concept, a trait that persists because it is built alongside an adaptive one, without itself being adaptive, is often invoked to suggest much of human cognition is a side effect of large brains. Defenders counter that an organism contains countless potential spandrels, so calling a trait a spandrel without specifying *what* it is a spandrel of is meaningless. Steven Gangestad argues a genuine adaptation should show "special design," a fit between features and hypothesised problem so precise that it is hard to imagine arising otherwise.

**Reductionism and determinism.** Critics including Richard Lewontin, Steven Rose, and Leon Kamin argue that treating genes as blueprints for behaviour ignores continuous interaction between biology and environment. Hilary Rose notes that most stepfathers do not abuse children, weakening the claim that abuse is an evolved disposition of non-biological fathers. Defenders (Pinker, Workman and Reader) reply that "genes cause behaviour" means a gene raises the probability of a behaviour on average, an interactionist, probabilistic claim shared across biology.

**The disjunction and grain problems.** Bradley Franks notes that because a single mechanism may respond to many correlated cues, it is unclear which is the "true" adaptive target (the disjunction problem), and because an adaptive problem can be split into sub-problems at many scales, it is unclear which level selection acted on (the grain problem). Relaxing these assumptions yields fewer, vaguer predictions, though the underlying evolutionary account of psychology survives.

**Replication failures.** A 2015 Open Science Collaboration project replicated only about 36% of 100 high-profile psychology findings, with effect sizes roughly half the originals. Several evolutionary psychology results, including cyclical shifts in women's preferences for masculine faces, mating primes on risk-taking, and the "watching eyes" effect on prosociality, have failed to replicate in well-powered studies. Critics argue the field is especially vulnerable to p-hacking, selectively analysing data until a desired pattern emerges.

## Empirical disputes

Smith et al. (2001) modelled the fitness costs and benefits of rape and concluded that, on average, costs outweigh benefits ten to one for a 25-year-old male, undermining Thornhill and Palmer's claim of an evolved rape adaptation. Beckerman et al. (2009) found that the most aggressive Waorani warriors had the fewest descendants. Studies in Peru and Tanzania found waist-to-hip preferences near 0.9 rather than the claimed universal 0.7, though later cross-cultural work using local stimuli, and studies of congenitally blind men, supports a low-ratio preference with local variation. Behaviors such as homosexuality and suicide reduce reproductive success and remain at least partially unexplained by current adaptive accounts.

## Political and ethical fallout

Critics such as Halford Fairchild have accused the field of cloaking racial or gender ideology in the language of science, and of implying current social arrangements are natural and therefore just. Evolutionary psychologists respond that describing what *is* does not entail what *ought* to be, a point philosophers call the naturalistic fallacy, the error of deriving moral conclusions from observations of nature. Pinker adds the mirror error, the "moralistic fallacy," of assuming that because something is bad, it cannot be natural. Empirical work by Dar-Nimrod et al. (2011) found that reading an evolutionary account of sexual aggression did not make men judge such acts more leniently; those given a sociocultural account judged them more harshly. Surveys of evolutionary psychology students in the United States find them predominantly left-liberal, undermining the stereotype that adaptationist researchers are politically conservative.
