# Behavior-based robotics

Behavior-based robotics (BBR), also called behavioral robotics, is an approach in which a robot produces complex-appearing behavior from a small internal state, without a detailed model of its environment. The robot continuously adjusts its actions through tight sensor–motor feedback, gradually correcting what it does as conditions change, so behavior emerges from this loop rather than from a stored world map.

The approach was developed in deliberate contrast to classical AI. Classical AI tends to follow a sequence of explicit reasoning steps grounded in internal representations of the world, then issues a plan. BBR instead treats biological systems as its model and relies on adaptability and sensory feedback rather than preset calculations, which made behavior-based robots cheap to build and useful for research and data gathering.

Most behavior-based systems are also reactive. A reactive robot carries no symbolic description of a chair, no category for floor type, and no stored map. Everything it knows about its current situation comes from its sensors this instant, and it incrementally corrects its actions as conditions change. Because no world model is maintained, the robot's actions look less deliberate than those of computationally heavy robots. A BBR often repeats motions, appears confused, sometimes fails, and yet shows a recognizable tenacity; comparisons between BBRs and insects are frequent for this reason. BBRs are sometimes considered examples of weak artificial intelligence, though some researchers have claimed they are models of all intelligence.

A behavior-based robot is normally launched with a small starting repertoire. Typical seed behaviors include obstacle avoidance and battery charging, which give the machine an immediate reason to move safely and to sustain itself. Rather than build a model, it reacts to environmental problems as they appear, drawing on internal knowledge accumulated from past sensorimotor experience together with those baseline behaviors to resolve new situations.

The intellectual lineage has three named milestones. The modern school is usually traced to the 1980s at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, where Rodney Brooks, working with students and colleagues, built a series of wheeled and legged robots organized by what he called the subsumption architecture. Brooks's papers carried deliberately provocative titles such as "Planning is just a way of avoiding figuring out what to do next," and the low cost and lifelike quality of his robots helped popularize the approach.

Two earlier pieces of work fed into that program. In the 1950s, W. Grey Walter, an English scientist with a background in neurological research, built a pair of vacuum-tube robots exhibited at the 1951 Festival of Britain; these machines already used simple but effective behavior-based control. The second milestone is Valentino Braitenberg's 1984 book *Vehicles: Experiments in Synthetic Psychology* (MIT Press), which uses thought experiments to show that very simple sensor–motor wirings can produce behaviors that look, from outside, like fear or love.

A more recent direction is BEAM robotics, which builds on Mark Tilden. Tilden started from Brooks's observation that a walking robot can run on a single microcontroller per leg and pushed the computational demand further down, onto logic chips, transistor-based electronics, and analog circuit design, so the resulting robots often have no programmable processor at all. Parallel work extends behavior-based robotics to multi-robot teams, where the research question is what minimal local mechanisms let a group coordinate, either implicitly through interaction with the environment or explicitly through communication, without giving any individual robot a full model of its teammates.
