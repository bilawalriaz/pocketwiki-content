# A Midsummer Night's Dream

A comedy by William Shakespeare, written around 1594–1596, that braids together four storylines set partly in Athens and partly in an enchanted nearby forest. The action hinges on the wedding of Duke Theseus to Hippolyta, queen of the Amazons, and uses fairies, mistaken identity, and a magical love potion to expose love as irrational, transformative, and unpredictable. Its lasting appeal lies in the way domestic quarrels, supernatural mischief, and a deliberately bad amateur play comment on one another.

## Four interlocking plots

1. **A royal marriage.** Theseus has conquered the Amazons and is preparing to marry Hippolyta. The pair also argue over an Indian changeling boy (a child secretly substituted for another) in Titania's keeping, which frames the play's quieter political questions about ownership and rule.
2. **Four young Athenians.** Hermia loves Lysander, but her father demands she marry Demetrius, who is loved by Helena. The lovers flee into the forest, where jealousy and confusion multiply.
3. **The Mechanicals.** A troupe of working-class amateurs, led by the carpenter Peter Quince and the weaver Nick Bottom, rehearse the tragic love story of Pyramus and Thisbe to perform at the wedding. Their earnest incompetence supplies the comic climax.
4. **The fairies.** King Oberon and queen Titania are estranged. To punish Titania, Oberon sends his sprite Puck to dab the juice of *love-in-idleness*, a magical flower, on her eyelids so she will fall in love with the first creature she sees on waking. The creature, after Puck's intervention, is Bottom, magically given a donkey's head.

## How the confusion unfolds

Puck, misidentifying the Athenian men in the dark, anoints Lysander instead of Demetrius. Lysander suddenly loves Helena, who thinks she is being mocked; Demetrius, later also enchanted, loves Helena too. The two men nearly duel. Titania wakes, sees ass-headed Bottom, and dotes on him. Oberon, amused and then remorseful, lifts the spells. The lovers are restored to their right pairs, Titania is reconciled with Oberon, the changeling boy is handed over, and the Mechanicals stage their absurd Pyramus and Thisbe before the royal wedding party.

The mechanism is precise: the flower only acts on the sleeper's first sight on waking, so identity, not affection, drives the chaos. Oberon's domestic quarrel with Titania causes the magical mishaps that engulf the human lovers; resolving the fairy quarrel is what allows the human plots to untangle.

## Themes and how critics have read them

Love is the play's controlling subject, presented less as romance than as a force that overrides reason, loyalty, and self-knowledge. David Bevington frames the drama as a contest between erotic desire and rational restraint. A second reading, endorsed by David Wiles and Harold Bloom, treats the play as a festive *carnivalesque* event, a temporary licensed overturning of social rank in which the fairy world loosens Athenian law. Maurice Hunt and Victor Kiernan stress how the forest dissolves individual identity, leaving characters unsure who they are or whom they want. Louis Montrose argues that female power, especially Titania's, is brought back under male control, while Leonard Tennenhouse contrasts rigid Athenian hierarchy with the fairy realm's lawless freedom. Jan Kott, reading the play in the twentieth century, emphasised the strain of violence and sexuality beneath the comic surface, and feminist and psychoanalytic critics have continued that work.

Time is deliberately fuzzy. References to a new moon and the May rites create a dreamlike, suspended present rather than a strict clock.

## Composition and early performance

Most scholars date the play to 1595 or very early 1596, partly because it alludes to Edmund Spenser's *Epithalamion* (1595), a wedding ode, and they often link it to an aristocratic wedding, possibly that of Thomas Berkeley and Elizabeth Carey, though the specific occasion is not certain. Shakespeare drew on Ovid's *Metamorphoses*, Chaucer's "The Knight's Tale," and folk material about fairies, without adapting any single source directly. Early performances took place at The Theatre and later at The Globe, with a recorded court performance at Hampton Court on 1 January 1604.

The text reached print piecemeal: a first quarto (Q1) appeared in 1600, entered into the Stationers' Register by Thomas Fisher on 8 October 1600, after Francis Meres had listed the play in his 1598 *Palladis Tamia*. A second quarto (Q2) followed in 1619, and the play was gathered into the First Folio of 1623. Samuel Pepys called it "the most insipid ridiculous play" in 1662; Henry Purcell's 1692 adaptation *The Fairy-Queen* turned it into a musical showcase. Madame Vestris restored the full text at Covent Garden in 1840, and the landmark twentieth-century productions by Max Reinhardt (1935) and Peter Brook for the Royal Shakespeare Company (1970) marked a turn from lavish cuts toward closer, more conceptual readings of the script.

## Unresolved questions

The exact year and any specific wedding occasion remain uncertain. Critics disagree about whether love in the play is fundamentally benign or dark and destructive. Theseus is read both as a figure of cold patriarchal reason and, in his speech on "the lunatic, the lover, and the poet," as a defender of imaginative vision. Oberon's desire for the Indian changeling boy has been read politically, as a power struggle, and psychologically, as a sublimated wish. The play's inconsistent internal chronology, since the new moon and the May rite cannot both fit a single four-day span, is variously treated as a flaw, a deliberate dream effect, or a reference to a "dark of the moon" interval.
