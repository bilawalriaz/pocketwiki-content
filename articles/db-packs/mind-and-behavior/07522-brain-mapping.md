# Brain mapping

Brain mapping is the practice of turning measurements of the brain into spatial pictures of anatomy, activity, or connection patterns, layered so structure and function can be compared across individuals and species. All neuroimaging counts as brain mapping in a loose sense, but the term usually means higher-order products that combine raw scans with further analysis, such as maps projecting behaviour onto anatomy, wiring diagrams of every neuron, or atlases a researcher can query like a database.

A few working definitions keep the rest coherent. A **voxel** is the smallest three-dimensional box a scanner measures, often containing hundreds of thousands of neurons. A **connectome** is a map of every neural connection in a defined region or whole brain, dense enough to count individual synapses. A **connectogram** is a circular visualisation: cortical regions arrayed around a ring, concentric circles encoding measurements such as thickness or curvature, and white-matter fibres drawn as lines whose weight reflects connection strength. A **brain atlas** is a standardised reference, usually a template brain, that lets laboratories compare their data against a common coordinate system.

The toolbox is broad. Structural and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), diffusion MRI (dMRI), magnetoencephalography (MEG), electroencephalography (EEG), positron emission tomography (PET), and near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) supply non-invasive views of anatomy, physiology, perfusion, and function in healthy and diseased brains. Microscopy-based methods (immunohistochemistry, electron microscopy) and molecular tools (optogenetics, cellular and stem-cell biology, nanotechnology) push the work to single cells and synapses.

The field has a credibility problem. Headlines about "the part of the brain responsible" for love, memory, or music are usually overstated. A typical voxel pools hundreds of thousands of neurons, and most functions are distributed across regions rather than localised to one, so a clean single-region claim is rarely defensible. Many underlying studies also have small samples, poor calibration, and poor reproducibility, and the same methods are sometimes sold commercially for lie detection or unsupported medical diagnosis.

The modern era began in the late 1980s when the US Institute of Medicine was asked to integrate neuroscience across techniques. A series of meetings produced the International Consortium for Brain Mapping (ICBM), and parallel efforts grew into the Human Brain Project, both aiming at flexible computational atlases.

Milestones have moved the field from coarse slabs to countable cells. In 2012 the citizen-science site Eyewire opened mouse retinal cells for public tracing. In 2021 Google and collaborators published the most detailed 3D human-brain map to date: a one-cubic-millimetre fragment of cortex, sliced into roughly 5,300 sections about 30 nanometres thick, scanned by electron microscope, stored in 1.4 petabytes. A Chinese team reported a neuron-level 3D map of a whole monkey brain in 100 hours, with only a fraction released because the full dataset exceeds a petabyte even compressed. The BRAIN Initiative Cell Census Network closed its first phase that October with seventeen studies, including a cell-type atlas of the mouse primary motor cortex.

In 2024 the FlyWire consortium, 287 researchers across 76 institutions, published the first complete connectome of an adult *Drosophila melanogaster* in *Nature*. Primary data came from electron microscopy; artificial intelligence did the initial tracing and citizen scientists corrected its errors. The model contains more than 140,000 neurons and over 50 million synapses. The only prior adult animal with a fully reconstructed brain was the nematode *C. elegans*, far smaller, so the fly map is the first complete wiring diagram of a complex brain, and is expected to support digital-twin simulations of how neural segments respond to stimuli.

Other 2021 work tracked brains over time. Eight isogenic *C. elegans* were mapped at different developmental stages to produce the first connectome following an animal's brain across its lifetime, and a separate study combined electron microscopy with Brainbow labelling to chart mammalian neuromuscular wiring in ten mice. The MICrONS programme released a functional connectomics dataset covering roughly 75,000 neurons across the mouse primary visual cortex and three higher visual areas, with the MICrONS Explorer exposing interactive visualisations across all six cortical layers. In 2022 a spatiotemporal transcriptomic atlas of the axolotl brain identified the progenitor cells involved in regeneration.

Standard atlases now in use include the Talairach Atlas (1988), the Harvard Whole Brain Atlas (1995), the MNI Template (1998, the reference for SPM and the ICBM), the Atlas of the Developing Human Brain (2012), and the Infant Brain Atlas (2023), which together provide the coordinate frames new maps are aligned against.
