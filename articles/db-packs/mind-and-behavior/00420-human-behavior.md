# Human behavior

Human behavior is the response of individuals and groups to internal and external stimuli across the lifespan, produced by a continuous interaction of genes, brain, environment, and culture. The "nature versus nurture" framing has given way to an interactionist view: inherited predispositions raise the likelihood of certain behaviors, while experience and cultural setting shape how those predispositions are expressed.

## What shapes behavior

Four layers operate at once. **Biology** supplies the substrate: hormones, neurotransmitters, and brain regions set baselines for emotion, motivation, and self-control. The **ventromedial prefrontal cortex (VMPFC)** integrates feeling and reasoning for moral decisions, the amygdala drives threat responses, and the nucleus accumbens underlies reward and habit formation. **Genetics** contributes roughly 50% of the variance for many behavioral traits and disorders; genes create probabilities, not destinies, and their effects depend on environment, so a gene linked to antisocial behavior often activates only with childhood maltreatment. **Environment and learning** work through **neuroplasticity**, the brain's ability to rewire with experience. Humans uniquely build **cultural accumulation**: knowledge stacks across generations through language, teaching, and imitation, so each generation inherits tools and norms it did not have to invent. **Culture and norms** are the shared layer: **social norms** are unwritten expectations enforced by approval, stigma, or exclusion. Cultures differ on individualism versus collectivism, and most published psychology comes from WEIRD samples (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) that are under 12% of humanity but over 95% of research participants, which limits how universal the findings really are.

## How behavior develops

Behavior changes systematically with age. Infants form **attachment** bonds with caregivers that act as internal working models for later relationships. **Theory of mind** (understanding that others have beliefs and perspectives different from one's own) emerges around age 4–5. Adolescence brings prefrontal reorganization, risk-taking, and identity work; about 1 in 7 adolescents worldwide face mental health challenges. Adulthood balances intimacy, career, and **generativity** (contributing to the next generation). In later life, socioemotional selectivity theory holds that older adults prioritize emotionally meaningful ties.

## The social animal

Humans coordinate through **in-groups** (cooperation) and **out-groups** (competition), which produces conformity, obedience to authority, and sometimes hostility to outsiders. **Deviance** is behavior that violates a group's norms; punishment aims to deter harm or enforce a worldview. In groups, **deindividuation** (loss of self-awareness) and moral disengagement can license aggression. Romantic love, family ties, and pair bonding draw on evolved attachment systems but vary widely across cultures, including in whether marriage is monogamous, polygynous, or polyandrous.

## Cognition, emotion, and bias

Most reasoning is automatic. **Deductive** reasoning draws certain conclusions from premises; **inductive** reasoning draws probable conclusions from patterns. Both are skewed by **cognitive biases** (confirmation bias, anchoring, availability) that account for 30–45% of judgment errors. Basic emotions (joy, fear, anger) appear universal; complex emotions such as guilt and pride are shaped by culture. Each person has a **hedonic set point** (a baseline level of happiness) adjusted modestly by health and relationships. **Addiction** hijacks the dopamine reward system, moving behavior from impulsive to compulsive.

## Moral behavior

Morality arises from emotional intuitions and rational deliberation working together, with the VMPFC as a key hub. **Kohlberg's stages** move from obedience in childhood toward universal principles in adulthood. **Moral Foundations Theory** identifies six recurring concerns: care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, and liberty, weighted differently across cultures. Situational forces such as the **bystander effect** (people help less when others are present) can override personal values.

## Antisocial and criminal behavior

Aggression and crime emerge from genetic risk, childhood trauma, and brain imbalances where limbic reactions overpower prefrontal regulation. Averages include 1–2 lies per day. Fraud and conspiracy beliefs exploit authority bias, social proof, and confirmation bias. Modern exploitation extends to **surveillance capitalism**, the conversion of lived experience into behavioral data sold for prediction. **Antisocial personality disorder and psychopathy** involve deficits in empathy and remorse; radicalization combines a quest for significance with extremist networks.

## Economic and digital behavior

Work shifted from hunter-gatherer subsistence, through agricultural surplus and labor specialization after the Neolithic Revolution around 10,000 BC, to factory labor during the Industrial Revolution, and now to service and knowledge work. Decisions are **reference-dependent** (judged against a status quo) and **loss-averse** (losses feel larger than equivalent gains). In the digital layer, people average 6.5 hours online daily. Heavy social media use is linked to brain changes in reward regions, accelerated cerebellum development in children aged 9–11, and 2–3 times higher odds of perceived social isolation, especially in young adults. Global smartphone addiction sits near 6.3%, rising above 80% in some high-use subgroups such as medical students. Cyberbullying victimization among youth ranges globally from about 14% to 57%, with image- and video-based harassment causing the most stress.

## Gender, sex, and identity

Sex, gender identity, gender expression, and sexual orientation each run on continua rather than binaries. Gender identity is usually stable by ages 3–5. Many cultures recognize non-binary categories: hijra in South Asia, fa'afafine in Samoa, Two-Spirit among some Native American groups. The **fraternal birth order effect** shows that each older biological brother raises the odds of homosexuality in men by roughly 33%, likely through a maternal immune response. **Minority stress theory** explains the elevated depression, anxiety, and suicide rates in LGBTQ+ populations as outcomes of chronic stigma rather than identity itself; family, peer, and community support are protective.

## Open questions

The relative weight of nature, nurture, structure, and free will remains debated. WEIRD-based findings may not generalize globally. Whether heavy digital media causes distress or simply attracts already-vulnerable users is unresolved, and long-term neurodevelopmental effects on children are unknown. Moral universals are proposed, but their cultural weighting varies sharply. The gene-environment pathways to psychopathy and extreme violence are only partly mapped, so prediction and prevention stay limited.
