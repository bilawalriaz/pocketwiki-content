# Cognitive epidemiology

Cognitive epidemiology studies the link between intelligence test scores (IQ, or the underlying "g-factor" extracted from a battery of mental tests) and later health. Across countries, decades, and millions of subjects, higher early-life IQ predicts lower mortality and lower rates of many illnesses. A meta-analysis found a 24% increase in mortality for every one-standard-deviation (15-point) drop in IQ, and a one-million-Swedish-men study found a strong link between cognitive ability and risk of death. The association holds for most major causes of death, with the main exceptions being cancers unrelated to smoking and suicide.

## Patterns by condition

**Cardiovascular.** Higher childhood IQ predicts lower risk of coronary heart disease and stroke, and the link persists after controlling for income and education. Visuospatial reasoning is the strongest single predictor of stroke. Atherosclerosis (thickening of artery walls from fatty deposits) is also tied to lower IQ.

**Metabolic and other physical illness.** A 15-point IQ increase is associated with about a 24% lower risk of adult obesity, and childhood IQ inversely predicts later blood pressure. Most studies find no consistent link between IQ and cancer overall, though some show higher IQ linked to skin cancer. Lower childhood IQ is also tied to higher rates of long-term sick leave and disability pensions.

**Psychiatric.** Lower childhood IQ is associated with higher later risk of schizophrenia, major depression, and generalized anxiety disorder. One large Swedish study found that the relationship between IQ and schizophrenia is not explained by shared genes or family environment, and that high IQ may be partly protective. In contrast, very high IQ is linked to higher rates of pure bipolar disorder without other psychiatric illness, with risk following a J-curve (risk falls as IQ rises, then rises again at the extreme high end). Schizophrenia patients average about 0.35 standard deviations below the population mean before symptoms appear, with further decline after onset.

**Substance use.** Higher childhood IQ is associated with greater alcohol consumption and illegal drug use (cannabis, cocaine, ecstasy, amphetamines) in adulthood, independent of socioeconomic status. A 15-point IQ increase is linked to higher drug use.

**Dementia.** Low childhood cognitive ability predicts late-onset Alzheimer's and other dementias up to a decade before symptoms, and childhood scores are significantly lower in those who later develop dementia.

## Why the link exists

Two competing hypotheses dominate. The **system integrity hypothesis** (Deary) treats childhood IQ as one marker of an underlying latent trait, where a well-developed body, including its brain, is also a healthy body. Markers of system integrity include speed of information processing, physical coordination, and metabolic health. The **evolutionary novelty hypothesis** (Kanazawa) argues that intelligence evolved to handle situations outside our ancestral environment, so higher-IQ individuals are better at recognizing modern hazards such as smoking, poor diet, and lack of exercise and avoiding them. The two make different predictions: system integrity predicts protection against genetically driven disease, while evolutionary novelty predicts protection against lifestyle-driven disease. Limited evidence favours the novelty account for non-heritable cancers.

Behavioural pathways explain much of the link. People with higher IQ tend to acquire more education, enter safer occupations, exercise more, eat better, smoke and drink less, persist with medication, and use preventive healthcare. They also have higher health literacy (the ability to read, understand, and act on health information), which is itself almost entirely explained by IQ. In one trial, higher-IQ participants were more likely to keep taking daily aspirin over a two-year cardiovascular study. The Scottish Lothian study found that in old age, the relative decline in cognitive abilities from age 11 to 79, particularly fluid abilities (on-the-spot reasoning), better predicted survival than current IQ scores.

## Limits

The relationship is observational, so causal direction is debated, though most studies use childhood IQ measured before illness, ruling out reverse causation for adult disease. Effects are graded rather than threshold-based, appearing across the IQ range, and associations with cancer, alcohol, and drugs remain inconsistent across cohorts and countries. One proposed extension is a general "f-factor" of fitness that would combine intelligence, health, and fertility into a single heritable dimension, analogous to g.
