# Brain abscess

A brain abscess is a walled-off pocket of pus inside brain tissue, formed when bacteria, fungi, or parasites reach the brain from a source elsewhere in the body. It behaves like a slowly enlarging mass: it raises pressure inside the rigid skull, destroys local neural tissue, and triggers inflammation around itself.

## Where the infection comes from

Brain abscesses almost never arise de novo inside the brain. A primary focus of infection elsewhere is almost always present, and failure to find and treat it leads to relapse. Three routes feed organisms in:

- Contiguous spread from nearby structures: the middle ear, the mastoid air cells of the temporal bone, the frontal or ethmoid paranasal sinuses, or a dental abscess. Ear infections tend to seed the middle and posterior cranial fossae; frontal and ethmoid sinus infections tend to collect near the subdural sinuses.
- Hematogenous spread from remote sites such as the lung, heart, or kidney. Children with congenital heart disease and right-to-left shunts are especially prone to this route, with abscesses typically landing in the distribution of the middle cerebral artery.
- Direct inoculation through compound skull fractures, penetrating head trauma, or neurosurgical procedures. Retained bone fragments or foreign bodies act as ongoing sources of infection if not removed.

## Microbiology

Most abscesses are polymicrobial. Predominant isolates are anaerobic and microaerophilic cocci plus gram-positive and gram-negative anaerobic bacilli, commonly *Staphylococcus aureus*, aerobic and anaerobic streptococci (especially *Streptococcus intermedius*), *Bacteroides*, *Prevotella*, *Fusobacterium*, Enterobacteriaceae, and *Pseudomonas*. Less common agents include *Haemophilus influenzae*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, and *Neisseria meningitidis*.

Fungi and parasites mainly affect immunocompromised hosts. In patients with AIDS, *Toxoplasma gondii*, *Cryptococcus neoformans*, and poliovirus are the most frequently associated organisms, although *Cryptococcus* usually presents as meningitis rather than a discrete abscess. The likely organism tracks with the source:

- Sinus or dental infection: aerobic and anaerobic streptococci, anaerobic gram-negative bacilli (*Prevotella*, *Porphyromonas*, *Bacteroides*), *Fusobacterium*, *S. aureus*, Enterobacteriaceae.
- Penetrating trauma: *S. aureus*, aerobic streptococci, Enterobacteriaceae, *Clostridium*.
- Pulmonary infection: aerobic and anaerobic streptococci, anaerobic gram-negative bacilli, *Fusobacterium*, *Actinomyces*, *Nocardia*.
- Congenital heart disease: aerobic and microaerophilic streptococci, *S. aureus*.
- HIV infection: *T. gondii*, *Mycobacterium*, *Nocardia*, *Cryptococcus*, *Listeria monocytogenes*.
- Transplantation: *Aspergillus*, *Candida*, *Cryptococcus*, *Mucorales*, *Nocardia*, *T. gondii*.
- Neutropenia: aerobic gram-negative bacilli, *Aspergillus*, *Candida*, *Mucorales*.

## Symptoms

The classic triad of fever, headache, and focal neurological deficit appears in only about 20% of patients. The most common features are headache, drowsiness, confusion, seizures, hemiparesis, and speech difficulties, usually with fever and a rapidly progressive course.

Headache is characteristically worse at night and in the morning because intracranial pressure rises naturally in the supine position; the same pressure elevation triggers the medullary vomiting center and area postrema, producing morning vomiting. Other findings depend on location: a cerebellar abscess can compress the brainstem and cause hydrocephalus, and a stiff neck can occasionally mimic meningitis.

## How the lesion evolves

The lesion begins as an unencapsulated area of inflamed brain called cerebritis. Over roughly four to five days, inflammation and dead brain tissue become surrounded by a capsule.

This capsule produces the ring-enhancing lesion seen on contrast CT or MRI. Intravenous contrast cannot cross the capsule and pools around it, forming a bright rim around a darker center. Cerebral hemorrhage and some brain tumors can produce the same appearance, so the scan is interpreted in clinical context: a rapidly progressive course with fever, focal neurological signs, and raised intracranial pressure points to abscess.

Lumbar puncture is contraindicated because withdrawing cerebrospinal fluid can shift pressure gradients across the skull and precipitate brain herniation.

## Treatment

Standard management lowers intracranial pressure, starts intravenous antibiotics guided by blood culture and the suspected source, drains the abscess surgically, and treats the primary infection site, including removal of any foreign material.

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy is used as primary and adjunct treatment. It lowers intracranial pressure, delivers high partial pressures of oxygen that are bactericidal to the anaerobes common in brain abscess, supports host immune function, and helps when cranial osteomyelitis coexists.

Two conditions are exceptions to routine surgical drainage. *Haemophilus influenzae* meningitis is often accompanied by subdural effusions that resemble subdural empyemas but resolve with antibiotics alone. Tuberculous abscesses can look identical to bacterial ones on imaging; aspiration is still needed to identify *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, but once diagnosed they are managed medically.

CT-guided stereotactic aspiration is the standard surgical technique.

## Outcome

Death occurs in about 10% of cases and roughly 70% of patients recover well, a substantial improvement over the 1960s driven by better brain imaging, more effective neurosurgery, and more effective antibiotics.
