# Cognitive biology

Cognitive biology treats natural cognition as a biological function, on a par with respiration or metabolism, rather than as a uniquely human capacity of brains or minds. Its working assumption is that any organism able to sense a stimulus and produce an appropriate response is engaged in cognition, what theorists call *sensorimotor coupling*: a loop in which the organism senses, selects a response from its repertoire, and acts.

A few related terms need distinguishing. *Cognitive science* is the interdisciplinary study of mind, which since the 1970s has mainly meant human thought and the conscious mind. *Cognitive ethology* is the study of animal thinking and communication in natural settings, proposed by Donald Griffin in 1978. *Autopoiesis* is the doctrine developed in 1970 by Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela that living systems are self-producing networks; their work belongs to the Chilean (Santiago) School, sometimes called "biology of cognition," a phrase that should not be conflated with cognitive biology.

Because Darwinian theory traces all species to a common root, three principles follow: studying one species illuminates another by comparison, simpler cognitive systems should be tackled before more complex ones, and a wider taxonomic sample yields a better general theory of what cognition is. Bacteria already exhibit the problems long familiar to cognitive scientists: integrating multiple sensory inputs, deciding under uncertainty, signalling honestly or deceptively to neighbours, and coordinating collective behaviour. Comparable capacities are now documented in plants and other single-celled organisms, so the phenomenon appears to scale continuously from microbes to mammals.

History. The term was coined by Brian C. Goodwin in a 1977 paper, where he tied cognition to morphogenesis, the biological generation of form. Without sustained advocacy, the idea remained marginal. In 1986 the Slovak biochemist Ladislav Kováč published an "Introduction to cognitive biology" grounded in bioenergetics and molecular biology, and over the following decades he elaborated a set of "Principles of Cognitive Biology" drawing on thermodynamics, information theory, and the energy flows that sustain living cells. His work had more influence in Slovakia than elsewhere, partly because several key papers were published only in Slovak. By the 1990s molecular, cell, and evolutionary biology were producing a flood of data relevant to cognition, but no other major figure was developing the field. A 2007 conference paper by Pamela Lyon and Jonathan Opie, "Prolegomena for a cognitive biology," framed the discipline as still in its founding stage: a science dedicated to cognition across all organisms had yet to be established, and there was no consensus on theory or method.

Institutional practice. Cognitive biology is currently pursued through three overlapping approaches. First, the comparative method used in William Bechtel's 2013 UC San Diego seminar, which studied cognitive activities in organisms that cognitive science traditionally ignores, including bacteria, planaria, leeches, fruit flies, bees, birds, and rodents, and drew lessons back to human cognition. Second, the biogenic approach of the University of Adelaide workgroup, which assumes cognition is a biological function similar to respiration, treats simple model systems (microbes, worms, flies) as entry points, and traces homologous mechanisms upward to crows, primates, and humans. Pamela Lyon's 2006 paper identified ten principles of biogenesis, the first four being that complex cognition evolved from simpler forms along a continuous line of descent; that cognition modulates an organism's physico-chemical and electrical processes; that it enables reciprocal causal exchanges with the environment needed for persistence and replication; and that it continuously assesses system needs against prevailing circumstances and current effectiveness. Third, cognitive phylogenetics, exemplified by the University of Vienna's Department of Kognitionsbiologie, embeds cognitive mechanisms in a phylogenetic framework, treating evolutionary history, adaptive function, and mechanism (neural, hormonal, or otherwise) as inseparable. A narrower variant at Otto-von-Guericke University in Magdeburg studies multistable perception, in which certain visual displays appear to waver between distinct interpretations. A 2006 workshop at the Konrad Lorenz Institute led to a 2009 volume, *Cognitive Biology: Evolutionary and Developmental Perspectives on Mind, Brain and Behavior*, whose participants proposed the label to capture the merger of the cognitive and life sciences. Gennaro Auletta's 2011 *Cognitive Biology: Dealing with Information from Bacteria to Minds* offers a complementary methodology using cybernetics and quantum information theory as formal tools for biology.

As a category, "cognitive biology" remains unstable. Some publishers list it as a sub-topic of cognitive science alongside psychology, linguistics, and neuroscience; others use it as a heading for biological work on information processing, animal learning, evo-devo, or cognitive ethology. The label is best read as a working name for an interdisciplinary project unified by the claim that cognition is a biological function distributed across all life, not a defined body of doctrine. The Comenius University center in Bratislava, founded by Kováč, continues to pursue his program in biochemistry-grounded cognitive biology.
