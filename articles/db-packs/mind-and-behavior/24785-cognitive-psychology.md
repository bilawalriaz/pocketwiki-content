# Cognitive psychology

Cognitive psychology is the scientific study of mental processes such as attention, language, memory, perception, problem solving, and reasoning. It treats the mind as an information-processing system that transforms, stores, and retrieves sensory input to guide behavior.

## Origins

From the 1920s to the 1950s, behaviorism dominated psychology and held that unobservable mental events lay beyond empirical study. Cognitive psychology emerged in the 1960s when four converging forces challenged that view. World War II research on human performance, especially Donald Broadbent's integration of attention with information theory, showed that mental processing could be measured. The computer metaphor developed by Allen Newell and Herbert Simon framed the mind as a system that encodes, stores, and retrieves information. Noam Chomsky's 1959 critique of behaviorism showed that language learning could not be explained by stimulus and response alone. Jean Piaget's earlier work on how children think supplied a developmental model of cognition. Ulric Neisser's 1967 book *Cognitive Psychology* named the field and defined cognition as all processes by which sensory input is transformed, reduced, elaborated, stored, recovered, and used.

## Core mental processes

**Attention** is focused awareness on a subset of incoming sensory information. The brain cannot consciously handle all auditory, visual, olfactory, taste, and tactile input at once, so attention filters out irrelevant data. Exogenous (bottom-up) control produces orienting reflexes and pop-out effects. Endogenous (top-down) control handles divided attention and conscious processing. In dichotic listening, where different messages reach each ear, listeners can shadow one stream yet still notice their own name or a pitch change in the other, a result known as the cocktail party effect.

**Memory** has three stages. Sensory memory briefly holds raw input; short-term memory holds a small amount of information for active analysis; long-term memory stores information over extended periods. Short-term memory is now understood as **working memory**, a temporary, capacity-limited system for holding and manipulating information. George Miller proposed a capacity of about7 items, later revised by Nelson Cowan to about 4. Hermann Ebbinghaus's forgetting experiments showed steep memory decay within 20 minutes, evidence for a separate short-term store. The Baddeley-Hitch working memory model adds visual and auditory subsystems coordinated by a central executive that draws on long-term memory. Modern evidence favors interference over decay as the main cause of forgetting in short-term memory.

**Long-term memory** has three subtypes. Procedural memory stores stimulus-response skills activated unconsciously, such as driving. Semantic memory stores factual knowledge whose access depends on recency, associations, and depth of encoding. Episodic memory stores autobiographical events by binding semantic memory to temporal context.

**Perception** combines the physical senses with cognitive interpretation. Early structuralists such as Edward B. Titchener tried to reduce perception to basic elements; modern work examines how people interpret and navigate their environment, including research at the University of Connecticut's Center for the Ecological Study of Perception and Action.

**Language** has been studied since Carl Wernicke's 1870s model. Broca's area handles language production; Wernicke's area handles comprehension; damage to either produces Broca's or Wernicke's aphasia. Contemporary research covers language acquisition, phonemes (the basic sound units of speech), and links between language and mood.

**Metacognition** is thinking about one's own thinking: monitoring performance, judging one's capabilities, and choosing strategies. Strengthening students' metacognitive skills improves learning. Related phenomena include déjà vu, cryptomnesia (unconscious plagiarism), the false fame effect, the validity effect (repeated statements seeming more true), and imagination inflation.

## Modern perspective: dual-process theory

Daniel Kahneman's2011 framework distinguishes System 1, which is fast, automatic, and emotionally colored, from System 2, which is slow, deliberate, and vulnerable to conscious bias. The contrast has been applied chiefly to reasoning and decision-making, though not all cognitive psychologists accept it.

## Applications and influence

Cognitive psychology shapes several applied fields. Aaron T. Beck developed cognitive behavior therapy (CBT) for depression, arguing that only about 60 to 65 percent of patients respond to antidepressants, that many who respond stop taking them, and that long-term medication use may weaken learned coping. Social cognition applies cognitive methods to interpersonal perception; Kenneth Dodge's social information-processing model links how children interpret social cues to aggressive behavior. In developmental psychology, Theory of Mind, the capacity to attribute independent mental states to others, typically emerges between ages 4 and 6 and is itself a metacognitive achievement. In education, the distinction between declarative (factual) and procedural (skill-based) knowledge and hierarchical organization of information both derive from cognitive research. Cognitive therapeutic approaches also treat personality disorders by targeting faulty schemata (stable mental patterns) and judgmental biases.

## Relationship to cognitive science and debates

Cognitive psychology forms the core of cognitive science, which also draws on neuroscience, linguistics, philosophy, artificial intelligence, and anthropology; some scholars use the two terms interchangeably. Critics argue that the field lacks cohesion, with competing models and little agreement on its domain, and that it over-focuses on the internal mind. The 4E cognition movement responds that cognition is embodied, embedded, extended, and enacted. Older behaviorist objections that internal mental states are unobservable have been weakened by cognitive neuroscience, which continues to link brain activity with mental states, though neuropsychologists and cognitive psychologists still disagree. Mental model theory (reasoning as image-based) and mental logic theory (reasoning as verbal) each find partial support; evidence suggests the operative system depends on whether a task is visuospatial or linguistic, and neither account is complete.
