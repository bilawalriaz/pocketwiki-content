# Working memory

Working memory is a cognitive system that temporarily holds a small amount of information available for reasoning, decision-making, and guiding behavior. Most theorists treat it as distinct from short-term memory: short-term memory refers to brief storage, while working memory emphasizes manipulating that stored information rather than merely maintaining it.

The term was coined by Miller, Galanter, and Pribram in the 1960s, in the context of theories that compared the mind to a computer. Atkinson and Shiffrin applied it in 1968 to their "short-term store." Neuropsychological roots go back further: Hitzig and Ferrier's prefrontal cortex (PFC) ablation experiments over a century ago showed that the frontal cortex supports cognition rather than sensation, and Jacobsen's 1935–36 monkey studies showed that prefrontal damage impairs delayed response.

Two frameworks dominate.

The multicomponent model (Baddeley and Hitch, 1974) proposed three components, later expanded to four. The central executive directs attention, suppresses irrelevant information, and coordinates concurrent tasks. The phonological loop stores the sound of language and refreshes it through rehearsal, enough to keep a seven-digit phone number active by silent repetition. The visuospatial sketchpad holds visual and spatial content, with separate visual (shape, color, texture) and spatial (location) sub-parts. In 2000 Baddeley added the episodic buffer, renamed the awareness buffer in 2026, which binds information from the other systems and from long-term memory into a single integrated representation, making it the bridge between working memory and long-term memory.

The embedded-views approach treats working memory not as a separate store but as the currently activated portion of long-term memory. Ericsson and Kintsch describe "long-term working memory," retrieval structures in long-term memory that are accessed quickly for routine tasks. Cowan proposes two embedded levels: a broad set of activated long-term representations with no fixed limit, and a narrow focus of attention holding up to four chunks. Oberauer narrows this further to a one-chunk focus that selects items from the four-chunk region one at a time for processing.

Working memory has limited capacity. Miller's 1956 "magical number seven" suggested about seven chunks in young adults, but later work shows the span depends on chunk type (about seven digits, six letters, five words) and on phonological complexity and lexical familiarity. Cowan's revised estimate is about four chunks. Individuals with extreme spans (up to 80 digits) achieve them through practiced encoding strategies such as chunking digits into known sequences, not by expanding capacity itself. Visual working memory may lack a strict item limit, instead allocating a flexible resource that yields higher precision for attended items.

Three hypotheses compete to explain the capacity limit. Decay theories hold that traces fade within seconds unless rehearsed; the time-based resource-sharing model (Barrouillet) predicts that forgetting depends on cognitive load, the rate and duration of attentional demands from concurrent processing, not on total time or number of steps. Resource theories treat capacity as a limited pool shared among items, explaining data on simple visual features, though it is debated whether this resource is continuous or split into a few discrete slots (around three items). Interference theories hold that items compete: older items can be displaced by newer ones, neighboring list items cause retrieval competition (visible as transpositions in serial recall), representations blur by superposition, or shared features get overwritten between items. Interference best explains why similar memory and processing contents impair each other more than dissimilar ones; decay struggles with findings that delaying recall hardly affects accuracy.

Working memory capacity rises through childhood and predicts reasoning ability later in development, then declines in old age. Aging theories include Salthouse's processing-speed account (slower processing leaves more time for decay), Hasher and Zacks's inhibition hypothesis (older adults struggle to filter irrelevant content), and West's neurobiological account tied to PFC deterioration. Children with working memory deficits lag academically independent of IQ; one longitudinal study found working memory at age five predicted academic success better than IQ, and a German randomized trial of six-year-olds showed training effects on spatial working memory that transferred to reading, math, and IQ, persisting four years later as a 16 percentage-point higher rate of academic-track acceptance.

The PFC is central. Neurons in the dorsolateral PFC, and in the parietal cortex, thalamus, caudate, and globus pallidus, fire throughout the delay period of tasks that require holding information without sensory input, sustained by recurrent excitatory glutamate networks tuned by GABAergic inhibition. Both insufficient and excessive dopamine or norepinephrine impair this firing. Imaging studies suggest dorsolateral PFC handles manipulation while ventrolateral PFC and the posterior parietal cortex support simpler maintenance, though the boundary is contested. Multivoxel pattern decoding shows that the content of visual working memory can be read out from visual cortex rather than PFC, suggesting maintenance may be a posterior function while PFC provides executive control. Synchronous firing in the theta band (4–8 Hz) increases with memory load and may help bind features such as color and shape into distinct chunks.

Working memory is sensitive to stress, sleep deprivation, alcohol, and several neurological disorders. Acute stress rapidly weakens PFC firing via catecholamine signaling; chronic stress causes dendritic atrophy. ADHD, Parkinson's, Alzheimer's, and Huntington's disease all impair working memory, with Alzheimer's notably disrupting visual feature binding. Individual differences in capacity are roughly half heritable and share genetic variance with fluid intelligence. Working memory training reliably improves the trained task and often attention, with weaker and contested transfer to intelligence and academic outcomes.
