# Evolutionary psychology

Evolutionary psychology treats the human mind as a collection of evolved information-processing mechanisms, in the same way evolutionary biology treats the body. Just as the heart evolved to pump blood, language acquisition, cheater detection, mate choice, and kin recognition evolved to solve recurrent problems faced by ancestors. Traits are classified as adaptations (solutions to ancestral problems), exaptations (repurposed traits), by-products of other adaptations (sometimes called "spandrels"), or random variation. George Williams' criterion identifies an adaptation by improbable complexity, species universality, and adaptive functionality. The view that the mind consists of many specialized mechanisms is called the modularity of mind.

The discipline traces to Charles Darwin, who in *On the Origin of Species* (1859) predicted that psychology would one day rest on an evolutionary foundation, and who later published *The Descent of Man* (1871) and *The Expression of the Emotions* (1872). Modern evolutionary psychology was established by Donald Symons' *The Evolution of Human Sexuality* (1979) and Leda Cosmides and John Tooby's *The Adapted Mind* (1992). Key influences include W. D. Hamilton's inclusive fitness theory (a gene-centred view in which altruism toward relatives evolves when the benefit, weighted by relatedness, exceeds the cost, summarized as Hamilton's rule: rb > c) and Robert Trivers's work on reciprocity and parental investment.

The brain is assumed to contain many domain-specific psychological adaptations shaped by natural and sexual selection, not a domain-general learning device, because general-purpose learning is rejected as computationally infeasible (a "combinatorial explosion"). These mechanisms evolved in the environment of evolutionary adaptedness (EEA), which corresponds to Pleistocene hunter-gatherer conditions, since *Homo* appeared 1.5 to 2.5 million years ago and the Pleistocene ended only about 12,000 years ago. Mismatch between ancient adaptations and modern environments explains phenomena such as fear of spiders and snakes (which were dangerous to ancestors) far exceeding fear of guns, and supernormal stimuli like junk food overshooting evolved cravings for salt, sugar, and fat.

Sexual selection follows from anisogamy, the difference in gamete size. Because female mammals invest more per offspring through gestation and lactation, they tend to be choosier about mates, while males compete for access. The Trivers-Willard hypothesis proposes that parents in good conditions invest more in sons, while parents in poor conditions invest more in daughters. Universally observed traits are prime candidates for adaptations, including basic emotions (fear, anger, disgust, happiness, sadness), incest avoidance, cheater detection, and sex-differentiated jealousy: men are more distressed by sexual infidelity, women by emotional infidelity, reflecting male paternity uncertainty. The FOXP2 gene, unique to humans, arose roughly 100,000 to 200,000 years ago, though the idea that it is a "grammar gene" is now widely discredited.

Research topics include perception, which evolved primarily to guide action rather than produce knowledge and consumes more than half the brain; learning, treated as a facultative adaptation shaped by environmental input; personality, viewed as variation around adaptive optima, frequency-dependent strategies, or developmentally sensitive responses; coalition behaviour and altruistic punishment of in-group norm violators; the Cinderella effect (higher abuse rates for stepchildren); depression, viewed by some as adaptive withdrawal; and antisocial behaviour linked to male status competition.

Criticism centres on testability, the speculative nature of EEA reconstructions, accusations of genetic determinism and pan-adaptationism, and ethically troubling implications. Some critics argue that internal contradictions weaken specific adaptive scenarios, while evolutionary psychologists caution against the naturalistic fallacy, the assumption that what is natural is therefore good.
