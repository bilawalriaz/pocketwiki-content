# Cognition

Cognition is the set of mental processes that handle knowledge: acquiring, storing, retrieving, transforming, and using information. These processes let people (and other organisms) interpret experience and respond to the world. They are usually divided into basic functions (perception, attention, memory, language) and higher-order functions (reasoning, problem solving, decision making), with the higher-order ones built from the basic ones. The word comes from Latin *cognōscere*, "to learn, to understand."

## Perception and attention

Perception turns raw sensory signals (light, sound, pressure) into a stable experience of objects and events. Specialized systems detect features such as edges, faces, and speech. The brain relies on heuristics that assume objects keep their size, shape, and color under changing conditions, which is why a desk looks like the same desk in dim or bright light. These shortcuts can also produce illusions.

Attention selects what to process in detail. Because the brain cannot handle all incoming information at once, attention prioritizes some stimuli over others. The cocktail party effect, isolating one conversation from background noise, is a classic example. Memory, thinking, and language also draw on attention.

## Memory and learning

Memory has three stages: encoding, storage, and retrieval. Working memory (sometimes called short-term memory) holds and manipulates a small amount of information briefly, as when doing mental arithmetic. Long-term memory stores information over much longer periods, in some cases indefinitely.

Long-term memory is usually split into:

- Episodic memory: personal experiences, such as recalling a holiday.
- Semantic memory: general knowledge, such as that water freezes at 0 °C.
- Procedural memory: skills like riding a bicycle, which operate automatically and cannot always be described in words.

Episodic and semantic memory are explicit and consciously accessible; procedural memory is implicit. Learning, the acquisition of new information and skills through experience, depends on forming and linking memories, and is shaped by attention, emotion, and context.

## Thinking

Thinking is the manipulation of concepts and mental representations. Reasoning draws conclusions from premises. In deductive reasoning, the conclusion follows necessarily: if all men are mortal and Socrates is a man, Socrates is mortal. Inductive and abductive reasoning produce conclusions that are probable or explanatory but not guaranteed.

Problem solving moves from an initial state to a goal, often by breaking the problem into subproblems or reusing strategies that worked before. Decision making weighs options; expected utility theory holds that the rational choice is the one with the highest expected benefit, though people rely on heuristics such as representativeness, availability, and anchoring rather than strict calculation.

## Language

Language is a rule-governed system that combines sounds (phonemes) into words and words into sentences, allowing an infinite number of messages from a finite vocabulary. Comprehension combines bottom-up decoding with top-down expectations, which is why an unexpected word in a familiar sentence feels jarring. Production involves planning a message, finding the right words (lexicalization), and fitting them into grammatical structure.

## Consciousness and control

Cognitive processes can be conscious or unconscious, and controlled or automatic. Controlled processes are slow, flexible, and effortful; automatic processes are fast, unconscious, and require few resources. A task that begins as controlled, such as driving a new route, can become automatic with practice. Metacognition is the mind's monitoring of its own processes, for example noticing that a memory is available or that a strategy is failing.

## When cognition fails

Cognitive biases are systematic deviations from rational norms, often driven by the same heuristics that make thinking efficient. Cognitive disorders arise when specific processes break down: prosopagnosia leaves face recognition impaired while other visual abilities remain intact, and anterograde amnesia prevents new long-term memories while leaving old ones accessible. Alzheimer's disease is a more general, progressive decline.

## Major theories

Classical computationalism models the mind as a system that manipulates symbols by formal rules, much like a computer running a program. Connectionism models it as parallel networks of simple nodes whose interactions produce cognition, closer in style to neurons than to a program. Both treat cognition as computation but differ on architecture and whether symbols are required.

Representationalists hold that cognition works on internal models of the world. Anti-representationalists argue that intelligent behavior arises from the organism's direct interaction with its environment, the position behind embodied, embedded, extended, and enactive (4E) cognition. Other frameworks include modularity of mind, Bayesian models that treat perception as probabilistic inference, and dual process theory, which separates fast automatic thought from slow deliberate thought.

## Development, other minds, and artificial systems

Jean Piaget described cognitive development in four stages, from sensorimotor exploration in infancy to formal abstract reasoning in adolescence, while Lev Vygotsky emphasized social interaction and language. Contemporary approaches integrate ideas from both. Animal cognition covers tool use, spatial memory, number concepts, and social learning in species from insects to great apes, and some researchers have proposed plant cognition based on adaptive responses to the environment. Artificial cognition aims to emulate these processes in computers and robots, with current systems excelling at fast data processing but still weaker at flexible, creative judgment. Whether artificial general intelligence, machines with the full range of human mental abilities, is achievable remains open.

## How the field is studied

Psychology tests behavior in controlled experiments. Neuroscience links cognition to brain activity using EEG, PET, and fMRI. Cognitive science, formed in the 1970s, unites psychology, neuroscience, philosophy, linguistics, and artificial intelligence, working on three levels: the computational level (what the process achieves), the algorithmic level (the steps it takes), and the implementational level (the neurons that run it). Applied fields include cognitive load theory in education, IQ testing in psychometrics, cognitive behavior therapy, and brain stimulation and pharmacology as forms of cognitive enhancement.

## A short history

Inquiry into cognition begins in ancient philosophy, with Aristotle's work on perception and logic, and runs through medieval faculty psychologies and the early modern split between rationalism (innate knowledge) and empiricism (the mind as a blank slate, associated with John Locke). Experimental psychology began with Wilhelm Wundt in the late 19th century. In the 1950s the cognitive revolution replaced behaviorism's focus on stimulus-response with the study of internal information processing, and the later rise of neuroimaging and artificial intelligence expanded the field's reach.
