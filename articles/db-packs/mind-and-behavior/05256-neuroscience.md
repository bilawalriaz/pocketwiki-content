# Neuroscience

Neuroscience is the scientific study of the nervous system, which includes the brain, spinal cord, and peripheral nerves, along with its functions and disorders. The system contains roughly 100 billion neurons and 100 trillion synaptic connections, so the field draws on anatomy, physiology, molecular biology, developmental biology, psychology, physics, computer science, chemistry, medicine, and statistics. Eric Kandel has described understanding the biology of learning, memory, behavior, perception, and consciousness as the "epic challenge" of the biological sciences.

The core idea is that behavior and thought emerge from cells. Neurons are specialized for communication. Most have a soma (cell body), dendrites that receive input, and a long axon that carries an electrical signal called an action potential to other neurons, muscles, or glands. The connection point is the synapse, a term introduced by Charles Scott Sherrington in 1897, where electrical or chemical signals pass from one cell to the next. Neurons link into circuits and networks; glia are supporting cells that maintain and modify those circuits.

## How the system is organized

The vertebrate nervous system divides into the central nervous system (brain and spinal cord) and the peripheral nervous system (the rest). Most complexity sits in the brain, which contains thousands of distinguishable substructures and uses at least a third of the roughly 20,000 human genes. Because the brain is highly plastic, synapses form, strengthen, weaken, and disappear across life in response to experience.

## Levels of analysis

Neuroscientists work at several scales simultaneously:

- Molecular and cellular: how genes and proteins build neurons, and how neurons generate and respond to electrical and chemical signals. Tools include genetics, molecular biology, electrophysiology, and microscopy.
- Circuits and systems: how neurons wire into functional networks that produce reflexes, sensation, movement, circadian rhythms, learning, and memory. Methods include lesion studies, multi-electrode recording, and imaging.
- Cognitive and behavioral: how neural substrates give rise to perception, attention, decision-making, language, and emotion. Tools include fMRI (functional magnetic resonance imaging, which tracks blood-flow changes tied to neural activity), PET (positron emission tomography), EEG and MEG (electrical and magnetic field recordings from the scalp), optogenetics (using light to control genetically modified neurons), and neuropsychology.
- Computational: what mathematical principles describe neurons and networks, with models such as Hodgkin–Huxley, FitzHugh–Nagumo, and Morris–Lecar.

A newer pipeline called patch-sequencing combines electrophysiology, single-cell genetic sequencing, and high-quality microscopy in one procedure, and has shown that human and mouse brains share fundamentally the same cell types, in different versions.

## A short history

For most of recorded history the heart, not the brain, was thought to house intelligence: Egyptian mummifiers discarded the brain through the nostrils, and Aristotle held the cardiac view. The Greek physician Hippocrates argued the opposite, and the Roman physician Galen confirmed it by observing that brain-injured gladiators lost mental faculties.

Localization of function came later. Marc Dax (1836) and Paul Broca (1865) tied language loss to specific cortical regions, Carl Wernicke mapped comprehension, and John Hughlings Jackson inferred the motor cortex from the spread of seizures. Korbinian Brodmann's 1909 map of cell structure (cytoarchitecture) defined 52 cortical areas still referenced today. Wilder Penfield's work during epilepsy surgery produced the cortical homunculus, a distorted map of body parts across the motor and sensory cortex.

On the cellular side, Camillo Golgi's silver staining in the 1890s revealed the shape of individual neurons, and Santiago Ramón y Cajal used the same technique to argue that the neuron is the brain's functional unit, the neuron doctrine. They shared the 1906 Nobel Prize. Luigi Galvani showed that nerves and muscles are electrically excitable, Emil du Bois-Reymond demonstrated the electrical nature of the nerve signal in 1843, and Richard Caton recorded electrical activity from the brains of rabbits and monkeys in 1875. In 1952 Hodgkin and Huxley produced a quantitative model of the action potential in the squid giant axon, which became a foundation of computational neuroscience.

As a distinct academic discipline, neuroscience emerged in the mid-twentieth century. The first freestanding department, then called Psychobiology, opened at the University of California, Irvine in 1964; Harvard's Department of Neurobiology followed in 1966. The Society for Neuroscience was founded in 1969, and the BRAIN Initiative launched in the US in 2013, with the International Brain Initiative following in 2017.

## Medicine and translation

Conditions of the nervous system span several specialties: neurology (for example ALS and stroke), psychiatry, neurosurgery, neuropathology, neuroradiology, anesthesiology and pain medicine, sleep medicine, and addiction medicine. Brain imaging now lets clinicians track the biology of disorders once defined only by symptoms.

Two engineering directions extend this work. Brain–computer interfaces are being developed to bypass damaged circuits and restore function. Neuromorphic engineering builds silicon chips whose architecture mimics biological neurons; the Heidelberg BrainScaleS system, for instance, runs about 864 times faster than real biology. The same neural measurements that support medicine now feed neuroeconomics, neuroeducation, neuroethics, and neurolaw, which revisit questions that once belonged only to the social sciences.
