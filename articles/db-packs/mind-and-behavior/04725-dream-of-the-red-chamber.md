# Dream of the Red Chamber

## What the book is

*Dream of the Red Chamber* (also *The Story of the Stone*) is an 18th-century Chinese novel by Cao Xueqin and one of the Four Great Classic Novels of Chinese literature. It is a psychological epic about the decline of the aristocratic Jia family, told through nearly 40 major and over 400 minor characters, many of them women portrayed with unusual depth. The novel is a foundational text of Chinese culture and the centre of a scholarly field called Redology (紅學).

The family is already doomed when the novel opens. Their wealth is signalled by the building of the Daguanyuan (Grand View Garden) for a daughter who becomes an Imperial Consort, yet the title itself hints at the collapse: *Jia* (賈) is a homophone for "false," pointing to the Buddhist and Taoist theme that worldly success is illusion. The book ends with the family's fall.

## The story at its core

The protagonist is Jia Baoyu, a sensitive young heir born with a piece of jade in his mouth. He rejects the Confucian path of examinations and official career his family expects. The emotional engine is a love triangle:

- **Lin Daiyu**, Baoyu's true love, a melancholic poet who arrives at the garden frail and dependent.
- **Xue Baochai**, his predestined bride, graceful and pragmatic, the model of the ideal Confucian wife.
- **Baoyu**, torn between them, whose own "feminine" traits quietly challenge the patriarchal norms of Qing society.

The novel also gives a rich portrait of Qing material culture, from medicine and cuisine to opera, architecture, and clothing, and stands as a memorial to the talented women of Cao's own youth, foregrounding their poetry and inner lives. A standout is Wang Xifeng, the shrewd household manager whose competence exposes the limits of male authority in the family.

## How the text survived

The book we have is not the book Cao wrote. Cao began composing the novel in the c. 1740s and died in 1763 or 1764 with about 80 chapters finished. The manuscript circulated for decades in hand-copied editions called the **rouge versions** (脂本), so named for the red ink annotations. These manuscripts include notes by a commentator called Zhiyanzhai (脂齋), believed to have been close to Cao, whose remarks offer rare clues to the original plot and to a lost ending.

In 1791, Gao E and Cheng Weiyuan published a 120-chapter printed edition, the **Cheng-Gao version**, completing the story with 40 additional chapters. A corrected second edition followed in 1792. This text is the basis of virtually all later editions and translations.

## The authorship debate

Whether Cao wrote those last 40 chapters is the central question of Redology and has never been settled.

- In 1921, Hu Shih argued the final 40 chapters were written by Gao E, not Cao, based on stylistic evidence.
- Zhou Ruchang's 1953 study helped establish the novel as a serious subject of modern scholarship.
- A 2014 data-analysis study argued statistically that the first 80 and last 40 chapters have different authors.
- A pre-1791 120-chapter manuscript has surfaced, which, if genuine, would undermine the claim that Gao composed the ending himself, though its status remains contested.
- In 2020, Zhang Qingshan argued it is unlikely Gao E wrote the last 40 chapters at all.

The dispute is sharpened by the rouge versions and the novel's own internal foreshadowing, which many readers feel points to an ending different from the one Cheng and Gao printed. A whole subfield, **Tanyi xue** (探佚學), exists to reconstruct Cao's lost original ending from these clues.

## Language, influence, and translation

The novel is written in **baihua** (白話), the vernacular Chinese of Beijing, which became a stylistic foundation for modern Mandarin and was championed by the New Culture Movement. Early reformers like Liang Qichao dismissed the book as obsolete, but the scholar Wang Guowei elevated it by calling it "the tragedy of tragedies."

Two English translations dominate. The Yangs' *A Dream of Red Mansions* preserves a classical, Beijing-inflected register. David Hawkes and John Minford's *The Story of the Stone* (1973–1980), in five volumes, is generally considered the more literary rendering and is the one most Western readers encounter. The novel has inspired countless adaptations in film, television, and opera, and remains a touchstone for feminist, Marxist, and philosophical readings of Chinese literature.
