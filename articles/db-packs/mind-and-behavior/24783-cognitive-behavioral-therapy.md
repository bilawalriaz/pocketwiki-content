# Cognitive behavioral therapy

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is a problem-focused, action-oriented psychotherapy that treats mental health conditions by changing distorted thinking and unhelpful behaviors, while training better-adapted ones. The core claim is that thoughts, emotions, behaviors, and physical reactions form an interconnected loop, so changing how a person thinks and acts reliably improves how they feel.

CBT assumes emotional distress is driven less by events themselves than by the meanings assigned to them. Treatment targets identifiable cognitions ("automatic thoughts," schemas, core beliefs) and the behaviors that maintain them. Sessions are structured and time-limited, typically 6–18 weekly hour-long meetings plus booster sessions. A defining feature is homework: patients practice skills between sessions, such as a person with social anxiety approaching a stranger in graded steps, and the therapist gauges progress by how thoroughly assignments are completed.

Mainstream CBT uses Socratic questioning (guided questioning that exposes contradictions in a person's beliefs), behavioral experiments, and thought records to surface and challenge cognitive distortions such as arbitrary inference, overgeneralization, catastrophizing, and personalization. The therapist functions as a teacher in a collaborative alliance; patients are active participants, not passive recipients. Aaron Beck, often called "the father of cognitive behavioral therapy," developed cognitive therapy in the 1950s–60s and published his first treatment manual in 1979. His cognitive theory of depression proposes that early negative experiences form a negative schema, producing a negative cognitive triad (negative views of self, world, and future) that biases interpretation. Behavioral exposure, such as a PTSD patient returning to the feared location, directly tests these beliefs.

The behavioral tradition predates and underlies CBT. Joseph Wolpe's systematic desensitization and B. F. Skinner's operant conditioning showed that maladaptive behaviors are learned and can be unlearned through conditioning and extinction. Exposure and response prevention (ERP), a specific CBT technique, is considered first-line for pediatric obsessive–compulsive disorder and is effective both in person and remotely.

CBT is conventionally divided into three waves. The first wave (1920s–1960s) is behavior therapy, grounded in Pavlov, Watson, and Wolpe, treating disorders through conditioning principles. The second wave (1950s–1970s) added cognition: Albert Ellis's rational emotive behavior therapy (1956) and Beck's cognitive therapy merged with behavioral methods to form classical CBT, which dominated from the 1970s onward. The third wave (1980s–1990s) shifted focus from the content of thoughts to one's stance toward them, drawing on Buddhist mindfulness and acceptance. This wave produced dialectical behavior therapy, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy, and compassion-focused therapy. Over time, "CBT" became an umbrella term covering this family of related therapies.

Originally designed for depression, CBT is now an evidence-informed treatment for many conditions: generalized anxiety disorder, obsessive–compulsive disorder, eating disorders (first-line for bulimia nervosa), substance use disorders, and post-traumatic stress disorder. For anxiety disorders, NICE, the American Psychiatric Association, the American Psychological Association, and the UK's NHS recommend it. CBT is the first-line psychosocial treatment for most psychological disorders in children and adolescents, including aggression and conduct disorder.

Delivery formats include individual or group face-to-face therapy, self-help books, computerized CBT (CCBT, delivered via internet or app, recommended by NICE since 2006 for mild-to-moderate depression), and smartphone chatbot apps. CCBT is cost-effective and, for conditions like anxiety and PTSD, as effective as face-to-face therapy. Brief CBT (BCBT) was developed for suicidality, compressing treatment into about 12 hours.

CBT faces four main criticisms. First, psychotherapy trials cannot be double-blinded (neither patient nor therapist is unaware of which treatment is given), and when placebo controls and blinding are accounted for, effects shrink; for schizophrenia and bipolar relapse prevention, CBT performs no better than non-specific control interventions. Second, effect sizes for depression have declined since 1977, with a steeper decline after 1995. Third, drop-out rates run about 17% higher than in other therapies, and high drop-out is especially evident in anorexia treatment. Fourth, critics argue CBT targets symptoms rather than underlying causes, and that its model overlooks free will by treating people as passive responders to stimuli. Side effects appear uncommon in trials but are rarely monitored; "negative wellbeing/distress" is the most frequently reported. The CDC omits CBT from ME/CFS guidance, and NICE states it should only be offered to support symptom management, not as a cure.

A 2017 meta-analysis found CBT has fewer dropouts than placebo or medication in children, evidence supports its effectiveness for anxiety and depression, and adding hypnosis as an adjunct improves outcomes across several conditions.
