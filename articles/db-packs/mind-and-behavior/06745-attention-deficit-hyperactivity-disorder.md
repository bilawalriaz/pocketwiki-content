# Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder

Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental condition marked by developmentally excessive inattention, hyperactivity, impulsivity, and emotional dysregulation that impair functioning in multiple life contexts. Symptoms must appear before age 12, persist for at least six months, and occur in two or more settings such as home, school, or work. The disorder sits at the extreme low end of a continuous distribution of executive functioning and self-regulation, the cognitive processes that select and monitor goal-directed behaviour.

## Core mechanisms

ADHD symptoms arise from executive dysfunction: impaired self-regulation affecting attention, working memory, cognitive inhibition, task initiation, and time management. These deficits trace to atypical development of prefrontal executive networks, which show reduced size, connectivity, and activation. Underlying this are imbalances in dopamine and norepinephrine signalling along the mesocorticolimbic (reward and motivation) and locus coeruleus-noradrenergic (alertness) pathways. The result is difficulty sustaining attention on low-stimulation tasks while remaining highly responsive to immediate rewards or intense stimulation. A related phenomenon is hyperfocus, an intense, narrow concentration on highly engaging tasks that becomes hard to disengage from, linked to risk for addiction and certain offending behaviour. Mind wandering, the drift of attention from a task to inner thoughts, is also more frequent and may be a stronger predictor of adult ADHD than the classic symptoms.

Heritability runs 70–80%, with risk accumulating across many small-effect gene variants affecting dopamine (DAT, DRD4, DRD5), serotonin (SERT, HTR1B), and synaptic function (SNAP25). Siblings of affected children are three to four times more likely to develop ADHD. Environmental contributors operate early, in the prenatal or perinatal period: tobacco or alcohol exposure, premature birth, low birth weight, lead, organophosphate pesticides, and certain infections. Traumatic brain injury causes about 5% of cases. Popular explanations involving sugar, television, bad parenting, or poverty lack scientific support, though chronic stress can worsen existing symptoms.

## Presentations and diagnosis

Three presentations exist: predominantly inattentive (forgetful, distractible, disorganised), predominantly hyperactive-impulsive (restless, interruptive, impatient), and combined. Girls more often show the inattentive form, misread as daydreaming or laziness. Symptoms change with age: childhood hyperactivity becomes inner restlessness in adulthood, and impulsivity shifts toward impatience and sensation-seeking. Many women report symptom fluctuation with the menstrual cycle, tied to estrogen and progesterone variation. Emotional dysregulation, meaning rapid mood shifts and irritability, is common but not an official diagnostic criterion.

Diagnosis relies on clinical assessment using DSM-5-TR or ICD-11 criteria, supplemented by standardised rating scales from multiple informants (parents, teachers, self-report). No biological test confirms ADHD; brain imaging and EEG are research tools only. Differential diagnosis must rule out anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder, autism, learning disabilities, sleep disorders, and substance effects, as symptoms overlap substantially.

## Comorbidities and risks

Nearly 78% of people with ADHD have at least one comorbid condition. Common psychiatric comorbidities include oppositional defiant disorder, conduct disorder, anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder, autism spectrum disorder, learning disabilities, and substance use disorders. Borderline personality disorder co-occurs in 30–60% of ADHD cases, driven by shared emotion dysregulation. Non-psychiatric comorbidities include obesity, asthma, sleep disorders, migraine, and epilepsy.

ADHD carries elevated risks of accidents, traffic collisions, substance abuse, criminal behaviour, and suicide. A 2025 study found men with ADHD live about seven years less and women about nine years less than peers without ADHD, likely through downstream effects on health behaviours, accidents, and co-occurring mental health conditions. As of 2019, ADHD affected an estimated 84.7 million people globally, though prevalence estimates range from 0.8% to 7% depending on diagnostic criteria and population. Diagnosis rates have risen since the 1970s, reflecting changed diagnostic thresholds and greater willingness to treat rather than a true change in incidence.

## Treatment

Medications are the most effective treatment. Stimulants (methylphenidate, amphetamine) work for roughly 70% of patients by increasing dopamine and norepinephrine in prefrontal networks. Amphetamines appear modestly more effective than methylphenidate in adults, while methylphenidate shows the greatest short-term benefit in children. Non-stimulant options include atomoxetine and viloxazine (norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors) and extended-release guanfacine and clonidine (alpha-2A agonists), which are modestly less effective but useful when stimulants are unsuitable. Combining medication with cognitive behavioural therapy addresses residual problems.

Side effects can include insomnia (11–45% with stimulants), appetite loss, and rarely psychosis. Benefits reverse if medication stops. Behavioural therapies and parent training are first-line for young children or mild cases. Dietary changes, exercise, and neurofeedback lack robust evidence for symptom reduction, though supplementation with zinc, iron, or omega-3 fatty acids may help when deficiencies are present. Social skills training, school accommodations, and family support provide additional benefit.
