# Behavioral medicine

Behavioral medicine is the interdisciplinary field that integrates biology, behavior, psychology, and social science to understand and treat health and illness. It draws on epidemiology, anthropology, sociology, psychology, physiology, pharmacology, nutrition, neuroanatomy, endocrinology, and immunology. Practitioners include nurses, social workers, psychologists, and physicians, who act as behavioral change agents in their clinical roles.

Health psychology is a subset of behavioral medicine rather than a synonym. It emphasizes psychology's specific contribution, while behavioral medicine is broader, also covering biofeedback, hypnosis, bio-behavioral therapy of physical disorders, occupational and rehabilitation medicine, physiatry, and preventive medicine.

The field exists because the leading causes of modern death, including smoking, sedentary lifestyle, and substance use disorders, are primarily behavioral, so changing behavior is as medically consequential as prescribing drugs.

## The biopsychosocial model

Behavioral medicine replaces the traditional medical model, which explains disease as a purely biological deviation from normal function, with the biopsychosocial model proposed by Schwartz and Weiss after the 1977 Yale Conference. The model treats biology, psychology, and social environment as joint causes of illness.

## Origins

The mind-body link appears in writings from the earliest civilizations, and psychosomatic medicine is an academic forebear, now obsolete as a psychological discipline. The modern field dates to the 1970s. Lee Birk used the term in a 1973 book, Ovide F. Pomerleau and John Paul Brady founded the Center for Behavioral Medicine at the University of Pennsylvania in 1973, and William Stewart Agras founded the Laboratory for the Study of Behavioral Medicine at Stanford in 1974. The 1976 NIH Behavioral Medicine Study Section and the 1977 Yale Conference institutionalized interdisciplinary research. The Society of Behavioral Medicine and the Academy of Behavioral Medicine Research formed in 1978, and the International Society of Behavioral Medicine in 1990.

## Behavior as direct treatment

Several chronic conditions improve significantly through behavior change rather than drugs alone. Substance use responds best to medication combined with behavioral intervention. Hypertension falls with deliberate stress reduction. Insomnia is treated first-line with cognitive and behavioral interventions. Diabetes depends on diet and exercise as core treatments.

## Adherence and the clinical relationship

Medications control chronic illness only when patients follow the regimen, and adherence improves when clinicians give clear information, explain what to do, and reinforce compliance through follow-up. Strong social support, especially through marriage and family, predicts better adherence. Telemonitoring by phone or video and case management by multidisciplinary teams are concrete adherence strategies.

The doctor-patient relationship is itself part of treatment. Effective care depends on honest, clear communication rather than specialist-driven transactions. Obstacles include power imbalances, patient vulnerability, helplessness, and fear, along with clinicians' difficulty handling uncooperative patients and delivering bad news. The field increasingly emphasizes shared power and patient empowerment, and now also intervenes with providers to sustain conduct and prevent burnout.

## Learning and stress

Behavioral medicine applies learning principles including reinforcement, avoidance, generalization, and discrimination, along with cognitive-social models such as Marlatt's relapse prevention model. Learning is a relatively permanent change in behavioral tendency produced by reinforced practice, which explains how maladaptive responses become psychosomatic and how patients can unlearn them, notably in addiction and phobia treatment. The three primary learning theories are classical conditioning, operant conditioning, and modeling.

Chronic stress, built from many small daily microstressors, drives disease through Hans Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome, which proceeds through alarm, resistance, and exhaustion.

## Chronic pain as application

Chronic pain illustrates the biopsychosocial model. Pure biomedical reasoning could not explain why some patients with severe tissue damage felt no pain, motivating the cognitive addition that produced gate control theory and recognition of the placebo effect. Psychological factors shaping pain include self-efficacy, anxiety, fear, abuse, life stressors, and pain catastrophizing, the last especially responsive to behavioral intervention. Genetic predisposition sets baseline pain sensitivity and distress risk, while social factors such as socioeconomic status, race, and ethnicity modify the pain experience. Fisher et al. (2011) showed behavioral approaches are cost-effective across cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer, HIV/AIDS, tobacco use, poor diet, physical inactivity, and excessive alcohol consumption, producing broad benefits in prevention, disease management, and well-being across the life span.
