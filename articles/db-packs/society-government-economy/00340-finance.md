# Finance

Finance is the discipline that studies how monetary resources are managed, created, and channeled. Its core function is connecting savers with surplus money to borrowers who need it, balancing risk against expected return while applying the time value of money: a dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow because it can be invested. The field splits into three domains: personal finance (household budgeting), corporate finance (maximising firm value), and public finance (government revenue, spending, and debt), supported by subfields such as investment management, risk management, and quantitative finance.

## The Financial System

The financial system moves capital between surplus units (savers) and deficit units (borrowers) across personal, corporate, and public sectors. Savers earn returns through interest or dividends; borrowers raise money by issuing debt (loans, bonds) or equity (shares). Most intermediation is indirect. Commercial banks aggregate deposits and lend out, profiting from the interest rate spread. Investment banks arrange equity and debt issuance (IPOs, bond underwriting). Securities exchanges give shares and bonds a secondary market where they can be resold. Institutional investors (pension funds, insurance companies) and retail investors participate in these markets. Large-scale inter-institutional activity is wholesale finance, dominated by bespoke derivatives (options, swaps, structured products) and managed by quantitative analysts ("quants").

## Core Areas

**Personal finance** covers budgeting, saving, investing, and insurance, alongside tax planning, retirement planning, and banking relationships.

**Corporate finance** maximises firm value through three decisions: capital budgeting (selecting investment projects), dividend policy (reinvesting versus paying out profits), and capital structure (the debt–equity mix, chosen to minimise the Weighted Average Cost of Capital, or WACC, the blended cost a firm pays for its financing). Managers also handle working capital, the inventory, receivables, and short-term cash needed to pay expenses and service debt.

**Public finance** manages sovereign and sub-national finances over long horizons, typically five or more years. It covers expenditure, taxation, budgeting, and sovereign debt. Central banks such as the Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank, and the Bank of England act as lenders of last resort and shape monetary conditions. Adjacent fields include development finance (non-commercial funding for economic projects, often via public–private partnerships) and climate finance (funding mitigation strategies).

**Investment management** is the professional management of securities, real estate, and alternatives to meet investor goals. Performance is driven mainly by asset allocation: diversifying across asset classes to match the client's risk profile and horizon. Managers also conduct fundamental analysis (valuing securities from underlying business value) and technical analysis (forecasting prices from past patterns), and they hedge using tools such as the "Greeks" (sensitivities measuring how a derivative's price responds to the underlying asset, volatility, time, and rates). Quantitative funds replace human judgement with mathematical models and automated trading.

**Risk management** measures and mitigates three core risk types. Credit risk is the chance a borrower defaults. Market risk comes from price and exchange-rate movements. Operational risk arises from process or system failures. In banks, the focus is hedging trading positions and calculating regulatory capital under Basel III, a global framework requiring minimum capital reserves, stress testing, and liquidity buffers to reduce systemic collapse. Insurers focus on solvency: life insurers manage longevity and interest-rate risk; property and casualty insurers maintain reserves against catastrophe losses.

## Financial Theory

**Financial economics** examines how financial variables (prices, rates, shares) interact, distinct from real variables (goods and services). It rests on two pillars. Asset pricing works from the investor's side, setting risk-appropriate discount rates and pricing derivatives using CAPM (Capital Asset Pricing Model, which links expected return to sensitivity to market risk) and Black–Scholes. Corporate finance theory works from the firm's side, addressing funding and dividend decisions. Under perfect-market assumptions, the Modigliani–Miller theorem holds that a firm's value is independent of its debt–equity mix; real-options analysis extends this by adding managerial flexibility under uncertainty. Financial econometrics uses statistical methods to estimate these relationships.

**Financial mathematics**, rooted in Louis Bachelier's 1900 doctoral thesis, models derivatives, interest-rate and credit risk, insurance mathematics, and portfolios. Key tools include Itô's stochastic calculus and partial differential equations (PDEs) for derivatives, plus Value at Risk, stress testing, and copulas (statistical functions describing how multiple risks move together) for risk. A central distinction is between risk-neutral probability ("Q"), an artificial measure that lets assets be priced as if they earn the risk-free rate, and physical probability ("P"), which reflects real-world outcomes. The Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing links the two, guaranteeing that arbitrage-free pricing exists whenever markets can be described consistently under Q.

**Behavioural finance** integrates psychology to explain deviations from classical rationality, including market anomalies and trading biases. It has expanded into social finance, which studies how networks diffuse ideas and influence prices, and quantitative behavioural finance, which models biases mathematically alongside valuation.

**Quantum finance** applies quantum computational approaches to high-complexity problems such as option pricing, where classical methods struggle to converge quickly in fast-moving markets.

## History

Finance traces to roughly 3000 BCE, when West Asian temples and palaces stored grain and valuables and Sumerian Uruk practised lending at interest. The Code of Hammurabi regulated banking, with Babylonians charging about 20% annually. Coinage emerged between 700 and 500 BCE, beginning in Lydia around 687 BCE and spreading to Greek cities in 595–570 BCE. Rome outlawed interest in 342 BCE, then imposed ceilings (Julius Caesar at 12%, later lowered by Justinian to 4–8%). Modern exchanges appeared in Antwerp (1531), London (1773), and New York (1793). Academic finance began with Bachelier in 1900 and matured with doctoral programmes in the 1960s and 1970s.

## Open Debates

Classical models (CAPM, Black–Scholes) assume rationality and efficient markets, but fat tails (extreme losses occurring far more often than normal distributions predict), volatility clustering, and behavioural biases regularly violate these assumptions. Bridging the risk-neutral ("Q") and real-world ("P") probability measures remains technically delicate. The reach of psychological biases into asset pricing is unresolved, with social-network effects now part of the dispute. Post-2008 Basel III rules stabilised banks but draw criticism for constraining lending or ignoring shadow banking (lending and trading outside the regulated banking system) and systemic interconnectedness. Quantum finance offers theoretical speedups for derivatives pricing but no proven edge over classical Monte Carlo (random-sampling simulation) and PDE methods at production scale, while climate finance still lacks a standard method for pricing transition and physical risk or for aligning regulation with net-zero targets.
