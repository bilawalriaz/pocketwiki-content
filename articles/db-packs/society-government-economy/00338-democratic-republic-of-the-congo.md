# Democratic Republic of the Congo

The Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) is Central Africa's largest country by area (2.34 million km²) and the most populous Francophone state, with about 124 million people. It holds extraordinary mineral wealth yet ranks 171st of 193 on the 2023 UN Human Development Index. The gap between resource wealth and lived poverty is the country's defining condition, and its modern history flows from that gap.

## Land, water, and mineral wealth

The country straddles the Equator and is dominated by the Congo Basin, the world's second-largest rainforest. The Congo River is the deepest in the world and the second-largest by water volume, draining most of the country. The Albertine Rift in the east forms high mountains (the Rwenzori range), four Great Lakes (Albert, Kivu, Edward, Tanganyika), and active volcanoes; Nyiragongo's2002 eruption devastated Goma.

The south and east hold roughly 70% of world cobalt production and reserves, 70% of coltan, and large shares of copper, diamonds, gold, and uranium. The Inga hydroelectric site on the Congo River could in theory generate 100,000 megawatts, more than enough to electrify the continent. None of this has translated into broad prosperity.

## The resource curse

The "resource curse" is the paradox in which mineral-rich countries often grow more slowly and fight more than resource-poor ones. The DRC is the textbook case. At independence in 1960 it was Africa's second-most-industrialised state; by the 1990s, hyperinflation and war had nearly destroyed the formal economy. Mining now accounts for 80% of exports, with China taking 41%. Yet 73.5% of Congolese live on less than $2.15 a day, 65% depend on subsistence farming, and only about 3,000 km of road are paved; the three economic hubs (Kinshasa, Lubumbashi, Kisangani) are not connected by road or rail.

## From colony to kleptocracy

Foragers lived in the Congo Basin at least 90,000 years ago. Between roughly 1000 BC and 1000 AD, Bantu-speaking farmers spread through the region and built organised states: the Kingdom of Kongo at the Atlantic mouth and the Luba, Lunda, and Mwene Muji empires inland.

In 1885, King Leopold II of Belgium acquired the Congo as a personal colony, the Congo Free State, and ran it through concessionary companies that forced villagers to gather rubber. Brutality, disease, and famine caused catastrophic population loss. A 1904 Casement Report forced Leopold to hand the territory to the Belgian state in 1908. Belgian rule ended the worst abuses but kept forced labour, segregation, and extraction.

In May 1960, Patrice Lumumba's party won elections, and the country became independent on 30 June 1960. Within days the army mutinied, the mineral-rich province of Katanga seceded under Moïse Tshombe, and most European settlers fled. The UN refused to crush the secessions; Lumumba turned to the Soviet Union, alarming the United States and Belgium. In January 1961, Lumumba was handed to Katangan forces and killed, with Belgian officers involved.

In 1965, Colonel Mobutu Sese Seko seized power in a US- and Belgian-backed coup and ruled as a one-party dictator for 32 years, renaming the country Zaire in 1971 and launching an "Authenticité" campaign that stripped colonial names from cities (Léopoldville became Kinshasa). His anti-communist stance kept US support throughout the Cold War; his regime became a personal kleptocracy and infrastructure collapsed.

## Africa's World War

Mobutu's fall unleashed two wars. The First Congo War (1996–1997) was triggered by the flight into eastern Zaire of Hutu extremists who had carried out the 1994 Rwandan genocide. Rwanda and Uganda invaded to destroy those militias, overthrew Mobutu, and installed Laurent-Désiré Kabila as president.

Kabila soon fell out with his backers, and in 1998 Rwanda and Uganda backed new rebel movements. Angola, Zimbabwe, and Namibia intervened on Kabila's side. The resulting Second Congo War (1998–2003), often called "Africa's World War", involved nine national armies and around 20 armed groups. Excess deaths, mostly from disease and starvation rather than bullets, are estimated at 3 to 5.4 million. A power-sharing agreement led to the country's first free elections in 2006, which Joseph Kabila, Laurent's son, won.

## The M23 crisis

Despite the 2006 transition, more than 120 armed groups remained active in the mineral-rich east. The most prominent is M23, a Tutsi-led rebel movement that briefly captured Goma in 2012, prompting the UN to authorise a Force Intervention Brigade. M23 was pushed back but resurfaced in 2022 with direct Rwandan military support. Rwanda cites the FDLR, a Hutu militia in eastern DRC, as its justification for intervention; UN experts and Western governments say Rwanda is backing M23 to control eastern mines.

In December 2018, the DRC held its second multi-party election. The official result handed victory to Félix Tshisekedi, but independent polling showed opposition candidate Martin Fayulu well ahead. A deal between the outgoing Kabila and Tshisekedi is widely suspected but unproven. Tshisekedi consolidated power by 2021 and won re-election in December 2023 with more than 70% of the vote. In early 2025, M23 and Rwandan forces captured both Goma and Bukavu.

A US-brokered peace deal in June 2025 required Rwandan troops to withdraw within 90 days and the DRC to stop supporting the FDLR. The UN Human Rights Office reported that hostilities increased in the following months, and in October 2025 former President Joseph Kabila was sentenced to death in absentia for alleged collaboration with M23. More than 5 million people are internally displaced in the eastern provinces, on top of about a million Congolese refugees abroad.

## Governance, security, and society

The 2006 constitution creates a semi-presidential republic, but Freedom House rates the country "Not Free," the Economist Democracy Index places it 156th of 167, and the Corruption Perceptions Index ranks it 163rd of 180. The army, officially 134,000 strong, is crippled by low pay, divided loyalties from integrating former rebels, and broken logistics. Sexual violence is routinely used as a weapon in the east, and child labour in mines is widespread. About 43.5% of children are stunted.

The population grew from 12.2 million in 1950 to about 124 million in 2024 and speaks 242 living languages. French is the official language, spoken by about 74%; Lingala is the army's working language. Christianity is practised by an estimated 93–96% of citizens, and the Catholic Church runs more than 60% of primary schools.
