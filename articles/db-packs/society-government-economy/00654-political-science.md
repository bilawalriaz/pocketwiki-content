# Political science

Political science is a social science that studies how power is allocated and transferred, how systems of governance operate, how people behave politically, and how public policy is made. The discipline describes and explains these political phenomena empirically rather than judging them. Because the subject depends on conscious human actors, the field draws on multiple methods and accepts less certainty than physics or chemistry.

## Origins as a discipline

Political science separated from political philosophy, history, and law in the late 19th century. Earlier work in moral philosophy, political economy, political theology, and history supplied the antecedents. University departments and chairs named "political science" appeared during this period, and "political scientist" came to denote someone with an advanced degree in the field.

The American Political Science Association (APSA) was founded in 1903, followed by the *American Political Science Review* in 1906. These institutions distinguished the study of politics from economics and other social phenomena. APSA membership grew from 204 in 1904 to 1,462 in 1915. Internationally, the International Political Science Association was founded in 1949 under a UNESCO initiative, followed by national associations in France (1949), Britain (1950), and West Germany (1951). The term "government" persists in some older or non-North American institutions, while "political studies" suggests a broader scope.

## Behavioral revolution and formal modeling

In the 1950s and 1960s, a behavioral revolution shifted emphasis toward the systematic, scientific study of individual and group behavior, moving away from institutional analysis and legal interpretation. Robert Dahl, Philip Converse, Paul Lazarsfeld, and Bernard Berelson exemplified this approach. In the late 1960s and 1970s, deductive game-theoretic formal modeling gained ground, borrowing theory and methods from economics to study institutions and behavior. William H. Riker and colleagues at the University of Rochester were central proponents. Despite these advances, systematic theoretical development remained modest and uneven.

Game theory models strategic interaction among rational actors and now extends to areas as varied as trade policy, electoral coalitions, and civil war onset.

## Methods

Political science uses both quantitative and qualitative methods. Controlled experiments are often impractical, so the field relies on observation, surveys, case studies, process tracing, historical analysis, ethnography, and interviews. Theoretical tools include game theory, agent-based models, equation-based models, and opinion dynamics. The research paper remains the most common form of academic writing.

A central distinction runs between positive political science, which describes and explains political phenomena as they are, and normative political science, which argues how politics ought to work. Scholars also debate methodological pluralism, the use of diverse methods and theories, against pressures toward a single dominant approach.

## 21st century debates

The Perestroika Movement, introduced in 2000, protested what supporters called the "mathematicization" of political science and advocated methodological pluralism and greater relevance to audiences outside academia.

Some evolutionary psychology theories argue that humans evolved psychological mechanisms suited to small-group politics, which may explain cognitive biases in modern political behavior. The universality of these mechanisms in modern political systems remains contested.

Crisis anticipation uses statistical indicators such as increased variance and shifting correlations to predict political transitions, as demonstrated in analyses of the 2014 Ukrainian crisis.

## Education

Most U.S. institutions offer BA programs, with MA, PhD, and professional degrees in public policy and administration also common. Pi Sigma Alpha is the main national honor society for undergraduate and graduate students in the United States. Political science sits between the sciences and humanities in the academy, usually housed in arts and sciences colleges or its own departments.

The field overlaps with sociology, anthropology, and public health, and sometimes inherits or develops contrasting methodologies from these neighbors.
