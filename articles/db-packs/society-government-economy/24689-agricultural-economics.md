# Agricultural economics

Agricultural economics is an applied branch of economics that uses economic theory to optimize how food and fibre are produced and distributed. It grew out of land economics in the late nineteenth century, when scholars began asking how to get the most from a fixed area of soil while keeping that soil healthy. Over the twentieth century the field broadened, so that today's agricultural economists work on farm management, marketing, trade, development, food consumption, and the environment, often using methods indistinguishable from mainstream economics.

The discipline traces its institutional start to 1909, when Henry Charles Taylor founded the Department of Agricultural Economics at the University of Wisconsin. A second formative figure was Theodore Schultz, who won the 1979 Nobel Prize in Economics. Schultz framed agriculture as a development problem and pushed econometrics, the use of statistical methods to test economic theories, into the field, complaining in a 1956 article that agricultural supply analysis was being done on "shifting sand," meaning that existing empirical work was unreliable. From the 1960s onward, as farm sectors shrank in rich OECD countries (the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, a group of largely wealthy nations), agricultural economists turned toward development, trade, and environmental questions. The farm sector is still routinely used in textbooks as an example of perfect competition, a market structure in which many small sellers offer an identical product and no one can influence the price.

Agricultural economists have shaped economics as a whole. They built the cobweb model, which explains how prices and output can cycle when farmers base planting decisions on last year's price; hedonic regression, a statistical technique that estimates how each attribute of a product contributes to its price; diffusion models for new technology, most famously Zvi Griliches's 1957 study of hybrid corn; multifactor productivity measurement, which tracks output per combined unit of labour, capital, and land; and random-coefficients regression, a method that lets economic relationships vary across individuals or firms.

Since the 1970s the field has clustered around seven recurring problems: agricultural environment and resources; risk and uncertainty; food and consumer economics; prices and incomes; market structure; trade and development; and technical change combined with human capital.

Environmental and resource work splits into two strands. One designs incentives to control externalities, the unintended side effects of farming on others, such as fertiliser runoff that pollutes rivers, and values things without a market price, like scenic countryside. The other builds quantitative tools for land management, erosion control, pest management, biodiversity protection, and livestock-disease prevention.

Food and consumer economics looks beyond the farm gate. Researchers study how households choose between restaurant meals and home cooking, how prices form through the supply chain, what counts as a poverty line, and how consumers react to income and price shifts. Surveys and experiments measure preferences for food quality and safety attributes.

Production economics and farm management tackles the core farming decision: what to plant, how much fertiliser to apply, how to respond to risk. Diminishing returns, the principle that each extra unit of input eventually yields less extra output, governs input choice. Risk analysis underpins crop insurance and explains why poor farmers often resist apparently superior technologies: a new seed may raise average yield but also raise the chance of catastrophic loss.

Development economics is closely bound to agriculture because farming still accounts for a large share of employment and GDP in low-income countries. Agricultural economists study how structural transformation, the shift of labour out of farming into industry and services, unfolds, how food systems connect to nutrition, and how agriculture interacts with the natural environment.

Professional life is organised around a handful of bodies. The International Association of Agricultural Economists (IAAE) holds a worldwide conference every three years and publishes the journal *Agricultural Economics*. Regional associations include the European (EAAE), African (AAAE), and Australian groups, alongside the International Food Policy Research Institute. In the United States the Agricultural & Applied Economics Association (AAEA) publishes the *American Journal of Agricultural Economics* and *Applied Economic Perspectives and Policy*; in the United Kingdom the Agricultural Economics Society, founded in 1926, publishes the *Journal of Agricultural Economics* and co-publishes *EuroChoices*.

Careers span agricultural management, agribusiness, marketing, finance, government, natural-resource management, and policy analysis. Entry requires at least a bachelor's degree; research roles require graduate training. A 2011 Georgetown employability study ranked agricultural economics tied for eighth of 171 fields.
