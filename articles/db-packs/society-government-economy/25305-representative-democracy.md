# Representative democracy

Representative democracy (also called indirect or electoral democracy) is government in which citizens vote for delegates who then decide policy on their behalf, in contrast to direct democracy, where citizens vote on policy themselves. Nearly all modern Western-style governments function this way, including the United Kingdom, Germany, France, and the United States, each a different constitutional form. Some political theorists, including Robert Dahl, describe the system as polyarchy. Political parties tend to become central when electoral rules push voters toward party labels rather than individual candidates.

## How representatives are organized

Elected delegates typically form a legislature such as a parliament or congress. A legislature may be unicameral, bicameral, or multicameral; where multiple chambers exist, members are often elected by different methods. The Canadian Senate and the British House of Lords, for instance, are not directly elected.

Representative democracy can take parliamentary or presidential form. Under a parliamentary system, elected representatives may also choose a prime minister or other officers. Power is usually constrained by a constitution, and an independent judiciary such as a constitutional court or supreme court may strike down laws that violate it. Constitutions sometimes permit direct-democratic tools like initiatives, referendums, or recall elections, but these are often advisory and still require legislative action, so final power stays with the representatives.

Representatives can be chosen directly by voters or by other representatives through indirect elections, and indirect selection tends to weaken political accountability.

## Delegate versus trustee

Edmund Burke set out a lasting distinction: a delegate follows the wishes of constituents, while a trustee uses independent judgment even when constituents disagree. Geographic districts contain voters with many different views, so a representative cannot perfectly mirror everyone. Under proportional representation, members are elected by a more unified constituency, so voter and representative views tend to align more closely.

## Historical development

Ancient Athens and the Roman Republic practiced direct democracy, gathering citizens in person, workable only for small polities. A medieval European tradition of estates (nobles, clergy, commons) advising monarchs spread familiarity with representation.

In Britain, representative government expanded after the Oxford Parliament of 1258 and Simon de Montfort's Parliament of 1265. The 17th-century Parliament of England advanced liberal-democratic ideas through the Glorious Revolution and the Bill of Rights 1689. The voting franchise widened through 19th- and 20th-century Reform Acts.

After Corsica's independence from Genoa in 1755, Pasquale Paoli drafted a constitution with representative democracy and Enlightenment features including women's suffrage in local elections. Some historians call it the first written national constitution. France revoked the constitution when it took the island in 1769. Alexander Hamilton coined "representative democracy" in a letter about New York's constitution, distinguishing it from direct democracy and mixed regimes, and used the term during ratification of the U.S. Constitution in 1787.

The American Revolution produced a national legislature partly based on direct elections every two years. Senators were not directly elected until the Seventeenth Amendment in 1913. Women, propertyless men, and Black Americans gained voting rights through 19th- and 20th-century law changes. Until the Fourteenth Amendment repealed it after the Civil War, the Three-Fifths Compromise gave slave states disproportionate House representation. France adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man in 1789 and elected the National Convention by universal male suffrage in 1792; universal male suffrage returned with the 1848 revolution. Many historians credit the Reform Act 1832 with launching modern British representative democracy. Industrial-era nation states, too large and complex for direct democracy, drove the system's global spread. The V-Dem Institute's electoral democracy index peaked in 2012 and has declined since, with the decline accelerating in the early 2020s.

## Criticisms and proposed alternatives

Robert Michels' 1911 *Political Parties* argues that representative systems slide toward oligarchy, the "iron law of oligarchy." Adolf Gasser, in *Gemeindefreiheit als Rettung Europas* (1943, revised 1947), argued stable representative democracies require bottom-up organization: armed free individuals, financially independent local communities, voluntary federation, no hierarchical bureaucracy, and competition among localities. Other critiques note that elected officials face no enforcement of campaign promises, that majority-won legislatures can marginalize minorities, and that representatives may form a closed elite serving capital rather than voters.

The V-Dem Institute treats electoral democracies as the bare-minimum form: governments accountable through periodic elections but lacking a fully developed rule of law, robust minority rights, or protections against majority tyranny. Two main reform proposals exist. Sortition (also called stochocracy or lottocracy), proposed by Roger de Sizif in 1998, would replace elections with random lottery selection, a method also used in ancient Athens and India to limit lobbying. Deliberative democracy blends majority and consensus rule and can coexist with either representative or direct institutions.
