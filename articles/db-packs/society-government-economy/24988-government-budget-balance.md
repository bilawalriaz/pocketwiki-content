# Government budget balance

A government budget balance is the difference between revenues and spending over a given period, typically a financial year. A positive result is a surplus, a negative result a deficit. The deficit is a flow measured per unit of time; government debt is a stock measured at one point in time, equal under cash accounting to the cumulative sum of past deficits.

Under cash accounting, every deficit adds to outstanding debt. Under accrual accounting, only spending on current operations affects the balance; capital expenditure is excluded. The total deficit splits into the primary deficit (current spending minus current revenue) plus interest payments on accumulated debt. With $G_t$ as spending, $T_t$ as revenue, $D_{t-1}$ as last year's debt, and $r$ as the interest rate:

$$\text{Primary deficit}_t = G_t - T_t$$

$$\text{Total deficit}_t = r \cdot D_{t-1} + G_t - T_t$$

Debt evolves according to the government budget constraint $D_t = (1+r)D_{t-1} + G_t - T_t$, since each year's deficit must be financed by issuing new bonds.

The observed deficit is often split into a structural deficit, which persists across the business cycle because baseline spending exceeds baseline tax levels, and a cyclical deficit, which arises because revenues fall and unemployment-related outlays rise at the trough. Some economists dispute the split, arguing the cycle is too hard to measure to be useful. The fiscal gap, proposed by Auerbach and Kotlikoff, extends the idea by comparing projected lifetime government commitments, including future health and pension spending, against projected revenues; in ageing societies this gap can be much larger than the headline deficit.

Because flows of saving, investment, and trade are linked through national accounts, the government balance cannot be analysed alone. GDP equals $Y = C + I + G + (X - M)$, where $C$ is consumption, $I$ private investment, $G$ government spending, and $X - M$ net exports. Since income also equals $C + S + T$, where $S$ is saving and $T$ is net taxes, combining gives the sectoral balances identity:

$$(S - I) = (G - T) + (X - M)$$

By definition, the surpluses or deficits of the private, government, and foreign sectors sum to zero. If the private sector moves into surplus, one of the other two sectors must move into deficit. Between 2007 and 2009, US households cut borrowing and saving rose as the housing bubble collapsed and business investment slumped. The 2011 US federal deficit of roughly 10% of GDP was largely the mirror image of a private surplus near6% and a foreign capital surplus near 4%, not the result of deliberate fiscal expansion.

Modern Money Theory draws a related point from the same identity: a government deficit, by accounting necessity, adds net financial assets to the private sector, since the state has injected more money and bonds than it withdrew in taxes. A surplus does the reverse. Within this view, deficits are required to support a growing economy that wants to avoid deflation, and surpluses are appropriate only when aggregate demand threatens inflation.

Four hypotheses describe how deficits affect the rest of the economy. The crowding-out hypothesis holds that government borrowing pushes up interest rates and displaces private investment. Ricardian equivalence holds the opposite: rational households anticipate future taxes and save more today, so deficit-financed tax cuts have no real effect; the result requires permanent deficits, no uncertainty, and no liquidity constraints, and empirical evidence is mixed. The Keynesian view, articulated by William Vickrey, treats recessions as moments when deficit spending is economically necessary, because it adds to disposable income, supports demand, and finances public investment that enlarges the capital stock left to future generations. The concern that deficits burden future generations is, in this view, a false analogy with household borrowing, since a government is not a household and deficits under unused capacity add to, rather than subtract from, the productive endowment.

In practice, deficit and debt levels reflect many forces beyond the cycle. Higher activity raises revenues; downturns raise automatic spending on unemployment programs. Higher interest rates raise debt service costs, and high debt-to-GDP ratios invite risk premia from creditors, worsening the balance further. Inflation erodes the real value of debt, but if investors anticipate it they demand higher yields, so inflation tends to be bought at a permanent interest-rate penalty. Political variables also matter: election years, coalition governments, the number of effective parties, and ruling-coalition ideology all correlate with measured balances, while institutional quality and fiscal rules shape longer-run sustainability.

When the structural balance is judged too large, the textbook remedies are to raise taxes, cut spending, or both. Loopholes can be closed in place of rate increases, and refinancing public debt can reduce debt service without touching primary spending. A balanced-budget rule, under which expenditure cannot exceed realised revenues, is one institutional way to enforce discipline. When debt becomes unsustainable, governments have sometimes cancelled part of it through restructuring that imposes losses on creditors, as in the 2011 Greek "haircut", which contributed to the subsequent Cypriot banking crisis.
