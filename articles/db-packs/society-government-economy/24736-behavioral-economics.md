# Behavioral economics

Behavioral economics studies how psychological, cognitive, and social factors cause people's economic choices to deviate from the predictions of standard economic theory, which assumes fully rational, self-interested agents.

## What it challenges
Standard economics models people as *homo economicus*: rational actors who optimize with perfect information and stable preferences. Behavioral economics shows this is wrong in systematic, predictable ways. Two foundational ideas carry most of the field.

**Bounded rationality** (Herbert Simon, Nobel 1978). Real decision-makers have limited cognitive capacity, limited time, and expensive information. So they *satisfice*, accepting a "good enough" option rather than the optimal one. Simon compared it to scissors: one blade is human cognitive limits, the other is the structure of the environment. Minds succeed by exploiting familiar patterns.

**Prospect theory** (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979). People evaluate outcomes as gains or losses relative to a reference point, not in absolute terms. Four core findings:

- *Loss aversion*: losses hurt about 2.25 times more than equivalent gains please (Kahneman and Tversky, 1992).
- *Reference dependence*: outcomes are judged against a neutral baseline, not absolute wealth.
- *Non-linear probability weighting*: small probabilities are overweighted, large ones underweighted, producing an inverse-S curve.
- *Diminishing sensitivity*: the marginal impact of additional gains or losses shrinks as they grow larger.

A revised *cumulative prospect theory* appeared in 1992, removing the original editing stage and allowing cumulative probability weighting.

## The underlying cognitive machinery

Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* frames this as two systems. System 1 is fast, automatic, and relies on heuristics, mental shortcuts that usually work but produce systematic errors like hindsight bias, confirmation bias, and framing effects. System 2 is slow, deliberate, and effortful. Most everyday economic decisions are driven by System 1, which is why biases are predictable rather than random noise.

## Biases and heuristics that matter

Common search heuristics include *satisficing* (stop at the first acceptable option), *directed cognition* (treat each search as if it were the last), and *elimination by aspects* (screen by one attribute at a time).

Biases that distort decisions include *present bias* (overvaluing immediate rewards), *anchoring* (over-relying on a reference number), *framing effects* (different wordings of the same choice produce different choices), *herd behavior* (copying the crowd), *confirmation bias*, *status quo bias*, and the *endowment effect* (valuing what you own more than what you don't). The *gambler's fallacy* and *hot hand fallacy* are mirror errors: believing a streak of independent events is "due" to reverse or continue.

## Applying it: nudge theory

Thaler and Sunstein's *Nudge* (2008) turns these insights into policy. A *nudge* is any change to the *choice architecture* (the environment in which people decide) that predictably alters behavior without forbidding options or changing economic incentives. Putting fruit at eye level nudges healthy eating; banning junk food is not a nudge. The UK created a Behavioural Insights Team in 2010; Sunstein led US regulatory policy under Obama. The companion concept *sludge* names pointless frictions that block beneficial actions.

## Criticisms and limits

Three serious objections recur. First, neoclassical defenders (Alchian 1950; Becker 1962) argue the law of demand and profit-maximization results hold even when individuals behave irrationally, because market-level selection pressure washes out individual mistakes. Second, experiments often rely on *WEIRD* samples (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic), limiting cross-cultural validity. Third, the field has a *replication crisis*: a 2024 *Wall Street Journal* review reported that fewer than half of nudging experiments sustain effects when re-tested at scale. Critics like David Gal also argue the field catalogs *how* behavior deviates without building a unified alternative theory to replace neoclassical economics.
