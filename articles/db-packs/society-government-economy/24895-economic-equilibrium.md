# Economic equilibrium

In economics, equilibrium is a state in which supply and demand are balanced, so prices and quantities stop changing on their own. The price at which this happens is the market-clearing or competitive price, and the matching quantity is the market-clearing quantity. The price stays put until an outside shift in demand or supply (income, tastes, technology, or costs) moves the curves.

Dixon frames any equilibrium by three properties: agents' behaviour must be internally consistent (P1), no agent should be able to improve its outcome by acting differently (P2), and the equilibrium should be the outcome of a dynamic process that returns the market to it after a disturbance (P3, or stability). Competitive equilibrium satisfies all three.

## Competitive equilibrium

The upward-sloping supply curve and downward-sloping demand curve cross at price $P_0$, where the quantity sellers offer equals the quantity buyers want. Above $P_0$, excess supply pushes the price down; below $P_0$, excess demand bids it up. Both forces restore $P_0$, which is why competitive equilibrium is stable in Dixon's sense.

Setting the supply function equal to the demand function gives the same answer algebraically. With $Q_s = 125 + 1.5P$ and $Q_d = 189 - 2.25P$, then $P = \frac{189-125}{1.5+2.25} = \frac{64}{3.75} \approx 17.07$. A worked table reaches the same point: at \$5.00, demand and supply both equal 12,000; at \$3.00 there is a shortage of 8,000; at \$8.00 there is a surplus of 12,000.

Shifts move the equilibrium. A rise in disposable income raises quantity demanded at every price, shifting demand right and lifting the equilibrium price (a positive comparative-static effect). A rightward shift of supply, from better technology or lower costs, lowers the equilibrium price. Comparing two such snapshots is comparative statics.

## Monopoly equilibrium

A monopoly finds its equilibrium where marginal revenue equals marginal cost (MR = MC). The quantity comes from that intersection and the price is read up to the demand curve. This outcome fails P1 (the quantity the firm offers is not what buyers would choose at that price) and fails P2 for buyers, who would prefer more at the monopoly price. It still satisfies P3: above the profit-maximising point the firm raises the price, and below it the firm restricts output. The equilibrium is stable even though it is not the competitive outcome.

## Nash and subgame perfect equilibrium

Where strategic interaction matters and price-taking fails, economists use the Nash equilibrium: a set of strategies where each agent is doing the best it can given what the others are doing. Its original use was the Cournot duopoly of 1838, where two firms selling a homogeneous product each pick output, knowing that total output sets the market price through the demand curve. Each firm's profit-maximising output, given the other's choice, is its best-response or reaction function, and the Nash equilibrium is the intersection of the two reaction functions. It satisfies P1 and P2, but its P3 stability is weaker: Cournot's best-response dynamics converge only when reaction-function slopes are shallower than -1, and the dynamics are myopic because firms ignore that the rival will react in turn. Evolutionary stability is an alternative stability concept.

For multi-stage games, the subgame perfect equilibrium (SPE) refines Nash: pure strategies are played on the equilibrium path, and off the path the equilibrium is supported by mixed strategies, so credibility rests on threats that are never carried out. The worst-case efficiency loss, the price of anarchy, can approach zero in decentralised oligopoly; the price of stability for two firms is at least $2\sqrt{2}-2 \approx \frac{5}{6}$, so roughly 83% of optimal surplus is preserved. Timing matters too: a simultaneous capacity-then-price game has no pure-strategy equilibrium, while a sequential one always does.

## Dynamic equilibrium and disequilibrium

Equilibrium need not be static. In dynamic equilibrium, several quantities grow at a common rate so their ratios stay constant; output per worker and capital per worker stay flat while the economy expands at the exogenous population growth rate, and in monetary models all nominal values inflate together while real values and the inflation rate are unchanged. Comparing two such paths is comparative dynamics: a permanent rise in the saving rate, for instance, lifts capital per worker permanently but leaves the growth rate unchanged.

A market in disequilibrium fails the supply-equals-demand condition. Goods markets often clear quickly once sluggish prices adjust, but labour markets can stay in disequilibrium for long stretches. Modern mainstream and Keynesian economics argue equilibrium need not coincide with market clearing: efficiency-wage models and credit rationing produce equilibrium with unemployment or unmet loan demand, and underemployment equilibrium combines persistent joblessness with deficient aggregate demand. Samuelson warns that an equilibrium price carries no moral judgement; during the Great Irish Famine of 1845–1852, Irish potatoes cleared at a price set by the wider British market, but Irish buyers could not afford that price and starved while food was exported, leaving distribution untouched by market clearing.
