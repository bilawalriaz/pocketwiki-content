# Civil society

Civil society is the "third sector" of social life, distinct from the state and the market. It groups the family, private sphere, civic associations, NGOs, charities, unions, religious bodies, and any non-governmental organization that aggregates citizens' interests and lets them act collectively.

## Origin of the term

Aristotle used *koinōnía politikḗ* ("political community") to name the polis as an association of associations aimed at *eudaimonia* (human flourishing). Cicero rendered this *societas civilis* in Latin, where it became a near-synonym for *res publica*, the Roman republic. After the rise of monarchical sovereignty it came to denote the corporate estates of feudal landholders, distinct from the prince's power.

## How the meaning shifted

For the Greeks and Romans, "civil society" was indistinguishable from the state: it named the good, just political community. Under feudalism the concept faded as political thought turned to just war.

The 1648 Treaty of Westphalia consolidated territorial monarchies. Enlightenment thinkers such as Hobbes and Locke argued that people escape a "war of all against all" by forming a state to protect life, liberty, and property. They still treated civil society and the state as fused.

Hegel broke the fusion. In *Elements of the Philosophy of Right*, *bürgerliche Gesellschaft* became a "system of needs" between family and state: a non-political realm of market exchange, private property, and individual rights, regulated by civil code. Marx kept Hegel's separation but read civil society as the domain of competing bourgeois private interests, with the state acting as that class's executive arm. Gramsci reframed civil society as the carrier of cultural and ideological hegemony, the spread of ruling-class ideas and culture that makes capitalist order seem normal.

Aleksander Smolar first used "civil society" in its modern oppositional sense in 1978–79, and Eastern European dissidents including Václav Havel popularized it against Communist states (Polish Solidarity in 1980–1981 notably did not use the term). After 1989 the concept was absorbed into a neoliberal agenda that recast the third sector as a substitute for welfare-state provision, a shift tied to the Washington Consensus and to structural-adjustment loans the World Bank and IMF imposed on indebted states.

## How it functions

**Democracy and political culture.** Gabriel Almond and Sidney Verba showed that participatory associations inform citizens and improve voting. Robert D. Putnam extended this: even non-political groups build social capital, the networks and norms of reciprocity (defined by the World Bank as "the institutions, relationships, and norms that shape the quality and quantity of a society's social interactions") that let strangers cooperate on public goods. Putnam's Italian study found civic involvement correlated with political sophistication, trust, and competence.

**Counter-evidence.** Thomas Carothers, Sheri Berman, and Larry Diamond showed the link is contingent, not automatic; dense associational life can also fragment a polity. Berman's study of the Weimar Republic is the canonical case: economic depression spawned many civic groups, which the NSDAP infiltrated, mobilizing networks of civic trust to anti-democratic ends. The NSDAP rose from 2.63% of the vote in 1928 to the largest Reichstag party after the 1932 elections. In established democracies, proliferating single-issue groups can crowd out broader debate and distort policy toward well-organized interests.

**Political participation.** Civil-society groups lower the cost of collective action and supply citizens with knowledge of rights, issues, and tactics. The drawback is single-issue myopia, which David Rieff argued diverts voters from structural problems like globalization toward narrow causes.

**Economic growth.** Strong civil society is usually treated as pro-growth because it voices labor, faith, and consumer concerns, while dense social networks raise productivity. U.S. data link stronger community networks to lower high-school dropout rates. Carothers qualifies the claim: South Korea industrialized before building a robust civil society, while Bangladesh's rich civic life has not prevented persistent poverty, and powerful Latin American unions may in some cases have retarded growth.

**Global sphere.** Critics of globalization frame civil society as a transnational counterweight to corporate and state power; others note it can be foreign-funded and contested. The UN's iCSO System, run by the Department of Economic and Social Affairs, channels NGO input into the UN. Environmental groups illustrate the mechanism: agenda-setting, public information, and demand creation all push policy change.

## Limits of the concept

Sociologist Bojan Baća argues that the dominant frameworks, functionalist (civil society as democratizer), normativist (civil society as civilized society), and structuralist (civil society as a sector), can reduce empirical reality. Civil society works better as a historically and geographically contingent bundle of practices than as a universal template, with its content and effects varying sharply by region and period.
