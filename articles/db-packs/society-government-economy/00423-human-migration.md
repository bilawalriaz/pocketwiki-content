# Human migration

Human migration is the cross-boundary movement of people who intend to settle, whether permanently or temporarily. The label "migrant" covers very different experiences: a nurse relocating for work, a family fleeing a war zone, a villager displaced by a dam project. Understanding migration means sorting this wide label into the categories that drive policy, economics, and human outcomes.

## Who moves, and why

The most important distinction is voluntary versus forced movement, though the line blurs where motivators overlap (economic collapse is often caused by conflict).

- **Migrants** change residence voluntarily for reasons such as employment or healthcare.
- **Refugees** (UNHCR definition) are forced to flee violence or persecution, often without documentation.
- **Asylum seekers** leave unwillingly because their quality of life has degraded (instability, crime) and formally request shelter; they become refugees only if the request is granted.
- **Internally displaced persons (IDPs)** are forced from home but remain inside their own country.

Nomadic movements and short-term travel (tourism, commuting) are excluded because they lack intent to settle.

Voluntary moves respond to **push factors** (war, unemployment, drought at the origin) and **pull factors** (wages, security at the destination). Everett S. Lee's push–pull model formalised what Ernst Georg Ravenstein had observed in the UK in the 1880s: most moves are short-distance, long-distance migrants head for big cities, families move less than young adults, and migration happens in stages, rural to town to city, a pattern now called step migration. Climate cycles drive analogous movements of whole peoples: Bogumil Terminski's "migratory domino effect" describes how shifts in Central Asian pastureland push successive tribes into Anatolia, the Pannonian Plain, Mesopotamia, and China.

## Scale and direction

Despite the political weight given to international migration, the dominant form globally is internal: seasonal farm work, urbanisation, suburbanisation. Internationally, the migrant share of world population has held remarkably steady at roughly 3% for five decades, 3.3% in 2015. In that year, migrants contributed 9.4% of global GDP, far above their population share. The Centre for Global Development estimates that open borders could add $78 trillion to world GDP.

The direction of flows is more varied than headlines suggest. In 2013, 38% of international moves were South–South (between developing countries) and 23% North–North (between rich OECD states), not the South–North flow that dominates political debate. The Global North added more migrants in absolute terms since 2000 (32 million versus 25 million), but the South grew faster (2.3% versus 2.1% annually). Women make up almost half of all migrants, a major shift over fifty years, though female migration is still often framed as family-linked rather than independent. Major corridors such as Mexico–USA each exceed two million people; New York City hosts the largest ethnic Chinese population outside Asia, about 894,000 in 2017.

## Why the economics work

The dominant economic theories each capture a different slice:

- **Neoclassical theory** sees migration as driven by wage gaps, from labour-surplus, low-wage regions to labour-scarce, high-wage ones, best where immigration law does not intervene.
- **Dual labour market theory** argues rich countries structurally need low-skilled workers for jobs natives reject, which raises wages and pulls more migrants.
- **New economics of labour migration** treats the household, not the individual, as the decision unit: migration is a risk strategy whose **remittances**, the funds migrants send home, provide capital and insurance.
- **World-systems theory** frames migration as a consequence of global capitalism: trade decline in one region pushes people toward vibrant economies, with post-colonial dependence persisting.
- **Osmosis theory** analogises countries to cells and borders to semipermeable membranes, with people moving from low to high "migration pressure" set by natural factors in simple cases and socio-economic factors in complex ones.

A 2021 Boston Consulting Group survey found 72% of executives believed migration benefited their countries. Remittances are so central to some developing economies that UN Sustainable Development Goal 10 targets cutting transaction costs to under 3% by 2030.

## Forced displacement today

By the end of 2018, forced migrants totalled 67.2 million (25.9 million refugees plus 41.3 million IDPs). The World Bank estimated 19.5 million refugees in 2014, 7.9% of all migrants. Recent shocks show the scale: roughly 6 million Ukrainians fled after the 2022 Russian invasion, and 3 million Syrians had fled over 2011–2014. **Transit migration**, a 1990 UN term for migrants moving through third countries (Central Americans via Mexico toward the US; sub-Saharan Africans via Libya toward Europe), is contested as Eurocentric, accused of externalising EU border control and hiding the multi-year violence of the journey. In November 2025, 13 NGO rescue ships suspended coordination with the Libyan coast guard over violent interceptions and abuse in detention.

## Governance and the sovereignty gap

Migration is transnational in fact, national in law. States keep sovereign control of entry and stay, so policies diverge sharply on visas, work permits, and residency. The 1951 Refugee Convention and the 1966 International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights are widely ratified; the 1990 Migrant Workers Convention is not, because traditional destination states have not signed it. The 2018 Global Compact for Safe, Orderly and Regular Migration is the first negotiated statement balancing migrant rights against state sovereignty, adopted by UN consensus (152 to 5), but it is non-binding. Colonial history still shapes flows: subjugation, slavery, indenture, then settler colonialism displaced indigenous peoples; late-colonial and post-colonial ties continue to channel migration. Modern states use selective foreign-worker programmes to import skilled or cheap labour, often against ethnic-nationalist resistance.

No legal category yet exists for people displaced by environmental disaster or climate change, distinct from refugees or economic migrants, and the forced-versus-voluntary boundary stays analytically slippery wherever war and economic collapse reinforce each other.
