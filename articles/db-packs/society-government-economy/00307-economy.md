# Economy

An economy is the social system that organises how a society produces, distributes, trades, and consumes goods and services. Production choices are shaped by culture, values, technology, political structure, and history. Making sense of any modern headline about growth, inflation, or jobs requires a small mental model: what an economy is, how it has changed shape, what its main sectors are, how it is measured, and which big ideas still divide economists.

## Etymology and the modern concept

The word *economy* comes from the Greek *oikonomia*, "household management." Its current sense, meaning the economic system of a country or region, only developed in the 1650s. People did not speak of "the economy" as a single object they could track until much later.

Early economies were based on subsistence farming. The Sumerians developed a large-scale economy using commodity money. Babylonians created early legal systems for debt, contracts, and property. The shekel was a unit of both weight and currency, like the British pound originally denoting a mass of silver.

## From mercantilism to industrial capitalism

In the Middle Ages, economies were largely local and subsistence-based. The Age of Discovery, including voyages by Columbus and da Gama, created the first global economy, financed by early venture capital. European nation-states then adopted mercantilism, using tariffs and colonies to control trade and accumulate national wealth.

Adam Smith challenged mercantilism directly. He defined a national economy driven by competition, supply and demand, and the division of labour. The Industrial Revolution, from the 18th century on, replaced mercantilism in practice with industrial capitalism and enabled mass production.

## 20th-century debates

The contemporary idea of "the economy" gained prominence during the 1930s Great Depression. After World War II, a major debate emerged between two schools of thought. Neoliberalism, advocated by Hayek and Friedman, pushed for global free trade, deregulation, and limited government. Keynesianism, from Keynes's 1936 *General Theory*, argued that the state should control markets through fiscal and monetary policy to manage aggregate demand. The post-war "economic miracle" in the West also birthed the mass consumption, or "affluent," society. In most countries today, the economic system is called a social market economy, a compromise that lets markets do most allocation while the state provides a welfare floor and stabilises demand.

## Sectors and measurement

Economists split activity into three sectors. The **primary** sector extracts raw materials: farming, fishing, mining, forestry. The **secondary** sector manufactures goods: construction, energy, factory output. The **tertiary** sector provides services: retail, finance, healthcare, education, transport, software. A predominantly primary economy is agrarian; a heavy secondary share is industrial; a tertiary share above roughly two-thirds is the marker Bell used in 1973 to call a society post-industrial. Some authors credit Ivan Illich with an earlier use of the same idea; Bell's book made the term standard.

Economic health is measured by indicators like **Gross Domestic Product (GDP)**, the monetary value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a specific period. "Final" is the key word: GDP counts a car once, when it leaves the factory, not the steel and glass that went into it. **Median income** often better represents the economic situation of the average person, since it tracks the person in the middle rather than an average pulled up by a few very high earners. Analysts distinguish between the **real economy** (actual production) and the **paper economy** (financial markets trading claims on real output).

## 21st-century shifts

The late 20th and early 21st centuries saw the post-industrial shift continue, with the service sector surpassing industrial production in most high-income countries. The spread of the Internet after 2000 created the information economy, centred on e-commerce. The growth of nations like China, Brazil, and India has brought non-Western economic models to global attention, challenging the dominance of traditional Western theory and reopening the question of which coordinating mechanism, whether market, state, household, or network, should handle which decisions.
