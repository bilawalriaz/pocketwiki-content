# Executive (government)

The executive is the branch of government that executes and enforces the law. In liberal democracies it operates as one of three coequal branches; in communist states it is typically fused into a unified state apparatus.

## The job: enforce the law and run the state

The executive's core tasks are enforcing legislation, running the bureaucracy, and conducting day-to-day governing. Its scope is set by a constitution, varies by country, and shifts over time. In democracies it usually wields broad influence, but the law almost always places limits on it.

A *decree* or *executive order* is a rule issued directly by the executive with the force of law. *Delegated legislation* is law the legislature explicitly hands over to the executive to write, as when the U.S. Congress delegates rulemaking to federal agencies.

## Two structural patterns

The shape of the executive depends on how power is divided from the legislature. This distinction is the single most useful lens for making sense of any government.

In systems based on the **separation of powers**, the executive is structurally independent of the legislature. Each branch checks the others: the legislature passes laws, the executive enforces them, and the judiciary interprets them. The U.S. federal government is the textbook case. Ministers are appointed by the president and do not sit in Congress.

In systems with **fusion of powers**, typically parliamentary systems such as the United Kingdom, the executive is drawn from the legislature. Ministers are usually members of the party or coalition that controls parliament, so the two bodies are fused rather than independent. Because parliamentary sovereignty means executive power flows from the legislature, parliament can withdraw confidence in the executive, forcing either a cabinet reshuffle or a general election. The executive still holds wide practical power through its control of the bureaucracy, especially over economic and foreign policy.

## Head of state versus head of government

Most countries split the top of the executive into two roles. The **head of state** is the symbolic representative of the country, a monarch in places like the UK or a president in republics, and typically survives changes of government. The **head of government** leads the executive in practice, usually as prime minister or chancellor, sets the policy agenda, and answers to the legislature.

In a presidential system the same person is both head of state and head of government, because the president is directly elected and appoints ministers. South Korea is a partial exception, with a prime minister who assists but is subordinate to the president. In some Swiss cantons, ministers are directly elected by voters. In the Westminster parliamentary model, the separation of powers is looser than in the U.S. model, because ministers sit in Parliament and help write the very laws they enforce.

## The cabinet

The cabinet is a small group of ministers who head the major departments of state. The standard portfolio set covers:

- **Head of government** (prime minister or equivalent): overall administration.
- **Defence minister**: armed forces, military policy, external security.
- **Interior minister**: police, law enforcement, internal order.
- **Foreign minister**: diplomacy, foreign policy, international relations.
- **Finance minister**: treasury, fiscal policy, national budget.
- **Justice minister**: criminal prosecution, corrections, court-order enforcement.

A confidence vote in the legislature can bring down the entire cabinet. In presidential systems, ministers serve at the pleasure of the president and are not answerable to the legislature in the same way.

## Why the split matters

The core tension the executive manages is the trade-off between acting decisively and being held accountable. A president with a fixed term and a separate legislature can act independently but can also deadlock with it. A prime minister fused with the legislature can pass a budget quickly but can be removed by a single vote of no confidence. Each country's institutional design is its answer to that trade-off.
