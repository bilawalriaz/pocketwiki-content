# Political party

A political party is an organization that fields candidates under a shared label in elections and helps them govern, usually uniting members around common policy or ideological goals. Parties now structure political competition in nearly every country, democracies and autocracies alike, though democracies tend to host more of them. From the late 18th century onward, parties turned politics from elite contests into mass-mobilized systems, becoming the main intermediaries between citizens and the state.

## Origins and history

Factional thinking goes back to Plato and Aristotle debating factions in Classical Athens, and to the Populares and Optimates in the late Roman Republic. Modern parties emerged in late-18th-century Britain and the United States. Britain's Conservatives and the US Democrats are often called the oldest continuous parties. The US First Party System of the 1790s split Federalists and Democratic-Republicans over federal power. In 19th-century Europe, stable systems formed around liberal-conservative or religious divides, accelerated by the 1848 Revolutions. The 20th century added socialist parties to those alignments, while decolonization exported party systems to new states in Asia, Africa, and Latin America, often born from independence movements. Universal suffrage turned parties into genuine mass organizations connecting voters to government.

## Structure

A party has a leader (who may become head of government), executives such as a chair or secretary, and a general membership that usually pays dues and signs up to a platform. Democratic parties choose leaders through open contests; autocratic ones manage succession from above. Narrowly, a party is its candidates; broadly, it is the whole apparatus of voters, volunteers, paid staff, and elected office-holders behind them. What separates a party from a faction, parliamentary group, or advocacy group is its focus on winning elections. Most countries legally define what counts as a party.

## Why parties exist

Three accounts compete. The **social cleavages** account says parties crystallize pre-existing divisions of class, religion, or ethnicity into electoral choices, though critics note this is hard to test empirically and may run backwards, with parties shaping the cleavages. **Incentive-based** accounts argue parties coordinate candidates and legislators so they do not split the vote and can make collective decisions. **Psychological** accounts treat parties as cognitive shortcuts: voters find it easier to judge a label than evaluate every candidate, though party identification has weakened in recent decades.

## Types of party organization

| Type | Period | Logic |
|------|--------|-------|
| Elite | 19th c. | Minimal organization, funded by wealthy donors, focused on electioneering |
| Mass | With universal suffrage | Mobilizes ordinary members, large rolls, centralized around ideology (e.g. German Social Democrats) |
| Catch-all | 1950s–60s | Softens ideology, widens appeal, weakens member dependence |
| Cartel | Post-1970s | Relies on heavy state financing, marginalizes members, acts as a semi-state agency |
| Niche | Late 20th c. | Single-issue focus (environment, immigration) outside left-right |
| Business-firm | Late 20th c. | Built around a political entrepreneur rather than ideology |

## Party systems

The mix of parties in a country is shaped mainly by electoral rules. Under **non-partisan** systems, parties are banned or sidelined, as in Saudi Arabia or many local elections. **One-party** systems concentrate power in a single party, either by law (Cuba, China) or by dominance (Mexico, South Africa). **Two-party** systems such as the US and UK tend to emerge from single-member plurality voting, the pattern Duverger's law describes, though scholars still debate how strict that link is. **Multi-party** systems in countries like India, Turkey, and Brazil usually come from proportional representation, which produces coalition governments, broader representation, and at times instability.

## Funding and regulation

Parties raise billions in major democracies through member dues, corporate donations, trade unions, self-funding by candidates, and direct state grants. Public funding aims to level the playing field; private funding advocates treat donations as political speech. Regulations cap contributions and spending and require disclosure, but clientelism and vote-buying persist despite the rules.

## Ideology and symbols

Parties use ideologies such as liberalism, conservatism, socialism, communism, nationalism, and environmentalism to signal intent, set themselves apart, and mobilize voters. The economic left-right axis is the most familiar, but liberal-authoritarian, establishment-anti-establishment, and pluralist-anti-system dimensions also organize competition, and some parties stand mainly for patronage or personal advancement. Colors and symbols help voters identify parties, with cross-national conventions (red for left, blue for right) that invert only in notable cases such as the United States.

The shift from mass-membership parties of the early 20th century toward catch-all and cartel forms has coincided with falling membership rolls, leaving open whether parties still link citizens to the state as effectively as they once did.
