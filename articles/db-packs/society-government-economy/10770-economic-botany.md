# Economic botany

Economic botany is the study of how people use plants, and how plants gain and lose value as a result. It sits at the intersection of botany, which supplies the plants, and anthropology, which supplies the people. In practice it borrows from agronomy, chemistry, medicine, pharmacology, nutrition, ethnobotany, forestry, horticulture, and genetics, because a useful plant is rarely useful in only one way.

Botany itself began as an economic subject. Early Europeans studied plants almost entirely for medicine, and the first botanical instructions issued to explorers, drawn up by a cosmographer of Charles V, told them to record what people ate, what they used as drugs, and how useful species could be reproduced. Two plants that still anchor the field are teosinte, the wild Mexican grass that Native Americans selectively bred over thousands of years into modern maize, and rice, domesticated in Southeast Asia around 5,000 years ago. In both cases the point is the same: useful plants are not found, they are made, generation by generation.

## How plant value moves through empires

The history of economic botany is largely the history of moving useful plants between regions. The standard story credits the British in the 19th century, but the practice is far older. Between the 10th and 14th centuries, Islamic scholars on the Iberian Peninsula classified citrus into lemons, limes, sweet and sour oranges, pomelos, and grapefruit, and diffused every one of those fruits except the citron across the Mediterranean.

Spain then took up the same logic during the Age of Exploration, but for profit rather than science. Spanish kings sent expeditions to the Americas to fill royal gardens and to look for spices. Because the Portuguese controlled the Spice Islands from 1513, the Spanish turned to transplantation. In 1542 the first Viceroy of New Spain, Antonio de Mendoza, illegally obtained seeds from the East Indies. His son Francisco de Mendoza received broad trading rights in 1558 and successfully cultivated ginger and China root, although documentation was poor and the project died with him. Ginger itself flourished in Hispaniola and by the late 16th century was the island's largest crop, larger than sugar, so successful that it began to damage the sugar industry. The same ginger later grew around Seville, but never became a major European export.

## Plants as medicine

A large share of modern plant research is medical. In the United States alone, medical research budgets reach roughly $95 billion, much of it spent tracing how plant compounds act on the body.

Ephedra, a gymnosperm, produces the alkaloid ephedrine, which mimics epinephrine. Because ephedrine can be highly toxic, chemists derived pseudoephedrine, now common in cold medicines and also used to manufacture methamphetamine. Echinacea is sold as a herbal remedy for colds, with mixed results, though some studies suggest it helps with upper respiratory infections. American ginseng has been tested for immune support, and steamed roots heated to 120 °C, exposing cancer cells to ginsenoside for 72 hours, reduced cell proliferation in laboratory work, suggesting anti-cancer potential. Grape seed extract is a strong antioxidant, useful against liver and kidney damage from drug overdoses and against aflatoxin contamination, and has been linked to weight control in studies in China.

## Plants as food

Research on food plants usually tries to enlarge the edible organ, extend the growing range, or, less often, find a new crop. Rice shows both the scale and the difficulty of the work. Dwarf variants allowed several Asian countries to feed themselves, but rice still needs careful nitrogen management, because fertiliser applied at the wrong moment attracts pests, so farmers read leaf colour to time applications. Rice husks, once waste, yield an ethyl acetate extract with useful antioxidant properties, a small example of how a by-product can become a second crop.

The teosinte-to-maize transition is the clearest case of human selection overriding natural reproduction. The earliest maize ears had only about eight rows of kernels. Modern corn has so fused its kernels to the cob that it cannot sow itself; the kernels simply rot. This is a failure for the plant and a gift for the harvester. Genotyping-by-sequencing now helps match corn varieties to soils and climates more cheaply than older methods.

Two other crops illustrate how appearance, not just yield, drives value. Florida dominated United States citrus from the 19th century until recently, but orange colour has nothing to do with ripeness. The orange pigments only appear in cool nights, so tropical growers expose the fruit to ethylene to strip away chlorophyll and reveal the underlying beta-carotenes. Apples were never native to North America, yet the continent now holds the world's greatest apple diversity, thanks in part to John Chapman (Johnny Appleseed), who spent 48 years planting seeds across the American northwest. Three varieties, Red Delicious, Golden Delicious, and Granny Smith, still dominate the market.

## Ornamental plants

Ornamentals are also economic crops, sold to homeowners, offices, and landscaping firms. Carnations are valued for colour, size, fragrance, and vase life, but they only thrive around 10–15 °C, which is why greenhouse production is standard in warm regions. Crape myrtles, imported from southern Asia more than 180 years ago, now number over 35 cultivated varieties and dominate landscaping in the southern United States, partly because they tolerate a wide range of conditions and can serve as trap hosts for pests.
