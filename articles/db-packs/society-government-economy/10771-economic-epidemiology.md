# Economic epidemiology

Economic epidemiology merges epidemiology and economics to model how individual incentives shape disease spread. Transmission is treated as a population-level outcome of personal decisions about risk, treatment, and prevention, so policies that ignore behavioral responses can backfire. A related branch, strategic epidemiology, applies game theory to the same interplay.

## Prevalence-dependence

The central mechanism is prevalence-dependence, also called disinhibition: people adjust protective behavior as their perceived risk changes with disease prevalence. When risk rises, individuals invest more in prevention, for example cutting contact, using bednets, or seeking vaccination. When risk falls, they do the opposite. The classic analogy is seatbelts, which lower the expected cost of a crash and can therefore encourage more reckless driving, partially offsetting their safety benefit.

The same feedback shows up in disease control. Studies of people receiving highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) for HIV found that those who believed treatment or an undetectable viral load protected against transmission were more likely to have unprotected sex regardless of their own HIV status, and models predicted that introducing HAART could raise HIV incidence even as it cut AIDS mortality. Mass indoor spraying against malaria reduces mosquito biting nuisance, which can cause people to abandon bednets.

Whether this helps or hurts depends on behavioral elasticity. With inelastic responses, per capita infection risk rises with prevalence. With elastic responses, individual action can flatten or reverse that rise. The downside is symmetric: when perceived risk wanes because of vaccination or falling prevalence, risk-taking rebounds, the susceptible pool rebuilds, and the disease can reinvade.

This dynamic complicates public subsidies. When prevalence is high, people already protect themselves, so subsidies add little. When prevalence is low, subsidies are useful, but so is the perception of risk. The lever often arrives at the wrong moment.

## Vaccination as social dilemma

Immunization is a textbook conflict between private and collective interest. Self-interested uptake falls below the social optimum because individuals do not internalize the benefit they confer on others. Combined with prevalence-dependence, this means demand rises as disease spreads and falls as it retreats, leaving a permanent unvaccinated fraction whenever a vaccine has monetary, medical, or perceived side-effect costs. As a result, voluntary vaccination alone cannot eradicate a vaccine-preventable disease if people act in their own self-interest.

Partially effective vaccines sharpen the problem. Models of a hypothetical HIV vaccine that reduced but did not eliminate risk found that small increases in risk behavior could raise HIV incidence even with a highly efficacious product, a finding that helped justify holding back semi-efficacious HIV vaccines.

## COVID-19 and excess mortality

The field took shape in the early 1990s around HIV/AIDS, when epidemiologists recognized that sexual decisions made transmission an endogenous variable in a Nash equilibrium, linking behavior to outcomes through game theory. The SARS-CoV-2 pandemic supplied a fresh wave of empirical material.

From 1 January 2020 to 4 December 2022, the central estimate of US excess deaths linked to COVID-19 was about 1,277,204. Combined with 2021 US GDP per capita of $69,288 as a rough proxy for lost output, that implies roughly $88.5 billion in forgone production from excess mortality alone, before adding vaccine development, shutdowns, hospitalizations, fiscal stimulus, retirement-account losses, and inflation.

Reported cases also understated reality. A 2020 cross-sectional study in *JAMA Internal Medicine* that ran blood tests at ten US sites found seroprevalence indicated about ten times more infections than official counts captured, and municipal wastewater surveillance, echoing John Snow's cholera pump investigation, tracked community spread that case reporting missed. Models fed by diagnosed cases alone will therefore misread the behavioral incentives that economic epidemiology tries to capture.
