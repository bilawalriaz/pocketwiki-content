# Democracy

Democracy is government whose authority rests on the people. The word combines Greek *dêmos* (people) with *krátos* (power). In **direct democracy**, citizens vote on laws themselves, as in referendums. In **representative democracy**, they elect officials to govern on their behalf. **Liberal democracy** adds constitutional limits on majority power so that minorities and individual rights (speech, press, assembly) cannot be voted away. Unchecked majority rule risks a "tyranny of the majority," in which 51% overrides the remaining 49%.

## What Democracy Requires

No single definition exists; over 2,200 adjectives modify the word in English. **Minimalist** theorists (Joseph Schumpeter) treat democracy as a competitive election among elites. **Maximalist** theorists add civil liberties, human rights, and serious deliberation. The UN definition centres on human rights plus the freely expressed will of the people, exercised through periodic, genuine elections. Common core features include political equality (one person, one vote), rule of law, political freedom, and majority rule bounded by minority rights.

A **republic** (rule without a monarch, by consent) overlaps with democracy but is distinct: the United States is a democratic republic, the United Kingdom a democratic constitutional monarchy.

## A Brief History

Democratic assemblies appeared independently in many societies. Athens in 508 BC, under Cleisthenes, created the first well-documented system: adult male citizens (about one in four residents) gathered in the *ekklesia* to legislate, and large juries were chosen by **sortition** (random lottery). The Roman Republic added popular assemblies but weighted votes toward the wealthy. Vaishali in ancient India and the Iroquois Confederacy (c. 1142–1660) developed republican and representative forms, while medieval Iceland, Scandinavia, and the Spanish Cortes of León (1188) used limited-franchise assemblies.

The **Magna Carta (1215)** curbed the English king and seeded the idea that rulers must obey law. The **Glorious Revolution (1688)** and **Bill of Rights (1689)** made Parliament sovereign, locked in regular elections, and protected property. John Locke's social-contract theory (1689), with claims of inalienable rights and consent of the governed, became the intellectual scaffolding for liberal democracy.

The American (1776–1789) and French (1789) Revolutions spread these ideas but excluded most adults: the early US electorate was roughly 6% of the population (white male property owners), and France's brief 1792 universal male suffrage excluded women. The nineteenth century saw property requirements fall, Black men gain nominal voting rights through the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments (enforcement came only with the 1965 Voting Rights Act), and France adopt universal male suffrage in 1848. Women won the vote in waves: New Zealand in 1893, the UK in 1918 (full equality in 1928). The **1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights** made periodic, genuine elections by universal suffrage a global standard.

## Waves and Backsliding

Samuel Huntington described democratization in waves: after World War I, after World War II (West Germany, Japan, Italy, and decolonising states such as India), and the "third wave" of the 1970s–1990s (Southern Europe, Latin America, much of Asia, and the post-Soviet bloc). Freedom House counted 123 electoral democracies by 2007, up from 40 in 1972, covering 58% of world population, though only 85 qualified as full liberal democracies.

Since 2005, the V-Dem Institute has recorded seventeen-plus consecutive years of net **democratic backsliding**: more countries losing democratic quality than gaining it. The V-Dem 2026 report counts 44 countries experiencing autocratization, and population-weighted measures show the decline is steeper than country counts suggest. Causes include economic inequality, polarization, disinformation, and pandemic-era emergency measures. Affected states include Poland, Hungary, Turkey, the Philippines, the US, and the UK. V-Dem simultaneously identifies 18 countries still actively democratizing, including Bolivia and Thailand.

## How Democratic Systems Are Built

**Direct democracy** (referendums, initiatives, recall) maximises participation but slows at scale. **Representative** systems use elected officials chosen by plurality, majority, or proportional methods. **Parliamentary** systems make the prime minister and cabinet answer to the legislature and removable by a vote of no confidence. **Presidential** systems separate a directly elected executive from the legislature, gaining stable terms but risking gridlock. **Semi-presidential** systems split executive power between president and prime minister, with the balance varying (France vs. Russia). **Constitutional monarchies** (UK, Japan) keep a ceremonial monarch alongside an elected parliament. **Sortition** selects decision-makers by lottery, the Athenian model now echoed in citizens' assemblies.

**Consociationalism** (Arend Lijphart) shares power among elites of distinct ethno-religious groups through grand coalitions and mutual vetoes, a model for deeply divided societies. **Deliberative democracy** (Jürgen Habermas, Joshua Cohen) holds that legitimacy comes from free, equal deliberation before decisions, not from vote-counting alone.

## Why Democracies Arise, and How They Fall

Modernization theory holds that wealth and education cause democracy, but evidence is weak: education often precedes democracy, and regimes use schooling for indoctrination. Deeper proposed drivers include stable borders, coastal access and trade, disease environments that shaped colonial institutions, and the spread of weapons accessible to ordinary citizens, which makes repression costly.

Democracies erode through several mechanisms. A **coup** is overt military or foreign-backed overthrow. A **self-coup** (Peru 1992, Russia 2003–04) is when an elected leader suspends the constitution or rigs elections to stay in power. **Gradual backsliding**, the more common modern route, sees elected executives weaken courts, capture regulators, attack press freedom, and tilt electoral rules in their own favour. The 1933 German Enabling Act is the canonical example. Steven Levitsky argues that voters usually prioritise everyday concerns over abstract democratic norms, so defending democracy falls mainly to institutions and political elites.

## Why Democracy, on Balance, Works

Three justifications carry weight. **Legitimacy** flows from consent: authority derived from the governed is the only kind they owe obedience to. **Epistemic superiority** rests on Condorcet's jury theorem: if each voter has even slightly better than a coin-flip chance of being right, large majorities converge on the correct answer. **Economic performance** is documented across long timescales: empirical studies covering 1960–2010 find transitions to democracy raise GDP per capita by roughly 20% over 25 years, and Acemoglu and Robinson argue in *Why Nations Fail* that democracies curb extractive monopolies and invest broadly in human capital.

Democratic governments can be slow, polarised, and vulnerable to misinformation when commercial media reward outrage and social platforms spread false stories faster than true ones. Public service broadcasting (strong in Europe and Japan, weak in the US) provides more policy-relevant, impartial content but faces deregulation pressure.
