# Technology and society

Technology and society co-produce each other: tools reshape how people live, and social values, choices, and institutions shape which tools get built. Evidence of this co-dependence stretches back to the earliest stone tools and continues through the printing press, telephone, and Internet. The first formal study of the relationship was *tektology*, an organizational science developed in early twentieth-century Imperial Russia. Modern scholars work in an interdisciplinary field called *science and technology studies*.

The simplest technologies are basic tools. Controlling fire and the later Neolithic Revolution expanded food sources, while the wheel extended human reach. Later advances lowered barriers to communication and made global interaction routine. New tools also created advanced economies and a leisure class, but generated pollution, depleted natural resources, and raised ethical questions. Neo-Luddism and anarcho-primitivism criticize technology as harmful and alienating; transhumanism and techno-progressivism treat further progress as beneficial.

Modern life is saturated with digital technology, which built a second global communication system. Education absorbed the Internet quickly: average school use sat near 2–3 percent in the early 1990s, roughly 60 percent by the late 1990s, and approached 100 percent by 2008. Social media platforms such as Instagram, Facebook, and Snapchat reshaped how Generation Y views itself, producing documented effects like "Facebook Depression," where users devalue themselves by comparison. Adolescent overuse is linked to sleep deprivation, obesity, and weaker academic performance.

Technology and economics have been intertwined since prehistoric barter, when toolmakers gained by specializing. In modern economies, technology is treated as a primary driver of growth; surplus wealth flows back into further technology. The COVID-19 pandemic showed firms using advanced digital tools, remote work, 3D printing, and big-data analytics adapting faster and sustaining job growth.

Funding for major technology comes from government, private firms, and individuals. Government agencies such as the US National Science Foundation and the UK's Microelectronics Education Programme have funded research and classroom technology. The military frequently drives innovation later adopted for civilian use. In OECD countries, industry carries out about two-thirds of scientific R&D, universities about 98 percent, and government about 10 percent (categories overlap). US military R&D has fallen from roughly 30 percent of federal research spending in the 1980s to under 10 percent. Crowdfunding platforms such as Kickstarter let inventors bypass corporate or government gatekeeping.

Science and technology interact complexly. Science can drive technology by demanding new instruments, while a supportive environment trains the engineers and innovators who exploit it. Stokes noted that pre-industrial innovators were often "improvers of technology" who knew no science. Research aimed at immediate technical application became common only after the Industrial Revolution. The United States relied on European science in the early twentieth century, and Japan advanced through applied science later; pure science is not the only path to technological leadership.

Technology influences social values and is influenced by them. Three values stand out: a mechanistic worldview that treats reality as analyzable parts; efficiency, extended from machines to all of society; and the belief in social progress, which replaced older cyclical theories of history after industrialization. Technology also enables large organizations, commercialized leisure, and near-instant global information flow.

Environmentally, technology cuts both ways. It can support sustainability and monitoring, but also produces pollution, depletes nonrenewable resources, and generates toxic, radioactive, electronic, plastic, and space waste. Information technology consumes energy from nonrenewable sources, contributing to global warming. Some polities phase out harmful technologies such as fossil fuels and nuclear fission when substitutes exist. Digital tools also enable the green transition through smart-city mobility, precision agriculture, and catastrophe prediction.

Societies shape technology through choices about distribution, cultural beliefs about consumption, and environmental values. Williams and Edge describe technology as a "garden of forking paths" rather than the unfolding of a single logic, so earlier decisions steer later outcomes. Jacques Ellul argued technology appears autonomous because humans avoid the responsibility built into it; Langdon Winner called this uncritical adoption "technological somnambulism." Governments steer outcomes through funding, liability law, and environmental legislation, yet the largest ecological disasters, including Chernobyl and the Aral Sea collapse, were themselves state projects.
