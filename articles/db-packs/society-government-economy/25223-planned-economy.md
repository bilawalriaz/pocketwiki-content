# Planned economy

A planned economy coordinates investment, production, and the allocation of capital goods through an economy-wide plan rather than market prices. Planning can be centralized, decentralized, participatory, or Soviet-type. The defining feature is *ex ante* coordination: decisions are set before production rather than adjusted afterward through prices.

## Varieties

A **command economy** adds public ownership and legally imposed allocation decisions to that definition. In practice it is the Soviet "administrative-command" model, run through hierarchical directives rather than a genuinely binding plan, a distinction Sovietologists drew in the 1980s. Yugoslavia kept central planning but added market socialism.

A **decentralized planned economy** distributes planning across producers and consumers, often through councils that negotiate output. It is associated with guild socialism, participatory economics, anarchism, libertarian Marxism, and Trotskyism. Revolutionary Catalonia (1936–1939) saw local collectives coordinate production, but the federated structure anarcho-syndicalist Diego Abad de Santillán envisioned was never built.

**Indicative planning** operates inside market economies, using government targets and incentives rather than directives, as in postwar French *dirigisme* or Sweden's Million Programme housing project (1965–1974).

## Historical roots and the Soviet path

Compulsory state planning existed in Hellenistic Egypt and India and parts of the Seleucid and Parthian empires; scholars have also described the Incan economy as a flexible command system built on labor mobilization. Some treat mercantilism as a form of planned economy.

In Russia, planning grew out of war communism (1918–1921) during the Civil War. Gosplan was founded in 1921, but the New Economic Policy (c. 1921–1928) partially relaxed controls before regular five-year plans began in 1928. Trotsky, an early advocate, argued planning could push industrial growth several times above the pre-war 6% rate. Sheila Fitzpatrick's consensus view is that Stalin adopted the Left Opposition's positions on industrialization and collectivization.

During the 1930s the Soviet Union cut private consumption's share of national income from 80% to 50%, driving rapid heavy-industry growth while agriculture contracted from labor shortages.

## Cybernetics and computer-aided planning

In 1959 Anatoly Kitov proposed a unified Soviet computer network ("Red Book"); the Defence Ministry blocked it. His 1962 OGAS network was also rejected, though Viktor Glushkov estimated OGAS would have returned fivefold over fifteen years. Polish economist Oskar Lange argued computers could solve the simultaneous equations of efficient allocation better than markets.

In 1971 Salvador Allende's Chile launched **Project Cybersyn**, installing telex machines in firms and feeding data into a real-time control room; during a 1972 truckers' strike it helped reroute supplies. The 1973 Pinochet coup ended the program. Cockshott and Cottrell's 1993 *Towards a New Socialism* revived the case for democratic, computer-based planning.

## The central debate

Supporters argue planning lets a state mobilize land, labor, and capital toward chosen goals: rapid heavy industrialization, universal welfare provision, or strong performance on social indicators such as life expectancy and (with definitional caveats) infant mortality.

Critics counter that planners cannot read dispersed consumer preferences and local conditions as well as prices do. Ludwig von Mises called this the **economic calculation problem**; Friedrich Hayek, extending Michael Polanyi's earlier insights, called it the **local knowledge problem**: only individuals know their own changing needs, and only a competitive market lets those with the most information serve the most consumers. Tibor Machan put it bluntly: without supply-and-demand markets, "it is difficult or impossible to funnel resources with respect to actual human preferences."

Robin Hahnel, defending participatory economics, argues that even a well-run central plan would suppress economic democracy and self-management, and that Soviet central planning survived mainly because it was propped up by totalitarian political power. Studies of Eastern Bloc command economies in the 1950s and 1960s found greater output fluctuations than in market economies, the opposite of what either supporters or Western critics expected.
