# Government debt

A country's gross government debt (also called public debt or sovereign debt) is the total of its financial liabilities in the form of debt instruments: bonds, bills, and loans that require future payment of interest or principal. It is a stock accumulated over time; the flow that adds to it is a deficit, which occurs when expenditures exceed revenues in a given period. Debt may be owed to domestic residents, to foreign residents (overlapping with external debt), or to both. In 2020, government debt worldwide reached about US$87.4 trillion, roughly 99% of global GDP, and accounted for almost 40% of all debt (corporate, household, and government combined), the highest share since the 1960s. The post-2007 rise traces mainly to fiscal stimulus during the Great Recession and the COVID-19 recession.

Governments borrow for three reasons. First, debt acts as a shock absorber: during recessions, tax revenues fall and automatic stabilisers such as unemployment benefits push spending up, and borrowing lets the state maintain services without raising taxes or cutting outlays mid-crisis. Second, borrowing can fund investments with long-term returns, such as infrastructure, that exceed the cost of servicing the debt. Third, the ability to issue debt has been central to state formation, and the rise of public debt markets is linked to the development of democracy, private financial markets, and modern economic growth. In 17th- and 18th-century England, including creditors in Parliament made lenders more willing to hold state debt because repayment could no longer be unilaterally suspended by the monarch.

Who lends depends on creditworthiness. Low-income, highly indebted states typically borrow from multilateral lenders such as the World Bank and from other governments, since private investors judge them too risky. Higher-income states issue sovereign bonds traded in secondary markets, where ratings agencies such as Moody's and Standard & Poor's reassess credit quality, and those ratings move bond prices.

## Measurement

The standard international measure is gross debt of the general government sector (central, state, regional, local, and social security funds, but not state-owned enterprises like commercial post offices), as defined by the IMF's Government Finance Statistics Manual 2014. Debt may be valued at market price, nominal value, or, as a fallback, face value (the undiscounted principal due at maturity). Net debt subtracts financial assets held as debt instruments but is reported less often because some assets are hard to value. Comparisons across countries use the debt-to-GDP ratio, where GDP is the annual value of goods and services produced. The OECD treats this ratio as a key indicator of fiscal sustainability.

Most governments also carry off-balance-sheet liabilities: unfunded mandates such as pay-as-you-go pension and healthcare obligations, and contingent liabilities such as disaster relief or implicit guarantees of subnational debt. EU member states have been required since 2010 to publish standardised figures that surface such hidden liabilities.

## Drivers and limits

Politicians face a deficits bias: each has an incentive to spend now and let the cost diffuse across taxpayers, a structure resembling a tragedy of the commons that, taken to its limit, ends in sovereign default. Many countries counter it with fiscal rules: Sweden's debt anchor, Germany's and Switzerland's debt brake, and the European Union's Stability and Growth Pact, which caps general government gross debt at 60% of GDP.

## Economic effects

Rising government debt can push up interest rates and crowd out private investment, since governments and firms compete for the same pool of savings. Empirical work finds growth effects turn negative above roughly 77% debt-to-GDP in developed countries (about 64% in developing countries), with each extra percentage point above the threshold costing around 0.017 percentage points of annual growth in advanced economies and 0.02 in emerging ones, per a World Bank study of 100 countries from 1980 to 2008. Very high debt also raises the risk of a debt crisis, in which a country cannot service its obligations and cannot borrow further; combined with a banking crisis, this can force economy-wide deleveraging, falling asset prices, collapsing tax revenue, and severe cuts to public services. Governments typically aim to keep enough fiscal breathing space to roughly double their debt if needed.

The Ricardian equivalence proposition challenges the crowding-out story by arguing that taxpayers, anticipating the future taxes needed to repay today's debt, save more, so debt financing has the same real effect as tax financing and interest rates need not rise. Beyond growth, debt imposes an inheritance on future generations because the citizens who benefit from today's spending are usually not the same ones who repay it, weakening intergenerational equity.

## Government debt is not household debt

Politicians often compare government borrowing to household borrowing. Most economists reject the analogy. Governments differ from households in several ways: a central bank that issues the country's own currency can in principle create money to repay debt; governments borrow at lower interest rates than individuals; they can raise revenue through taxation; they have indefinite planning horizons; much of their debt is owed domestically, so citizens owe other citizens; they typically hold more collateral; and contractions in government spending can themselves cause or deepen recessions. For a sovereign that prints its own currency, the main risk of overspending is inflation rather than the size of the debt itself.

## Risks

Three risks matter most. Credit (default) risk: governments have defaulted many times, including Spain in the 16th and 17th centuries, the Confederate States after the American Civil War, and revolutionary Russia in 1917. Debt issued in a country's own fiat currency is sometimes called risk-free for that reason, but subnational governments and eurozone members cannot print money and so face real default risk. Inflation risk: if a central bank finances the government by buying its bonds (debt monetisation), it can generate price inflation, with Weimar Germany in the 1920s as the extreme case. Exchange rate risk: a government that borrows in foreign currency cannot erode that debt through inflation, and bears the risk of currency movements the foreign lender faces.

## A short history

Public borrowing has existed for millennia. The founding of the Bank of England in 1694 transformed public finance: it ended defaults like Charles II's 1672 suspension of payments, and from then on the British government never failed to repay its creditors, a credibility other states later emulated. At the close of the Napoleonic Wars in 1815, British government debt peaked above 200% of GDP and was paid down over decades through primary budget surpluses, where revenue exceeded non-interest spending.
