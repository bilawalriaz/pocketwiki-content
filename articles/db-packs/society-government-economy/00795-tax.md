# Tax

A tax is a compulsory payment levied by a government on individuals or entities, used to fund public services and to steer economic behaviour. By transferring resources from households and firms to the state, taxation shapes growth, redistribution, and the cost of everyday goods. Across history, almost every society has raised revenue this way. The instruments and the rates have changed enormously, but the core mechanism, taking a share of private output for collective ends, has not.

The earliest recorded systems appeared in Ancient Egypt around 3000–2800 BC, where the state drew on corvée (forced labour by peasants unable to pay in cash or kind) and tithes (one-tenth of agricultural produce). The Roman Republic levied property taxes of 1–3% but collected them through private contractors known as *publicani*, who advanced revenue to the state and kept any surplus. Augustus later replaced this with direct provincial levies: a 1% tax on wealth and a flat per-adult charge. Britain introduced the modern income tax in 1798 to finance the Napoleonic Wars, and excise duties (taxes on specific goods such as beer, tobacco, and paper) first appeared in England in 1643. By the seventeenth century, taxation absorbed 15–20% of final goods production in France, the Netherlands, and Scandinavia. The U.S. Internal Revenue Code alone now runs to 3.8 million words.

Modern systems classify taxes in two main ways. First, by base: income, corporate, capital gains, social-security, payroll, property, inheritance, wealth, goods and services (VAT, sales, excise), tariffs, and user fees. Second, by design. A tax is *ad valorem* when set as a percentage of value and *per unit* when set as a fixed amount. It is *direct* if the taxpayer legally bears it (income tax) and *indirect* if it is shifted through prices (VAT). A *progressive* tax takes a rising share of income as income rises; a *regressive* tax takes a falling share; a *proportional* tax takes a constant share; a *lump-sum* tax is fixed regardless of ability to pay. *Tax incidence*, the question of who really pays, depends on relative elasticities: the side of the market that is less able to adjust behaviour bears more of the burden.

Taxes produce both benefits and costs. *Pigovian* taxes, named after Arthur Pigou, who formalised the idea in *The Economics of Welfare* (1920), correct negative externalities such as pollution by making private cost match social cost. Progressive taxes can reduce inequality. Most taxes also create *deadweight loss*, meaning transactions that would have benefited both parties are deterred, and impose compliance and administration costs. Levies on inelastic bases, such as land value, minimise this distortion. The *Laffer curve* captures the idea that beyond a revenue-maximising rate, estimated at roughly 70%, higher rates shrink the tax base faster than they raise the rate, so total revenue falls.

Adam Smith offered four enduring criteria for a well-designed tax: proportionality to ability to pay, certainty of assessment, convenience of payment, and low administrative cost. *Chartalist* theory goes further, arguing that a state which issues its own currency need not tax to fund itself at all; instead, it taxes to create demand for the currency and to express policy priorities. A 2019 study found that tax cuts for low-income groups raise employment more than equivalent cuts for the wealthy. Public acceptance depends heavily on whether citizens perceive the system as fair and see concrete benefits in return.

Internationally, tax-to-GDP ratios cluster around 30–40% in advanced economies and are much lower in many developing countries, where weak institutions and large informal economies limit collection. Resource-rich states often raise revenue more easily; trade taxes are giving way to domestic consumption taxes such as VAT; the World Bank's *Doing Business* survey tracks compliance burdens, with Gulf states typically scoring best. Wars have repeatedly driven innovation: Britain began income tax as a wartime measure, and the United States adopted a permanent version during the Civil War. Cross-border issues such as tax havens, base erosion, and profit shifting now drive efforts at global coordination.

Ideological perspectives diverge sharply. *Geoists* favour a single land-value tax on the grounds that land is a common inheritance and that taxing its unimproved value imposes no deadweight loss. *Libertarians* regard most taxation as coercive and prefer a minimal state. *Socialists* treat taxation as a transitional tool expected to wither once private property is abolished. Democratic theorists argue that citizens consent to taxation through elected representation, and some reformers propose letting taxpayers allocate a share of what they owe directly to chosen public services.

OECD countries collect on average 34.3% of GDP in tax, ranging from 0.8% in Kuwait to 45.9% in Denmark.
