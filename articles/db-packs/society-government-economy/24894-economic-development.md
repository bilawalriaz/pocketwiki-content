# Economic development

Economic development is the deliberate policy effort to raise well-being and quality of life across a nation, region, or community. Amartya Sen treats economic growth, the rise in a country's market output, as "one aspect" of development rather than its whole. The discipline formally emerged after World War II when US President Harry Truman, in his 1949 inaugural address, framed global poverty as a problem the West could solve through technical and industrial aid. The field crystallised between Keynesian support for state intervention and neoclassical skepticism of it, shaped by the rise of high-growth planned economies in Singapore, South Korea, and Hong Kong alongside state-led projects in Argentina, Chile, Sudan, and Uganda.

The concept evolved as economists and sociologists contested its scope. Greenwood and Holt argue that per capita income alone misses the target: a "broadly based and sustainable increase in the overall standard of living" must be the test. The United Nations Development Programme in 1997 redefined development as expanding people's choices, built on four pillars: empowerment, equity, productivity, and sustainability. Karl Seidman captures the practitioner view as creating and using physical, human, financial, and social assets to generate widely shared well-being. This deliberately broader framing pushes policy beyond industrial output toward health, education, literacy, and life expectancy.

Four overlapping phases of development theory have followed. Modernization theory from the 1940s through 1960s put the state at the center of industrialization in poorer countries. A brief 1970s turn to basic-needs policies prioritized human capital, the stock of skills and education in a workforce, and redistribution. The 1980s brought neoliberalism, free trade, and the dismantling of import-substitution policies. From the late 1960s, Robert McNamara's thirteen years leading the World Bank redirected international development toward targeted poverty reduction and reframed poverty as a condition of people rather than of countries.

Critics argue that the current free-trade orthodoxy contradicts historical success. Ha-Joon Chang shows that every now-developed country, from fourteenth-century Europe through late-twentieth-century East Asia, used tariff barriers, export subsidies, directed credit, investment planning, and public-private cooperation to nurture infant industries, tools that richer countries now discourage in others. Alice Amsden, analyzing World Bank data, finds that from 1950 to 1980, developing economies grew at over 5% annually, outpacing developed economies at 4%. Chang reports that developing-country per capita income growth fell from 3.0% under state-led industrialization to 1.7% after the neoliberal turn, with the Middle East's GDP growth dropping from 7.5% to 2.9% and per capita GDP from 4.8% to 0.3% between 1964–1974 and 1985–1995. Robert Pollin estimates that neoliberal policies cost developing countries roughly $480 billion in annual GDP between 1995 and 1999. Joseph Stiglitz, Noam Chomsky, Walden Bello, Jason Hickel, and David Harvey add that International Monetary Fund and World Trade Organization conditions strip infant industries of protection, while Martha Finnemore describes the World Bank under McNamara as having "sold" poverty reduction to states through persuasion and coercion.

Development policy covers three overlapping domains: macroeconomic management (price stability, employment, monetary and fiscal policy, trade and tax rules); infrastructure and services (highways, housing, crime prevention, K–12 education); and direct job creation through business finance, small-business support, workforce programs, and real estate development. Practitioners work in public agencies and public-private partnerships, increasingly organizing around industry clusters, geographic concentrations of interconnected firms, whose success depends on clear property rights and low transaction costs.

States often lack the administrative capacity to carry these policies out. Pritchett, Woolcock, and Andrews warn against "premature load bearing," adopting ambitious programs beyond state capability, and "isomorphic mimicry," copying organizational forms that look successful elsewhere while hiding institutional dysfunction and leaving countries in "capability traps." Foreign aid can deepen the problem: governments funded largely by donors, like those funded largely by oil, face weaker pressure to build effective public institutions and remain accountable to citizens.

Indicators track progress across these dimensions. The World Bank's World Development Indicators compile GDP per capita, median income, life expectancy, literacy, and infrastructure access. The UNDP's Gender-related Development Index and Gender Empowerment Measure, introduced in 1995, capture disparities in health, knowledge, living standards, and women's economic and political power. Median income has been proposed as a more representative measure than average income because it reflects the typical voter's circumstances. European economists also use rail-network indices such as the BRTI to gauge a country's developmental stage.
