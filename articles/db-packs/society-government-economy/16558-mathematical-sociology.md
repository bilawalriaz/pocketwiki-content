# Mathematical sociology

Mathematical sociology expresses sociological theory in formal mathematics: stating assumptions about a social phenomenon, encoding them as equations or graphs, deducing implications, and comparing the deductions with survey or network data. Its best-known output is social network analysis, the study of relations among people and groups.

## Origins in the 1940s and 1950s

The field began with Nicolas Rashevsky in the early 1940s and Anatol Rapoport in the late 1940s, who used probabilistic and relational methods to study large social networks in which nodes are people and links are acquaintances. A central early result connected a local property, the tendency for two of a person's contacts to know each other (triadic closure), to a global property, the overall connectivity of the network.

Rapoport and the mathematician Frank Harary extended this work to *signed graphs*, where links carry a sign: positive for acquaintanceship, negative for animosity. A signed graph is *balanced* when the product of the signs along every cycle is positive. Harary's structural balance theorem states that a balanced network splits into two internally positive groups (a *clique* is a set whose members are all linked to each other), with only negative ties between the groups, expressing the principle "my friend's enemy is my enemy." A small network can also balance with one group empty. A complementary model assigns ties a relative strength, treating acquaintanceship as weak and friendship as strong, with a corresponding *strong triadic closure* rule: a strong tie from A to B and from B to C forces a tie, weak or strong, from A to C.

Rapoport's 1961 study of a large friendship sociogram, co-authored with Horvath, became a foundational reference. Mark Granovetter drew on it in 1973 to argue that "weak ties" carry their own strength, for example by bridging otherwise disconnected groups. In 1952 Herbert A. Simon formalised George Homans's published theory of social groups as a system of differential equations, producing theorems about the dynamics and equilibrium states of groups. Stanley Milgram's small-world experiments in the late 1960s grew out of this network tradition. These structural and process models emerged alongside information theory, game theory, and cybernetics during the same postwar decade.

## From theory to data

A recurring question was how to connect such equations to survey data, where outcomes are proportions of people holding a behaviour or belief. The standard answer, developed by James S. Coleman and others, is to derive the equations from assumptions about the probability that an individual changes state in a short interval, a procedure borrowed from the mathematics of stochastic processes, which model systems that evolve through random events. Coleman's 1964 *Introduction to Mathematical Sociology* showed how this could be turned into a testable model.

Coleman also imported general equilibrium theory from economics, arguing that social theory should begin with purposive action approximated by rational choice. His 1990 *Foundations of Social Theory* extended rational choice to include interpersonal sentiments as modifiers of outcomes while preserving self-interested behaviour as a special case. The framework has critics but persists as a template for moving from individual action to aggregate outcomes.

## Key research programs

Harrison C. White and collaborators developed formal structural analysis. Core tools include the *blockmodel*, a map of a network's major subgroups, and *structural equivalence*, the idea that actors with identical ties to others can be treated as equivalent. His 1970 *Chains of Opportunity* applied a *vacancy chain* model, which traces how openings move through organisations, to organisational mobility. White's later work extended structural analysis to markets and to a general theory of identity and control.

Joseph Berger and Morris Zelditch Jr built Expectation States Theory, which links external status to differential influence in small groups; after the late 1970s it used graph theory to represent social information processing. Thomas J. Fararo advanced formal theoretical sociology and computational sociology through work on macrostructural theory and stratification images. Linton C. Freeman founded *Social Networks* in 1978 and clarified the concept of *centrality*, a measure of how important a node is within a network.

Kenneth C. Land combined statistics, mathematics, and sociology in demographic and criminological modelling. David R. Heise's Affect Control Theory, from 1979 onward, uses a cybernetic mechanism: actors compare immediate feelings with fundamental sentiments and adjust behaviour to restore correspondence. Guillermina Jasso's distributive justice theory, developed since 1980, anchors a justice evaluation function in the natural logarithm of the ratio of actual to just reward and derives testable implications about perceived fairness.

## Present scope

Mathematical sociology remains a small subfield but has spawned larger ones. Social network analysis is among the fastest-growing areas of contemporary sociology. Computational sociology extends the toolkit with computer simulations, artificial intelligence, and advanced statistical methods applied to internet-scale data. Persistent substantive problems across these programs include social diffusion, social influence, status, segregation, cooperation, collective action, and power; persistent formal tools include differential equations, stochastic processes, game theory, and agent-based simulation, in which many autonomous "agents" interact according to simple rules.

The ASA Section on Mathematical Sociology has awarded the James S. Coleman Distinguished Career Achievement Award biennially since 2002, recognising figures such as Harrison White, Thomas Fararo, Linton Freeman, and Kathleen M. Carley in 2024.
