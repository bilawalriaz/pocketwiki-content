# Social democracy

Social democracy is a centre-left political ideology that aims to make capitalism more equal and humane through democratic reform rather than revolution. In modern practice it combines a capitalist market economy with a strong welfare state, government regulation of business, and redistributive taxation.

The defining commitments are three: parliamentary democracy, a partly state-regulated economy, and universal social support. The model rests on collective bargaining, trade unions, and tripartite negotiation between employers, workers, and government. It is often associated with Keynesian economics, the Nordic model, and universal public services such as health care, education, elderly care, and workers' compensation.

## Origins and evolution

Friedrich Engels coined the term "social democracy" in an 1844 article on English politics. By 1868–1869 the Social Democratic Workers' Party of Germany had become the first party to officially adopt Marxian socialism as its theoretical basis. In the 19th century "social democrat" was a broad label covering almost all non-anarchist socialists, so the German party could be described as "organised Marxism."

The decisive break came with the Russian Revolution of October 1917. Those who supported the Bolsheviks renamed themselves Communists; those who opposed revolution but kept the reformist label kept the name Social Democrat. After the German Revolution of 1919, social democracy settled into what scholars call "moderate parliamentary socialism." By the 1920s and 1930s social democracy was the dominant tendency in the broader socialist movement worldwide, with communism representing the revolutionary wing.

The pivotal theorist was Eduard Bernstein, who rejected orthodox Marxism's prediction of revolutionary collapse and instead argued for evolutionary socialism achieved through gradual legislative reform, cooperation across classes, and ethical rather than materialist foundations. His revisionism was controversial when published, but became mainstream after the First World War.

After 1945, social democratic parties across Western Europe built the post-war consensus. Bismarck had already pioneered social welfare programmes in Germany between the 1880s and 1890s, partly to weaken socialist electoral appeal, and this early welfare-state model spread to Britain, France, and eventually the United States through the New Deal. The Keynesian mixed economy delivered unusually high growth and full employment through the 1950s and 1960s.

## The Third Way turn

The 1970s energy crisis, stagflation, and the collapse of Bretton Woods discredited Keynesian interventionism. Neoliberal policies of privatisation, deregulation, and free trade replaced the post-war settlement. By the 1990s social democrats had accepted a predominantly private-property economy regulated in the public interest, and the welfare state was redefined as a complement to capitalism rather than a step toward replacing it.

The Third Way, associated with Tony Blair's New Labour and Bill Clinton's New Democrats, fused social democratic welfare commitments with economic liberalism. Critics from both left and right charge that this amounted to a neoliberal project in social democratic clothing, abandoning any commitment to transcending capitalism. Many Third Way social democratic parties adopted austerity after the 2008 financial crisis rather than the traditional Keynesian response of deficit spending.

## Social democracy versus democratic socialism

The terms overlap in policy but differ in goal. Social democracy works within a capitalist framework, regulating and redistributing through existing institutions, while democratic socialism seeks a post-capitalist economy, typically through gradual or evolutionary means but with systemic transformation as the end. Some European social democratic parties, such as the German SPD and the Swedish Social Democrats, still describe their goal as developing democratic socialism, while in the United States the Democratic Socialists of America grew from about 24,000 members in 2017 to over 120,000 by July 2026, surpassing the historical peak of the Socialist Party of America. In colloquial use, especially in the United States, the labels are often used interchangeably, which obscures the distinction.

## Legacy and outcomes

Social corporatism, meaning negotiated agreements between capital, labour, and government, developed in Norway and Sweden during the 1930s and was consolidated in the 1960s and 1970s, becoming a defining feature of the Nordic model. The Nordic countries consistently rank highly on GDP per capita, equality, public health, life expectancy, and democratic indices, outcomes frequently attributed to social democratic institutions.

Critics from the Marxist left argue that welfare-state reform cannot resolve capitalism's structural contradictions of cyclical crisis, exploitation, and alienation, and that by stabilising the system social democracy removes the pressure for deeper change. The dominant practical constraint, as economists John Roemer and Pranab Bardhan note, is that sustained redistribution requires a strong labour movement, and that Scandinavian social democracy has itself eroded as union density has fallen.
