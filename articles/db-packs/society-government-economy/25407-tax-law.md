# Tax law

Tax law is the area of legal study in which governments use a body of rules and procedures to assess and collect taxes. It sits within public law and covers how existing tax rules apply to individuals, businesses, and other entities. The taxes it addresses include income, estate, business, payroll, property, gift, and import or export duties. Rates and the merits of any given tax are set through the political process rather than by tax law itself, which is concerned with applying rules that legislatures have already chosen.

In the United States, federal tax law rests on three layers of authority. The Internal Revenue Code is the primary statutory basis enacted by Congress. The Code of Federal Regulations supplies the Treasury Department's regulatory interpretation, which carries the weight of law when the interpretation is reasonable. Tax treaties and case law from the U.S. Tax Court and other federal courts fill in the remainder.

## Issues in developed economies

Mature tax systems share a recurring set of problems. Revenue sometimes falls short of what governments need to spend. Complexity tends to benefit higher-income earners, who can afford advice that lowers their effective burden. Evasion and avoidance both shrink revenue, and poorly designed taxes can lower productivity by discouraging business growth. Corporate rules in particular can hold back smaller firms. The treatment of capital gains (profit from selling assets such as stocks) versus wages is a frequent flashpoint, since investors often pay proportionally less on investment income than workers do on earnings. Ecotax, a levy that uses pricing to encourage environmentally friendly activity, remains contested in the European Union over whether it achieves its stated goals.

## Issues in developing economies

Lower-income countries face a different mix of constraints. Most workers earn a living in small, often unregulated enterprises, which makes income hard to standardise for tax purposes. Tax administrations may lack the institutions and trained staff needed to run the system efficiently. Without reliable data on incomes, transactions, and businesses, regulations are hard to enforce or update. Tax systems in these settings tend to place a heavier burden on wealthier taxpayers.

## A competing proposal

Some scholars argue that consumer law offers a more efficient route to large-scale redistribution than tax law, because it can pursue similar goals without fresh legislation and can sidestep some of the complexities that make tax reform difficult.
