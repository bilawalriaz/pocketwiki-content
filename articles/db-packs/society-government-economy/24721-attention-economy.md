# Attention economy

Human attention is finite. Information is not. That imbalance drives the attention economy, a system in which firms treat the time and focus of users as a scarce resource to be captured, traded, and sold.

The economist and psychologist Herbert A. Simon framed the core insight in 1971: in an information-rich world, the wealth of information creates a poverty of attention, and society needs systems that allocate attention efficiently rather than pour out more information. Matthew Crawford put it plainly: "Attention is a resource — a person has only so much of it." Thomas H. Davenport and John C. Beck defined it as focused mental engagement on a particular item of information, followed by a decision to act. Erik Brynjolfsson and colleagues showed in 2023 that the economic value of attention can be quantified and compared to monetary expenditure.

The driving mechanism is incentive. When a firm's revenue comes from advertising, profit rises with the human attention it captures. Once the cost of transmitting an ad falls near zero, the binding constraint shifts from delivery to grabbing the viewer's mind. The result is an arms race for engagement: notifications, autoplay, infinite feeds, and recommendation algorithms that exploit the brain's dopamine reward system to keep users scrolling and returning. In the 2010s, social media and online advertising fused into the dominant business model of the internet.

Attention traded in this market is a private good for the firm, but its consequences fall on everyone, which is a negative externality. Four overlapping harms follow. First, social media addiction: platforms designed to maximise engagement are linked to depression, anxiety, and low self-esteem. Second, disinformation amplification: a 2024 study by Carlos A. Diaz Ruiz in the *Journal of Marketing Management* showed that pairing the attention economy with programmatic online advertising creates financial incentives to circulate fake news. Third, surveillance capitalism: companies harvest personal data and behavioural patterns to personalise content and sell profiles to third parties, often without informed consent. Fourth, marginalised communities lose visibility when engagement-maximising algorithms favour dominant cultural identities; Black TikTok creators have reported sharp engagement drops after posting about Black Lives Matter, a pattern consistent with shadow-banning.

The same logic explains older problems. Thomas H. Davenport and Michael Goldhaber popularised the term in the mid-1990s, and Georg Franck argued in 1999 that for advertising-based media, "income in attention ranks above financial success." Email spam is the canonical example: sending a million messages costs almost nothing to the sender but demands attention from every recipient, so conversion rates as low as one in 100,000 make spam profitable. Web spam follows the same arithmetic through search-engine rankings, where appearing high in results for a profitable query is valuable because the ranking directs collective attention. Link farms, reciprocal linking, and Google bombs are attempts to manipulate that ranking.

Once attention is treated as a resource, the question is who owns it. Ronald Coase's 1960 paper *The Problem of Social Cost* argued that externalities, including pollution, can in principle be managed by defining property rights and letting markets trade them. Applied to information, this logic yields proposals such as "Sender Bond" (senders post a bond forfeited if recipients report their email as spam), "interrupt rights" (a small fee charged for the right to demand someone's attention, with the price varying by recipient and context), and "attention bonds" (a warranty held in escrow that the message will not waste the recipient's time). A different model, used by Google's AdSense and Yahoo's Publisher Network, treats the consumer's attention as the property of the platform or publisher rather than of the user, a structure that shapes the ad-supported web.

The concept also illuminates why digital content is often nearly free. By 2008, the digital-culture writer Kevin Kelly observed that when reproduction costs approach zero, suppliers compete by adding valuable intangibles: immediacy, personalisation, interpretation, authenticity, accessibility, embodiment (live music, printed books), patronage, and findability. When millions of items compete for notice, being noticed is itself the scarce resource.

Attention economics has begun to affect science itself. In 2022, Rice University professor Adrian Lenardic and two co-authors warned that the public attention a study attracts now feeds into its perceived value, with scientists listing media-exposure counts on résumés and PhD theses reporting popular-press coverage. Results are sometimes publicised without adequate peer input or reproducibility checks, a sign that the field's incentives have drifted toward capturing attention rather than certifying truth.
