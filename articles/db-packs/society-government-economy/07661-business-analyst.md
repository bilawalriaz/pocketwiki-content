# Business analyst

A business analyst (BA) is a person who processes, interprets, and documents business processes, products, services, and software through analysis of data. The role sits at the overlap of IT and business, making the analyst a bridge between technical staff and decision-makers. Its purpose is to increase efficiency by turning data into recommendations that stakeholders (the people affected by or owning a decision) can act on.

Business analysis has been defined as a disciplined approach for introducing organisational change that identifies the solution maximising the value delivered to stakeholders. Day-to-day work includes budgeting and forecasting, planning and monitoring, variance analysis (measuring the gap between planned and actual figures), pricing, reporting, and defining business requirements. Analysts support both tactical improvements to existing products, processes, and applications and longer-term strategic planning.

## Four areas of focus

Business analysis applies at four levels, each with a different scope:

- **Operations focus**: using large datasets to see how day-to-day operations affect value, then cutting costs, upgrading equipment, raising employee efficiency, or scaling popular products.
- **Project focus**: ensuring historically overlooked areas are examined, integrating strategic planning with portfolio planning for information systems, and modelling the current state ("as-is") and the target state ("to-be") for staff at every level.
- **Enterprise focus**: building current and future business architecture, analysing opportunities and feasibility, proposing new projects, validating forecasts, and comparing planned against actual results.
- **Competitive focus**: studying competitors, consumer behaviour, and brand substitutability (how easily a customer can switch to an alternative product) to develop strategy across the business, including marketing.

A single analyst may operate in one area, but the underlying skill set transfers between them.

## Core skills

The role combines hard skills (teachable, technical) and soft skills (interpersonal). The soft side covers oral and written communication, facilitation, analytical thinking, problem solving, attention to detail, and stakeholder analysis. The hard side covers requirements engineering (eliciting, documenting, and managing what a system must do), cost–benefit analysis, process modelling, and an understanding of networks, databases, and other technology.

Four functional competencies tie these together:

- **Mediation**: acting as liaison between business and IT professionals, who otherwise speak different professional languages.
- **Requirements elicitation**: gathering and analysing the needs of both computer-based systems and the business itself, so quality problems are caught early and waste is minimised.
- **Solution design and business modelling**: using past performance and forecasting to design functions, processes, and the future business.
- **Information-system strategy evaluation**: continually monitoring strategic plans against competitors and industry trends.

The same analytical base supports many job titles, including business systems analyst, systems analyst, requirements engineer, process analyst, product analyst, product manager, product owner, enterprise analyst, business architect, management consultant, business intelligence analyst, data scientist, and CRM analyst. Analysts also work alongside project management, product management, software development, quality assurance, and interaction design.

## Qualifications

Common entry paths are a bachelor's degree in information technology, business administration, or economics, followed, for deeper specialisation, by a master's in business analytics, business informatics, business intelligence and analytics, data science, management information systems, or information technology. IT professionals often move laterally into the role because their technical skills map directly onto BA work.

## Challenges

Delivering value depends on access to large volumes of data, and that access creates four recurring risks. Data privacy becomes harder as social media and big data expand, requiring that only necessary data reach appropriate employees. Analytical resources carry high up-front costs, which must be managed to protect profit. Team dynamics matter because conclusions are strongest when group work is balanced. And external communication is difficult because BA terminology is often opaque to other departments, so findings must be translated into language non-specialists can act on.
