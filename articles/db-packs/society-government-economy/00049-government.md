# Government

A government is the system that leads a state and directs the only entity whose use of force is socially acceptable. Governments shape how societies organize power, create laws, and manage public life. Historically they have taken forms dominated by one person (autocracy), a small ruling class (oligarchy), or the masses (democracy).

## Origins and development

The origins of government are unclear. About 5,000 years ago the first small city-states appeared; by the 3rd–2nd millennia BC, some grew into larger governed areas, including Sumer, ancient Egypt, the Indus Valley civilization, and the Yellow River civilization. One explanation ties emergence to the Neolithic Revolution: farming produced food surpluses, enabling specialization, including governance itself, and supporting denser populations that required administration. A competing explanation points to centralized management of infrastructure such as water systems, though archaeology also documents successful egalitarian, decentralized complex societies.

From the late 17th century, republican forms spread, boosted by the English Civil War, the Glorious Revolution, the American Revolution, and the French Revolution. In 1922 the Soviet Union became the first large country with a Communist government. Since the fall of the Berlin Wall in 1989, liberal democracy has continued to spread. The 19th and 20th centuries saw national governments expand through corporate regulation and welfare state development. As of 2021, 97 of 167 nations were democracies, but roughly a quarter of the world's population lived under democratically backsliding governments.

## Classification

The word "government" derives from the Greek *kubernáo* (to steer with a rudder), used metaphorically in classical antiquity, including Plato's Ship of State. While all organizations have governance, "government" usually refers to the roughly 200 independent national governments and their subsidiary bodies. In British English it can also mean a ministry or administration—the policies and officials of a particular executive.

Plato's *The Republic* (375 BC) divided governments into five types—aristocracy, timocracy, oligarchy, democracy, and tyranny—which he saw as progressively degenerating. Aristotle's *Politics* classified governments by who rules: one person (autocracy, including monarchy), a select group (aristocracy), or the people (democracy, including republics). Thomas Hobbes identified three forms of Commonwealth: monarchy, democracy, and aristocracy. According to political scientist Juan José Linz, three main modern systems exist: democracies, totalitarian regimes, and authoritarian regimes with hybrids between them; monarchies are sometimes treated as standalone or hybrid. Federalism divides sovereignty constitutionally between a central authority and constituent units such as states or provinces.

Classification is contested because self-identification is not objective. The United States is a federal constitutional republic, but the Soviet Union was a federal socialist republic *de jure* yet a centralized autocratic one-party state under Stalin. Voltaire quipped that the Holy Roman Empire was "neither Holy, nor Roman, nor an Empire." Parties named after ideologies blur the line between movements and forms of government, and labels like "conservatism" mean different things across countries. Even the most liberal democracies limit rival political activity, while the most tyrannical dictatorships must organize broad support, complicating narrow categorization. Some argue the United States functions as a plutocracy shaped by wealthy donors.

## Institutions and administration

Governments are typically organized into three branches—legislature, executive, and judiciary (the *trias politica* model)—though parliamentary and semi-presidential systems often have intersecting branches, and some add bodies such as electoral commissions. Party systems include majority, minority, coalition, and single-party governments. The public sector comprises state-owned enterprises, public services, and civil servants. In developed countries, public services include courts, education, emergency services, health care, military, and policing. Public policy spans education, health care, employment, finance, economics, and transportation, with government spending on social protection, health care, education, economic affairs, security, defense, and recreation.

## Key terms

- **Autocracy**: Supreme power concentrated in one person, free of external legal restraints or popular control.
- **Democracy**: Rule by the people, directly or through elected representatives.
- **Federalism**: Sovereignty divided constitutionally between a central authority and constituent units.
- **Republic**: A state treated as a "public matter" (*res publica*), with offices elected or appointed, not inherited.
- **Trias politica**: Separation of government into legislative, executive, and judicial branches.
