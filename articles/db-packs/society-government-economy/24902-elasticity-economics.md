# Elasticity (economics)

Elasticity measures how much one economic variable responds to a change in another. If the price elasticity of demand for a good is −2, a 10% price increase cuts quantity demanded by 20%. The concept, first cited informally by Alfred Marshall in *Principles of Economics* (1890), is central to neoclassical theory and to questions about taxation, pricing, and welfare.

## The formula

The elasticity of variable *x* with respect to variable *y* is the ratio of the percentage change in *x* to the percentage change in *y*:

ε_{x,y} = (% change in x) / (% change in y) = (∂x/∂y) · (y/x)

For continuous P-elasticity of Q:

ε = (∂Q/Q) / (∂P/P)

For discrete changes:

ε = [(Q₂−Q₁)/Q₁] / [(P₂−P₁)/P₁]

Because both numerator and denominator are percentages, elasticity is unitless: a number, not a quantity.

## Elastic, unit elastic, inelastic

The absolute value of ε determines the label:

| |ε| > 1 | elastic | Q changes more than P |
| |ε| = 1 | unit elastic | Q changes like P |
| |ε| < 1 | inelastic | Q changes less than P |

If price rises 1% and supply elasticity is 0.5, quantity rises 0.5%; at elasticity 2, quantity rises 2%. Two extremes: perfectly elastic (ε → ∞), where any tiny price change provokes an unlimited quantity response, and perfectly inelastic (ε = 0), where quantity never moves.

The same good can sit at different elasticities at different prices. Suppliers often find supply elastic at low prices but inelastic at high prices, so a rise from a low base brings a more-than-proportionate quantity jump, while a rise from a high base brings less.

## Revenue maximisation

Seller revenue equals P × Q. Differentiating and setting the derivative to zero gives (P/Q)(dQ/dP) = −1, so revenue peaks where |ε| = 1. A firm should cut price when demand is elastic (|ε| > 1), since the quantity gain outweighs the price loss, and raise price when demand is inelastic (|ε| < 1), since the quantity loss is small. Most demand curves, including the standard linear demand, hit this point somewhere; isoelastic curves such as Q = 3P^(−0.5) have fixed elasticity (−0.5) and revenue rises without bound as P grows.

## Major types

**Price elasticity of demand** measures how quantity demanded responds to the good's own price, holding other factors constant. A 40% price rise producing only a 10% quantity fall means the good is inelastic.

**Price elasticity of supply** mirrors demand for the supply side, capturing horizontal movement along the supply curve against vertical price movement.

**Income elasticity of demand** tracks how quantity responds to consumer income, separating normal goods (positive), luxury goods (high positive), necessity goods (low positive), and inferior goods (negative).

**Cross-price elasticity of demand** measures how the quantity of one good responds to the price of another. A positive value signals substitutes (US domestic tuna and imported tuna register 0.45); a negative value signals complements (US domestic tuna and bread register −0.33).

**Elasticity of scale** (output elasticity) tracks the percentage change in output against a collective percentage change in all inputs. Elasticity equal to 1 marks constant returns to scale; greater than 1 marks increasing returns; less than 1 marks decreasing returns.

**Elasticity of substitution** and **elasticity of intertemporal substitution** apply the same logic to factor inputs and to choices across time.

## Determinants

Demand elasticity rises with the number and closeness of available substitutes, falls for necessities (petrol, salt) and goods that take a small share of income, and shifts with the time horizon: a price rise feels inelastic in the short run when buyers cannot yet adjust, but elastic in the long run as habits, substitutes, and budgets catch up.

Supply elasticity depends on scarcity of inputs, spare capacity, the time horizon (longer horizons let firms hire labour, raise capital, and build capacity, making long-run supply more elastic than short-run), and the number of competitors producing similar goods.

## Applications

Enterprises use elasticity to set prices: cut price when demand is elastic, raise price and trim output when it is inelastic, and avoid crossing into the elastic region, where further price changes only shrink revenue. Governments use it to gauge tax incidence, since a tax on an inelastic good raises revenue without much curbing demand, while a tax on an elastic good mainly suppresses the market. Elasticity also underlies analysis of international trade terms, the burden of taxation, advertising's pull on demand, and the welfare effects of price ceilings and floors. In empirical work, elasticity appears as the coefficient on a logged variable in a linear regression.
