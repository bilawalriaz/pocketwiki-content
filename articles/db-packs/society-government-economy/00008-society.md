# Society

A **society** is a group of individuals bound by persistent social interaction, sharing a spatial or social territory under common political authority and dominant cultural expectations. Societies are organized through patterns of relationships—*social relations*—linking members who share a distinctive culture and institutions. Human social structures are unusually cooperative and feature specialization of labor through social roles, allowing benefits individuals cannot achieve alone; in turn, society shapes the humans who create it.

## Etymology

"Society" dates to at least 1513, from 12th-century French *societe* ("company"), from Latin *societas* ("fellowship" or "alliance"), from *socius* ("comrade"). "Social" derives from Latin *socii* ("allies"), especially the Italian Socii states allied to Rome, who rebelled during the Social War of 91–87 BC.

## Biological foundations

Humans, bonobos, and chimpanzees are highly social, suggesting sociability is hardwired. Human groups differ from chimp and bonobo groups through male parental roles, language, labor specialization, and multi-generational "nests" (camps, towns, cities). E.O. Wilson classifies humans as **eusocial**—the highest level of animal sociability, shared with ants—though others disagree. Group living may have evolved through group selection in harsh environments.

## Sociological paradigms

Western sociology rests on three dominant paradigms. **Functionalism** treats individuals as cooperating like organs in a body to produce emergent collective behavior; Comte and Durkheim saw society as a distinct level of reality. **Conflict theory** holds that interaction is driven by conflict; Marx argued an economic base determines a superstructure of government, family, religion, and culture, with change driven by conflict between laborers and owners of the means of production. **Symbolic interactionism** studies how shared language creates symbols and meanings that shape individual behavior. Marx held that humans cannot survive except through cooperation, with relations of production independent of their will. Weber defined action as "social" when it accounts for others' behavior. Berger and Luckmann described society as a dialectic: humans create society, which molds humans.

## Non-Western views

These paradigms are criticized as Eurocentric. Syed Farid al-Attas cites Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406), who identified two social forms: nomadic life with high cohesion (*asabijja*) from kinship, customs, and defense; and sedentary life marked by secularization, lower cohesion, and luxury. José Rizal (1861–1896) argued Filipino "indolence" was caused by colonial exploitation, economic disorder, and anti-farming policies, not inherent character.

## Types of society

Sociologists classify societies by technology: pre-industrial, industrial, and postindustrial. Cultural evolution generally runs hunter-gatherer → agrarian village → town → city → nation-state, but is not unidirectional.

- **Hunter-gatherer**: wild-plant collection and hunting; constant movement limits communities to under 50 people; egalitarian bands with consensus decisions. Sahlins called them the "original affluent society" (adults work 3–5 hours daily), though critics cite high mortality and warfare.
- **Pastoral**: nomadic, based on domesticated herds; multiple communities can total thousands because open terrain enables political integration, producing food surplus, specialized labor, and high inequality.
- **Horticultural**: garden-plot cultivation with simple tools, emerging about 10,000 years ago; rotating plots allow permanent villages and surpluses supporting craftspeople, shamans, and traders.
- **Agrarian**: plow-based large-scale cultivation, Lenski's dividing line; extreme stratification between governing and peasant classes, urban and rural populations, and literate and illiterate groups. Caste systems in South Asia are associated with agrarianism.
- **Industrial**: emerged in the 18th-century Industrial Revolution; machine-powered mass production shifts labor from extracting raw materials to processing them, driving urbanization, capitalism, and high but mobile inequality.
- **Post-industrial / information / knowledge**: dominated by services. An *information society* makes creating and distributing information a significant activity; a *knowledge society* transforms information into resources for effective action.

## Characteristics

**Norms and roles**: norms are shared standards of acceptable behavior, informal or codified as law; roles tie duties to status. Goffman's dramaturgical lens treats roles as scripts.

**Gender and kinship**: gender roles divide norms, dress, rights, and power and have varied historically. Kinship organizes relationships by consanguinity (blood), affinity (marriage), and fictive ties (godparents, adoption). All societies have incest taboos; some impose preferential-cousin rules.

**Ethnicity**: identified by shared traditions, ancestry, and language, though no accepted definition exists; it is socially constructed like race.

**Government**: early political power clustered around fresh water, fertile soil, and temperate climates; denser farming populations increased governance development.

**Trade and economics**: long-distance trade may have given *Homo sapiens* advantages over Neanderthals, who lacked such networks. Early economies favored gift-giving over barter; earliest money included cattle and cowrie shells. As of 2018, the richest tenth held over seven-tenths of wealth in China, Europe, and the US.

**Conflict**: war's origins are debated. Evidence suggests warlike behavior became common only about 10,000 years ago. Phylogenetic analysis predicts 2% of deaths by homicide, matching band societies; societies with legal systems show roughly 0.01%.

## Open questions

Debates include whether humans are eusocial, the Eurocentrism of Western sociology, the validity of Sahlins's "original affluent society" thesis, the recency of organized war, and the absence of a generally accepted definition of ethnic group.
