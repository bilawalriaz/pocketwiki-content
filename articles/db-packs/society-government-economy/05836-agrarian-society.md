# Agrarian society

An agrarian society is a community whose economy is built on producing crops and maintaining farmland, so that cultivating the land is the primary source of wealth. Agrarianism has been the most common form of socio-economic organisation for most of recorded human history, appearing as far back as 10,000 years ago and still present in many regions today.

## Origins and the Neolithic Revolution

Agrarian society was preceded by hunter-gatherer and horticultural societies (small-scale garden cultivation without ploughed-field farming). The shift to farming is called the Neolithic Revolution and happened independently in several regions. Horticulture and field agriculture first developed between 10,000 and 8,000 years ago in the Fertile Crescent of the Middle East. By at least 6,800 BCE East Asia cultivated rice, and later Central and South America grew maize and squash; small-scale farming also arose in India (rice) and Southeast Asia (taro). A long transition is documented at Takarkori rockshelter in the Central Sahara, where between 7500 BCE and 3500 BCE hunter-gatherers farmed undomesticated cereals and managed Barbary sheep until the Pastoral Neolithic began. Full dependence on domestic crops and animals, with wild food making up a nutritionally insignificant share of the diet, did not arrive until the Bronze Age. Why agriculture began is debated, with climate change and the accumulation of food surplus for competitive gift-giving as leading candidates.

The old view treated farming as unambiguous progress: bigger populations, towns, leisure, writing and civilisation. Archaeology now shows that health deteriorated in early farming populations, returning to pre-agricultural levels only in modern times, mainly through lower dietary quality under intensive cereal farming, compounded by infection in crowded settlements. Many groups who knew about agriculture still chose to keep hunting and gathering.

## Why farming changed everything

Agriculture supports far higher population density than foraging and produces a surplus that can be stored or sold. That surplus lets a minority of non-farmers practise specialised crafts, writing, religion, administration and warfare, producing the layered social structures, cities, inequality and standing armies characteristic of agrarian states. Settlements grew because improved land and sea transport could feed cities of a million or more, such as Rome, supplied with grain from Sicily, North Africa, Egypt and southern France.

Agrarian populations fluctuated sharply around a slowly rising trend, driven by famines, epidemics and political disruption. At peaks, densities often exceeded what current technology could productively employ, producing Malthusian pressure in which a larger population does not translate into a higher average standard of living.

## Social structure

Because land is the main source of wealth, hierarchy is built on land ownership rather than on labour, in patterns often labelled feudalism. Stratification is marked by three overlapping contrasts: rulers versus masses, a small urban minority versus a peasant majority, and a literate minority versus an illiterate majority. Landowning strata tend to fuse government, religion and the military to defend their holdings, while primary producers commonly live under slavery, serfdom or peonage. Rigid, lifelong caste systems, as in India, fit this model well, because settled agricultural routines demand discipline and a fixed sense of duty. The modern Western emphasis on personal liberty grew largely as a reaction against the steep stratification of agrarian life.

## Energy and limits

An agrarian society runs on plant biomass, depending on current solar energy captured by photosynthesis and only secondarily converted by animals and people. The energy available is limited by the low density of sunlight and the low efficiency of the technologies that capture it. Wind and water wheels can be added but do not change the ceiling. To produce more, a society must intensify work on existing land or expand into new land, and because the best land is already farmed, expansion usually means taking poorer land, displacing other communities, or colonising new ecological niches.

## Transition to industrial society

A society becomes industrial once less than half its workforce is directly engaged in farming. This shift began with the Commercial Revolution in Mediterranean city-states from 1000 to 1500 CE, especially Venice, Florence, Milan and Genoa; by about 1500 some had crossed the threshold into commercial societies that imported food and concentrated on trade and manufacture. Mechanised industry, driven by inanimate energy sources applied to production, followed. By about 1800 Britain's agricultural workforce had fallen to roughly one third, and by the mid-19th century all of Western Europe plus the United States had more than half their populations in non-farm work.

In the 20th century the tractor mechanised sowing, harvesting and threshing, while crop breeding, soil-nutrient management and weed control raised yields per unit of land. Today more people worldwide still work in agriculture than in any other sector, yet farming accounts for only about 4% of world GDP, and the developing world lags in yields because it has less access to the latest science, capital and technology.

## Agrarianism as a philosophy

Agrarianism is a social philosophy that treats agrarian life as morally superior to industrial life, idealising the farmer as self-sufficient and independent against the urban wage labourer seen as vulnerable and alienated. It links working the land with virtue and links cities, capitalism and technology with lost independence and corruption. It is close to, but not the same as, back-to-the-land movements: agrarianism is a normative stance about the good society, and it explicitly rejects industrial-scale farming, which keeps the form of agriculture but loses the rural, small-scale character it values.
