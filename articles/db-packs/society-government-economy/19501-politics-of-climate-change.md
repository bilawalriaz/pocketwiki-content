# Politics of climate change

Climate change emerged as a political issue in the 1970s and gained global prominence in the 1990s. Greenhouse gas emissions from burning fossil fuels, cement and steel production, and agricultural land use cause global warming, yet fossil fuels remain the backbone of the world economy, supplying about 80% of primary energy as of 2019. The politics of climate change is the arena where societies weigh the costs of climate action against entrenched economic interests, with resistance concentrated in carbon-intensive industries.

## Why the issue is hard

Greenhouse gases mix globally, so emissions from any country warm the planet for everyone, but the harms fall unevenly. Some regions and economies are far more vulnerable than others, producing persistent disputes over who should cut first, who benefits from fossil fuel use, and who faces the greatest risks. Early international talks made little progress because nations could not agree on these questions.

Three classes of response are now on the table. Mitigation cuts emissions and enhances carbon sinks, natural absorbers such as forests. Adaptation defends against climate impacts already underway. Solar geoengineering would reflect sunlight back to space to cool the planet. Twentieth-century debate focused almost entirely on mitigation; adaptation was once treated as defeatist. By the early 2000s adaptation was widely accepted as essential, and by 2019 geoengineering had entered mainstream discussion.

## International negotiations

Annual UN Climate Change Conferences, called COPs (Conferences of the Parties), have run since the early 1990s. Three stand out.

- 1997 Kyoto Protocol: binding emissions targets, but results disappointed and the United States never ratified.
- 2009 Copenhagen Summit: tried to go beyond Kyoto with stronger commitments, but largely failed.
- 2015 Paris Agreement: introduced Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs), country pledges resubmitted and strengthened every five years, and is widely regarded as successful, though its long-term effectiveness remains uncertain.

Three negotiating tools have been tried. Fixed emissions targets have rarely been met, and penalties have always been blocked by at least one or two nations. A carbon price is widely considered the most effective in theory, but nations have been reluctant because of carbon leakage, the practice of relocating polluting industries to jurisdictions without a carbon price. Despite this, the share of global emissions covered by a carbon price rose from 5% in 2005 to 15% by 2019 and is projected to exceed 40% once China's system takes full effect. Voluntary pledge-and-review was abandoned before Kyoto and revived through Paris's NDCs.

Climate finance matters because vulnerable developing nations need help adapting. Wealthy nations pledged $100 billion per year at the 2009 Copenhagen Summit, but as of December 2020 funding had fallen short and most delivered money went to mitigation rather than adaptation.

## National, regional, and sub-national action

Policies are set by national governments, sub-national jurisdictions such as US states, or regional bodies such as the European Union. Many of the strongest measures exceed international commitments, including Costa Rica reaching 99% renewable electricity in the 2010s. The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) taxes imports based on their carbon content to prevent leakage. Four policy instruments dominate: carbon pricing and financial incentives, prescriptive regulations such as renewable portfolio standards, direct government spending on clean technology, and information-based approaches encouraging voluntary action.

## Actors and movements

The fossil fuel industry remains the most powerful interest resisting strong action. Major oil companies and lobby groups spent $2 billion on US climate lobbying between 2000 and 2016, and starting in the 1970s ExxonMobil suppressed its own scientists' findings on the climate impact of fossil fuels.

Counter-movements have grown quickly. The fossil fuel divestment campaign, urging institutions to sell holdings of fossil fuel companies, spread from US campuses in 2011 to more than 1,593 institutions holding over $40.5 trillion by July 2023. Youth-led activism surged after Greta Thunberg began a school strike outside the Swedish parliament in August 2018; the September 2019 Global Week for Future drew roughly 4 million protesters across more than 150 countries, including 1.4 million in Germany. By 2019, OPEC's secretary general called these activists the greatest threat to the fossil fuel industry.

## Shifts in the 2020s

Several developments have made stronger action more feasible. Renewable energy supplied 29% of world electricity in 2020, and dozens of countries now generate more than half of their electricity from renewables. Outright climate denial became far less influential after 2019; the Cato Institute closed its climate program that year, and opposition shifted toward strategies of delay. The COVID-19 pandemic prompted calls for a green recovery linking economic stimulus to clean energy and efficiency, though only about 6% of G20 pandemic spending went to measures that also cut emissions. At COP26 in Glasgow in 2021, nearly 200 nations agreed to accelerate action, and in 2025 the EU and China struck a joint climate agreement amid tensions over the US withdrawal from Paris.

## Persistent obstacles

Fossil fuels still supply about 80% of world energy, and removing consumer subsidies has historically triggered riots. Doomism, the view that it is already too late to act, discourages action among some who are not outright deniers. Some environmental groups have blocked climate policies they judged insufficient, as when Australia's Greens voted against a 2009 carbon trading scheme. Military forces are responsible for 5.5% of global emissions, and wars such as the conflict in Ukraine divert resources from climate action. Adaptation can backfire when it ignores local trade-offs, as when Tanzanian coral reef protection pushed villagers into higher-emissions farming. A 2024 analysis identified 100 US representatives and 23 senators, all Republicans, as climate change deniers.
