# Derivative (finance)

A derivative is a contract between two parties whose value derives from an underlying asset or variable. Four elements define every derivative: an underlier (a commodity, stock, bond, currency, interest rate, or index), a future transaction, a price fixed today, and a future date by which the transaction occurs. The underlier itself need not change hands; derivatives separate ownership of the asset from exposure to its price.

Derivatives serve two opposing purposes. Hedging transfers risk to a counterparty: a wheat farmer fearing a price drop locks in a sale price with a miller fearing a shortage, and both reduce their uncertainty. Speculation accepts risk for profit, betting on price moves without owning the underlying. The same contract structure can serve either purpose.

The basic contract types are forwards, futures, options, swaps, and credit derivatives. Forwards and futures are "lock" products, obligating both parties to transact on a future date at today's price. Options are "option" products, giving the buyer the right but not the obligation to transact, in exchange for an upfront premium. Swaps exchange cash flows, such as a fixed interest rate for a floating one, calculated on a notional amount, a reference sum that usually is not exchanged. Credit default swaps (CDS) insure against a borrower's default; the buyer pays periodic fees, and the seller covers losses if the borrower fails. Warren Buffett called CDS "financial weapons of mass destruction" in his 2002 shareholder letter.

Forwards are private, customizable agreements between two parties. Futures are standardized contracts traded on exchanges, with a clearing house acting as intermediary. To reduce default risk, exchanges require both parties to post margin and settle daily gains and losses (mark-to-market). Most derivatives by volume trade over the counter (OTC) rather than on exchanges, which exposes parties to counterparty risk, the chance the other side cannot honor the contract.

Derivatives are one of three categories of financial instruments alongside equity (stocks) and debt (bonds). Aristotle described a sixth-century BCE olive contract by Thales, often cited as the earliest example though Aristotle classed it as a monopoly rather than a derivative. Rice futures have traded on the Dojima Rice Exchange since the eighteenth century. Modern options pricing rests on the Black–Scholes model, published in 1973.

The market is enormous but largely nominal. Notional amounts outstanding in OTC derivatives reached roughly $700 trillion in 2011 and $846 trillion by June 2025, though true market value (the cost to replace contracts) was about $21 trillion in both years, and credit exposure around $3 trillion. CDS notional fell from $55 trillion in 2008 to $25.5 trillion by early 2012.

Leverage makes derivatives powerful and dangerous. A small price move in the underlier can produce outsized gains or losses. Notable failures include Barings Bank's $1.3 billion loss in 1995 from Nick Leeson's unauthorized futures trades, Long-Term Capital Management's $4.6 billion collapse in 1998, Amaranth Advisors' $6.4 billion loss in 2006, Société Générale's $7.2 billion loss in 2008, and AIG's $18+ billion in CDS losses in 2008, which prompted an $85 billion Federal Reserve rescue.

Because the 2008 crisis revealed how opaque OTC derivatives, especially CDS, could threaten the financial system, the Dodd–Frank Act (2010) mandated central clearing, trade reporting, and registration for major swap dealers. CDS contracts had allowed repeated bets on the same mortgages, fueling the subprime crisis. By June 2025 the OTC derivatives market is larger than it was before the crisis, and critics argue that shifting risk to clearing houses relocates systemic vulnerability rather than removing it.
