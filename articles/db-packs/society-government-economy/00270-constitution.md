# Constitution

A constitution is the supreme law of a polity, the set of fundamental rules that decides who governs, how they govern, and what they may not do. It defines the structure of government, the procedures for making ordinary law, and the rights and duties of citizens. Because it sits above ordinary legislation, changing it usually requires more than a simple majority, often a supermajority in the legislature, a referendum, or both. This asymmetry is what gives a constitution its defining feature: it is harder to replace than the laws it authorises.

The cleanest way to picture the concept is to treat the constitution as a rulebook about rulebooks. Parliament may pass statutes, but the constitution specifies the conditions under which statutes are valid, who may issue them, and which rights even a legislature cannot remove. In many systems, courts have the power of judicial review, meaning they can strike down laws or executive actions that contradict the constitution. Acting outside constitutional authority is called *ultra vires*; acting within it is *intra vires*.

**Codified and uncodified.** Constitutions come in two main forms. A codified constitution is written in a single document and is treated as the highest source of law, as in the United States (ratified 1788), France (1791), India (1949), and Canada (1867). An uncodified constitution is assembled from multiple sources: statutes, court decisions, conventions, and treaties, as in the United Kingdom, where no single document holds supreme constitutional status. A few systems mix the two. Codified constitutions typically emerge from political rupture and usually include strict amendment procedures. Uncodified ones evolve gradually.

**Central design choices.** Three choices recur in constitutional design and shape everything else.

1. *Distribution of sovereignty.* A unitary state concentrates sovereignty in the national government; any regional authorities exist at its pleasure. A federal state divides sovereignty constitutionally between a central government and regional units such as states or provinces. A confederal arrangement leaves most sovereignty with the regions and grants the centre only limited, delegated powers.
2. *Separation of powers.* Influenced by Enlightenment thinkers, especially Montesquieu and Locke, most modern constitutions divide government into executive, legislative, and judicial branches so that no single body accumulates unchecked power. Communist state constitutions historically rejected this separation and instead codified the ruling party's programme as the guiding principle of the state.
3. *Entrenched clauses.* Some provisions are designed to be especially hard to amend, protecting core principles such as the form of government or fundamental rights. A supermajority vote, a referendum, or a special constituent assembly may be required to alter them.

**Rights, duties, and emergencies.** Most modern constitutions enumerate individual rights, such as freedom of expression and the right to a fair trial, alongside citizen duties such as paying taxes. They also typically provide for a state of emergency, during which some rights may be temporarily suspended. Constitutional courts or equivalent judicial review bodies then serve as the arbiters of whether emergency measures or ordinary laws remain within constitutional bounds.

**A short history.** Constitutional thinking stretches back to the earliest written law codes, including the Sumerian code of Urukagina (c. 2300 BC) and the Code of Ur-Nammu (c. 2050 BC). The Athenian reforms of Cleisthenes in 508 BC established an early democratic system. Aristotle (c. 350 BC) was the first to formally distinguish constitutional law from ordinary law. In medieval Europe, Magna Carta (1215) limited royal power and established due process. The Leges Statutae of San Marino (1600) remain the oldest active constitution. England's Instrument of Government (1653) was an early detailed written framework, and the United States Constitution (1788), the oldest still-operating codified constitution, became the model for later republican charters. India adopted the world's longest written constitution in 1949. The philosophical basis, the social contract idea that citizens consent to surrender some freedoms in exchange for the protection of their remaining rights, runs from Locke through the Enlightenment into modern democratic constitutions.

**Endurance and open debates.** Research suggests the average lifespan of a written constitution since 1789 is roughly 19 years, so endurance is the exception rather than the rule. Three live debates frame the field. The "dead hand problem" asks whether one generation can legitimately bind later ones to a framework they never consented to. The autochthonous-origin debate, traceable to Hegel, asks whether a constitution must grow from a nation's own history or can be designed and adopted deliberately. And scholars disagree on enforcement: whether constitutions ultimately restrain governments because citizens coordinate against abuse, or because the state's own institutions (courts, police, bureaucracy) do the restraining.
