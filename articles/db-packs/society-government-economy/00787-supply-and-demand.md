# Supply and demand

Supply and demand is the foundational model of how prices and quantities are set in perfectly competitive markets. The core claim: holding everything else equal, the price of a good adjusts until the amount buyers want to buy equals the amount sellers want to sell, producing a **market-clearing equilibrium** with no surplus and no shortage. *Perfect competition* is the setting where the logic works cleanly: many buyers and sellers, none with market power, and full information.

## How the two curves work

The **supply curve** shows how much producers are willing to sell at each price. Under perfect competition each firm is a *price-taker*, accepting the market price rather than setting it, and chooses output by comparing price to **marginal cost** (the cost of producing one additional unit). The curve shifts when input prices, technology, expectations, or the number of firms change. Two standard forms are the linear function Q = 3P − 6 and the constant-elasticity form Q = 5P^0.5, in which the percentage change in quantity for a percentage change in price is fixed.

The **demand curve** shows how much consumers want to buy at each price, driven by the *marginal utility* they expect from each extra unit. The **law of demand** states that, holding other factors constant, higher prices reduce quantity demanded, so the curve typically slopes downward. Standard forms are linear (Q = 32 − 2P) and constant-elasticity (Q = 3P⁻²). Two exceptions break the downward slope: **Veblen goods**, luxury items whose appeal grows with price because of status, and **Giffen goods**, inferior staples whose demand rises with price because the income effect outweighs the substitution effect.

## Equilibrium and comparative statics

Equilibrium is where the two curves cross. **Comparative statics** compares the old equilibrium to a new one after a determinant shifts. An increase in demand shifts the demand curve right, raising both price and quantity. An increase in supply, from new technology for example, shifts the supply curve right, lowering price and raising quantity. Movements *along* a curve reflect price changes; *shifts* of a curve reflect changes in non-price determinants.

## Partial versus general equilibrium

**Partial equilibrium** analyses one market while holding all other prices and incomes fixed, ignoring spillovers. It fits small markets and targeted policy questions. **General equilibrium** models the whole economy at once, capturing interactions across markets. Supply and demand is essentially a partial-equilibrium tool.

## Beyond goods markets

The same logic extends to other markets by treating their central price as the equilibrating variable. In labor markets, wages play the role of price, though empirical support is weaker than for goods. In money markets, central banks can fix either the money supply (a vertical supply curve) or the interest rate (a horizontal supply curve). Applications also include physician, nursing, and teacher shortages.

## Empirical estimation

Because price and quantity are determined together, econometricians use **simultaneous-equation methods**. **Structural estimation** aims to recover the underlying supply and demand equations but must confront the **parameter identification problem**, which requires at least one *exogenous* variable, one determined outside the model such as weather or policy, to distinguish the two curves. **Reduced-form estimation** sidesteps this by regressing outcomes directly on exogenous variables, giving simpler but less interpretable results.

## Macroeconomic limits

At the macro level, **aggregate demand–aggregate supply (AD–AS)** models apply the logic to total output and the overall price level. The catch is the **Sonnenschein–Mantel–Debreu theorem**, which proves that an aggregate demand curve need not inherit the downward slope of the individual demand curves that compose it, so a unique, stable macro equilibrium is not guaranteed. The simple two-curve picture stretches only so far to the whole economy.

## Critiques

**Piero Sraffa** argued that partial equilibrium's assumption of an upward-sloping supply curve is shaky. **Post-Keynesian** economists note that many real prices are **administered prices**, set by firms using markups over cost rather than by market clearing, so supply and demand do not literally meet. The Sonnenschein–Mantel–Debreu result reinforces this by showing that aggregation can break the micro-level properties on which the model rests.

## A short history

The price-adjusting logic has old roots: the *Tirukkural* (c. 2000 BCE) contains an early articulation, and John Locke (1691) and Francis Hutcheson (1755) wrote about price set by buyer–seller ratios. James Denham-Steuart first used the phrase "supply and demand" in 1767, followed by Adam Smith's *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) and Thomas Robert Malthus (1803). David Ricardo titled a 1817 chapter "On the Influence of Demand and Supply on Price." Augustin Cournot (1838) built the first mathematical model with diagrams, Fleeming Jenkin (1870) drew the first English-language curves including comparative statics, and Alfred Marshall (1890) popularised the modern form in *Principles of Economics*.
