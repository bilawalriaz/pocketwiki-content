# Human rights

Human rights are moral standards that belong to every person by virtue of being human. No government grants them and no person can surrender them, a property called inalienability. They cover civil and political rights (life, free speech, freedom from torture), economic and social rights (work, education, healthcare), and cultural rights. The UN Human Rights Council added a right to a clean environment in 2021.

The defining claim is equal dignity: everyone is entitled to a baseline of protection regardless of citizenship, location, or government. This distinguishes modern human rights from earlier privileges that depended on rank, class, or religion.

## Origins

Conceptual roots run through medieval natural law, the idea that some moral rules exist independently of human law. Early Church fathers (Hilary of Poitiers, Ambrose, Augustine) asked whether unjust laws bind the conscience. The 13th-century Kouroukan Fouga charter of the Mali Empire was among the first texts to affirm a right to life and physical integrity. In the 16th–17th centuries the Spanish School of Salamanca (Vitoria, de Soto, Molina) defined law as a moral power over one's own body and spirit and circulated the term *iura naturalia*, natural rights. Their reasoning shaped the 1550–51 Valladolid Debate on the conquest of the Americas.

The Enlightenment translated these claims into political language. John Locke held that rulers violating life, liberty, and property could be overthrown. The Virginia Declaration of Rights (1776), the US Declaration of Independence, and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789) encoded them into law. The phrase "human rights" probably entered English between Thomas Paine's *Rights of Man* and William Lloyd Garrison's 1831 *Liberator*. Labour unions, women's suffrage movements, and anti-colonial struggles, notably Gandhi's, widened the scope in the early 20th century.

## The post-war foundation

The Holocaust discredited the idea that states alone could be trusted to protect their citizens. The 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR), adopted in Paris by the UN General Assembly, became the global reference. Eleanor Roosevelt chaired the drafting, John Humphrey conducted the cross-national research, and René Cassin structured the text around dignity, liberty, equality, and brotherhood. The vote was unanimous, with the Soviet bloc, apartheid South Africa, and Saudi Arabia abstaining. The UDHR framed all rights as a single indivisible whole.

Because the UDHR is non-binding, two treaties followed in 1966 and entered force in 1976: the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR). The split was a Cold War compromise: capitalist states prioritised civil and political rights, socialist states economic and social ones, and the United States has ratified only the ICCPR (in 1992). The "full belly thesis" holds that economic security enables people to exercise political rights, so the division itself undermines indivisibility. Other instruments include the Genocide Convention (1948), conventions on racial discrimination, women, torture, children, and migrant workers, and the Rome Statute (2002) creating the International Criminal Court.

## How rights are enforced

Enforcement takes six forms: accountability through courts; inducement via conditional aid; capacity-building assistance; domestic contestation with local activists; compulsion through sanctions or military intervention; and external adaptation of global rules. Military intervention rests on the Responsibility to Protect doctrine but is controversial when mandates expand; the 2011 NATO intervention in Libya is cited as having shifted from atrocity prevention to regime change. Economic sanctions are criticised as collective punishment that can entrench authoritarian rule. Targeted domestic remedies such as girls' education, minimum income guarantees, and Brazil's Bolsa Família cash transfers have reduced child labour and child marriage.

Global bodies: the UN Human Rights Council (47 members, Geneva, since 2005) investigates violations and can refer cases to the Security Council; treaty bodies monitor each covenant; the International Court of Justice settles disputes between states only; and the International Criminal Court prosecutes individuals for war crimes, crimes against humanity, genocide, and aggression, limited by non-membership (the US, China, Russia, and India have not ratified) and by complementarity, the principle that national courts hold primary jurisdiction until local remedies are exhausted. NGOs such as Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch monitor abuses and apply pressure.

## Regional systems

Three regional systems bind members. The Council of Europe (1949, 47 members) administers the European Convention on Human Rights (1950) and the European Court of Human Rights. The Organization of American States (35 members) runs the Inter-American Commission and Court (San José, since 1979) under the American Convention, American Declaration, and OAS Charter. The African Union (55 states, since 2001) hosts the African Commission and African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights, whose full operation has been delayed by merger plans with the AU Court of Justice. Asia had no continent-wide instrument until ASEAN adopted its declaration on 18 November 2012; the Arab Charter followed on 22 May 2004.

## Distinctions that matter

Some rights are non-derogable: the right to life, freedom from slavery and torture, and the prohibition of retroactive criminal law cannot be suspended even in wartime. These are *jus cogens*, peremptory norms binding on all states and unmodifiable by treaty; other rights may be limited only in genuine, temporary, last-resort emergencies.

Karel Vasak's "three generations" model groups rights as first generation (civil and political), second generation (economic, social, cultural), and third generation (solidarity rights such as environment and development). It is widely used but criticised for implying a hierarchy that contradicts indivisibility.

Universal jurisdiction lets any state prosecute certain crimes regardless of where they occurred or the perpetrator's nationality. Belgium's 1993 law and the 1998 London arrest of Augusto Pinochet on a Spanish indictment are landmark cases. Supporters frame it as a moral duty; critics, including Henry Kissinger, argue it threatens sovereignty.

Multinational corporations can commit serious abuses but currently lack binding human-rights obligations. A 2003 draft of UN Norms on business responsibilities remains non-binding and unmonitored.

## The open tension

Universalism versus cultural relativism is the sharpest unresolved conflict. Critics such as Said Rajaie-Khorassani have called the UDHR a "secular Judeo-Christian" text incompatible with Islamic law, and leaders including Lee Kuan Yew and Mahathir Mohamad have asserted distinct "Asian values." Counterarguments note that modern human rights are a post-1948 invention everywhere, that the UDHR was drafted by representatives of many cultures, and that relativism is most often invoked by powerful actors against victims. Every international instrument reaffirms universal applicability, and the gap between that principle and uneven state practice defines the field.
