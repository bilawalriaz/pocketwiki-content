# Production (economics)

Production combines material inputs (metal, wood, glass, plastics) and immaterial inputs (plans, knowledge) into goods or services that have value and satisfy human needs. The branch of economics that studies this process is production theory, closely linked to consumer theory.

## Factors of production

Economists traditionally identify four primary factors of production:

- **Land**, all natural resources above and below the soil.
- **Labor**, the human effort applied to production, distinct from human capital, which is the knowledge and skill workers accumulate over time.
- **Capital**, the manufactured tools, machines, and structures used to produce other goods.
- **Entrepreneurship**, the capacity to organise the other factors, innovate, and bear risk.

Classical economics treats materials and energy as secondary factors, byproducts of land, labor, and capital. Some schools add technology as a fifth, evolved factor that shifts what production can achieve.

A production function expresses the relationship between input quantities and output quantity. Efficiency is actual output divided by maximum potential output: if 100 units are possible and only 60 are produced, efficiency is 0.6, or 60%. Economies of scale describe whether returns rise, fall, or stay constant as scale grows.

## Three forms of production

The economy runs on three forms of production. Market production creates goods, services, and the incomes that pay for everything else, financed by sales revenue. Public production supplies services the market undersupplies, such as defense and roads, financed by taxes. Household production creates care, meals, and shelter inside the family, financed by incomes from market production. Because only market production both generates output and generates income, public and household production depend on the incomes it creates.

## Five processes inside a producing firm

A producing firm can be broken into five linked processes:

1. The **real process**, in which inputs are converted into output and described by the production function.
2. The **income distribution process**, in which the resulting gains are split among customers (lower prices), suppliers, staff (wages), society (taxes), and owners (profit).
3. The **production process**, the combination of the real process and the income distribution process.
4. The **monetary process**, events that finance the business.
5. The **market value process**, in which investors value the company in stock markets.

## Growth and performance

Economic growth is an increase in real output. Real income equals real output minus real input, so it grows from two sources: more inputs, which move the economy along the existing production function, and higher productivity, which shifts the production function upward. Innovation drives productivity growth, but replication of existing technologies through investment in equipment, structures, software, and labor expansion accounts for roughly 80% of US economic growth since 1947; innovation accounts for the remaining 20%.

Two measures of performance matter. Average productivity is real output divided by real input. Real income, in absolute terms, is real output minus real input. The two can disagree. Hiring a previously unemployed worker into a low-productivity job lowers average productivity but raises real income per capita when the firm operates in a region of diminishing returns, and because firms rarely know which region they occupy, real income is the more reliable guide to performance.

## Measures of production

Three quantities describe production at the firm level. Total product is the full quantity produced, easy to count for motor vehicles, harder for services. Average product is output per worker or per unit of capital. Marginal product is the extra output from one more worker or one more machine in the short run. The law of diminishing marginal returns states that as more units of a variable input are added to a fixed input, marginal output first rises, then falls. In the short run, at least one factor is fixed; in the long run, all factors become variable, though the transition can take years in capital-heavy industries such as nuclear power.

## Surplus value and the income model

Profitability can be summarised by surplus value, the difference between the value of output and the cost of inputs, including the cost of equity. If surplus value is positive, the output is worth more than the resources consumed. Real surplus value to the producer is what remains after real inputs are subtracted from real output, and its growth rate is productivity.

Accounting separates changes in profitability into two effects, holding other variables constant (ceteris paribus, "all else equal"). The income distribution effect is the change attributable to shifts in output and input prices, calculated with quantities held fixed. The real process effect is the change attributable to shifts in quantities and productivity, calculated with prices held fixed. In a worked example, real income grew by 58.12 units, of which 41.12 came from productivity growth and 17.00 from production volume growth, putting the firm on the increasing-returns segment of its production function. The 58.12 was distributed as 39.00 units to customers and suppliers and 19.12 to owners.

The income generated in the real process is always exactly equal to the income distributed among stakeholders. This duality lets the same result be reached either by adding up quantity and productivity changes or by adding up unit-price changes multiplied by quantities. Jobless growth, in which output and income rise without new jobs, arises when productivity growth alone carries real income upward.
