# Health economics

Health economics applies economic reasoning to a sector where ordinary market assumptions break down. Health is wanted, produced, and consumed at the same time; prices are hidden by insurers; and decisions ripple onto other people.

## Why health is unusual as an economic good

Health behaves like a *durable capital stock* that depreciates with age and can be raised through investment. The dominant model is Michael Grossman's 1972 model of health production, in which each person is both producer and consumer of their own health. The optimal stock is set where the marginal cost of holding it equals the marginal benefit:

$$MC_{HK} = r + \delta$$

where r is the interest rate and δ is the depreciation rate. Because δ rises with age while the marginal benefit of further health falls, the model predicts the optimal stock declines over a lifetime. Lifestyle choices (smoking, diet, exercise) act as inputs that either raise or erode that stock.

Three structural features keep healthcare markets from working like textbook markets:

- **Third-party payers.** Insurers or employers, not the patient, usually pay the bill, so the consumer sees a subsidized price rather than the true cost. This splits demand into a "real" curve based on what health is worth and an "effective" curve based on the subsidized out-of-pocket price. The gap drives *ex-post moral hazard*: insured people consume more care because they do not bear the full cost.
- **Asymmetric information and adverse selection.** Insurers cannot fully predict who will be sick, so the sickest people are most eager to buy coverage. Features like risk pools, group plans, and exclusions on pre-existing conditions exist to keep insurance markets from collapsing.
- **Externalities.** One person's infections, untreated mental illness, or opioid use imposes costs on others, so private decisions produce socially suboptimal outcomes. Smoking cessation, vaccination, and infection control are standard policy targets for this reason.

## Scope

Alan Williams's "plumbing diagram" divides the field into eight questions: what influences health besides healthcare, how to value health, demand for care, supply of care, micro-evaluation at treatment level, market equilibrium, system-level evaluation, and planning and budgeting. Five health markets are typically analyzed: financing, physician and nurse services, institutional services, input factors, and professional education.

## History

Health economics crystallized when Kenneth Arrow's 1963 paper, "Uncertainty and the Welfare Economics of Medical Care," drew explicit distinctions between health and ordinary goods. After World War II, spending rose sharply: U.S. health expenditure grew from 5.0% to 17.4% of GDP between 1960 and 2013, with nominal spending growing 9.2% per year versus 6.7% for GDP overall. European healthcare moved from roughly 4% of GDP in the 1950s to 8% by the late 1970s. The AMA Bureau of Medical Economics (1931), a WHO seminar in Moscow (1968), and Selma Muskin's 1958 paper all marked the field's institutional formation. Nursing economics spun off in the 1980s, formalized by *Nursing Economic* magazine in 1983.

## Evaluating treatments

Cost-utility analysis compares an intervention's incremental cost against the Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALYs) it produces. Agencies such as the UK's NICE use this to decide what a public system will pay for. Cost-effectiveness thresholds vary sharply by country: a 2019 cross-country study found thresholds ranging from 0.14 GDP per capita per QALY in Ethiopia to 1.47 in the United States. The RAND Health Insurance Experiment showed that cost-sharing reduces utilisation, confirming moral hazard in real populations.

## When markets fail

Health insurance markets can fail because of adverse selection, or because catastrophic conditions (cancer, HIV/AIDS, COVID-19) are too expensive or unpredictable to insure privately. Underinsurance leaves gaps that governments typically fill; India's Ayushman Bharat scheme (launched 2018) is the world's largest such program. Monopoly power is common in markets with few hospitals or patented drugs, and physician-owned-hospital restrictions are debated as a brake on competition. The Baumol effect adds a slower pressure: wages in low-productivity-growth sectors like healthcare rise to match the rest of the economy, pushing costs up even when productivity is flat.
