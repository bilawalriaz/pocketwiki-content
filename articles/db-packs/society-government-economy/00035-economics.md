# Economics

Economics is a social science studying the production, distribution, and consumption of goods and services, focusing on how economic agents behave and how economies function. It divides into **microeconomics**, which analyses individual agents (households, firms, buyers, sellers) and markets, and **macroeconomics**, which analyses whole economies where production, consumption, savings, and investment interact. The discipline evolved from "political economy," from the Ancient Greek *oikonomia*, the management of a household.

## Defining the field

Lionel Robbins's 1932 definition, "the science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses," is the most commonly accepted. Robbins framed it as analytical, focused on the aspect of behaviour imposed by scarcity. Critics find it overly broad. Economists including James M. Buchanan and Ronald Coase prefer subject-matter definitions such as Jean-Baptiste Say's 1803 description of economics as the science of production, distribution, and consumption of wealth.

## Classical political economy

Adam Smith's *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) is treated as the effective birth of economics as a separate discipline. Smith identified land, labour, and capital as the three factors of production, argued for specialisation through the division of labour, and proposed the "invisible hand," by which individuals pursuing their own gain frequently promote that of society. Thomas Malthus (1798) argued that population grows geometrically while food production grows arithmetically, producing diminishing returns and chronically low wages. David Ricardo (1817) focused on income distribution among landowners, workers, and capitalists and was the first to state the principle of comparative advantage, under which each country should specialise in goods with a lower relative cost of production. John Stuart Mill (1848) broke with earlier classical economists by arguing society must intervene in the market's distribution of income.

## Marxian economics

Karl Marx published the first volume of *Das Kapital* in 1867. Marxian economics descends from classical economics but centres on the labour theory of value, which held that a commodity's value is determined by the labour needed to produce it, and the theory of surplus value, which holds that workers are paid only part of the value their work creates, a mechanism of exploitation by capital.

## Neoclassical economics

Neoclassical economics formed between about 1870 and 1910. Alfred Marshall's *Principles of Economics* (1890) popularised the term "economics" and extended analysis to the microeconomic level. The school integrated supply and demand as joint determinants of price and quantity in market equilibrium, replacing the labour theory of value with marginal utility theory on the demand side and a more comprehensive theory of costs on the supply side. Twentieth-century theorists adopted ordinal utility, ranking preferences rather than measuring total utility, and assumed agents act rationally with stable preferences.

## Keynesian economics and its successors

John Maynard Keynes's *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936) introduced contemporary macroeconomics, explaining why high unemployment might not self-correct due to insufficient **effective demand** (aggregate spending by households, firms, and government). After World War II, Keynesian economics dominated the US and its allies while Marxian economics dominated the Soviet Union. Milton Friedman's monetarism (1950s–1960s) argued that monetary policy matters more than fiscal policy. New classical economists (Robert Lucas, Thomas Sargent, Edward Prescott) introduced rational expectations in the 1970s, the assumption that agents form expectations using all available information. New Keynesian economists (George Akerlof, Janet Yellen, Gregory Mankiw, Olivier Blanchard) adopted rational expectations but emphasised market failures and price or wage rigidity. By the 2000s, a new neoclassical synthesis emerged, producing dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models, mathematical simulations of the whole economy subject to random shocks, now standard in most central banks. After 2008, macroeconomic research placed greater weight on the financial system and behavioural economics.

## Methodology and branches

Mainstream economics relies on analytical models tested through econometrics; controlled experiments are difficult, though experimental economics is growing. Microeconomics studies market structures from perfect competition to monopoly, oligopoly, and monopsony, along with the production–possibility frontier (a graph showing the maximum output combinations an economy can produce), Pareto efficiency, and market failure. Macroeconomics examines national income, unemployment (frictional, structural, cyclical), inflation, economic growth, monetary policy (typically policy-rate targeting by central banks), and fiscal policy, which can be limited by crowding out (private spending falling as government spending rises) and Ricardian equivalence (the idea that government borrowing and future taxation have similar effects to current taxation, so consumers save against expected tax rises). Economic inequality, often measured by the Gini coefficient, has been linked to political and social instability.

## Open questions

There is no single agreed definition of economics. Ha-Joon Chang argues that Robbins's method-based definition is unusual because other sciences define themselves by subject matter. Empirical testing in economics is generally regarded as less rigorous than controlled experimentation, and shared methods need not produce consensus on a given question.

## Key terms

- **Comparative advantage**: each country should specialise in goods with a lower relative cost of production.
- **Labour theory of value**: a commodity's value is determined by the labour required to produce it.
- **Pareto efficiency**: no change can make one person better off without making another worse off.
- **Market failure**: problems including information asymmetries, natural monopoly, public goods, and externalities.
