# Local government

Local government is the lowest tier of public administration in a sovereign state, the level closest to residents, responsible for delivering everyday services within small geographic areas. A municipal council (also called city council, town council, borough council, board of selectmen, or many other names depending on the country) is the legislative body that governs it.

## How it fits into the state

Local governments sit below state or national governments and act within powers delegated by higher levels. In a **federal** system (where power is shared between national and regional governments, e.g., the US, Germany, Brazil), local government is usually the third or fourth tier. In a **unitary** system (where ultimate authority rests with the national government, e.g., France, Japan), it is the second or third tier. Local entities typically provide roads, water, waste, schools, and policing; raise some taxes; and depend on grants from higher levels for the rest.

## What makes a body "local government"

The International Monetary Fund offers a precise definition: an institutional unit whose fiscal, legislative, and executive authority extends over the smallest geographical areas distinguished for administrative and political purposes. The OECD adds that these bodies are usually elected by universal suffrage (the right of all adults to vote) and have autonomy over budget, staff, and assets. Many countries also have **special-purpose** local governments, such as school districts or water boards, that perform one function rather than acting as general-purpose municipalities.

## How local councils are structured

A common pattern worldwide is a directly elected council plus an executive:

- **France** runs 36,679 *communes*, each with a mayor and council of identical legal powers regardless of population, a deliberate legacy of the Revolution that erased old local privileges. Above them sit *départements* and *régions*, with *intercommunalities* (shared structures pooling several communes) in between.
- **Japan** uses 47 prefectures mediating between national and municipal councils, which were historically villages and now often merge for efficiency.
- **Israel** recognises three main types: cities (over 20,000 residents), local councils (2,000–20,000), and regional councils governing clusters of small communities; elections occur every five years.
- **Norway** has 357 municipalities grouped into 15 counties, with elections every four years and municipal councils (*Kommunestyre*) dating back to 1838.
- **Sweden** funds its 290 municipalities mainly through a roughly 20% local income tax, with county councils handling healthcare.
- **The Philippines** layers provinces, cities or municipalities, and barangays (the smallest administrative division, essentially a village or neighbourhood).

A contrasting pattern is the **Dutch model**: in the Netherlands, municipal mayors are appointed by the Crown for six-year terms rather than elected, while councils are elected every four years. Water boards, the oldest tier, manage flooding and water levels through stakeholder representatives (landholders, residents) rather than residents alone.

## Why the variation matters

The same word can mean very different things across borders. A "city" in Australia requires a special grant of city status; in New Zealand it requires at least 50,000 residents; in France it can be a hamlet of a few hundred. Local elections may be abolished entirely (Malaysia since 1976, with councillors appointed by state governments) or run on universal suffrage (most of Europe). Some local governments, like Hong Kong's Municipal Council (disbanded 1999), have been abolished when their functions were absorbed upward. This diversity explains why a single legal template rarely travels successfully between countries.

## Key trade-offs and constraints

Local governments everywhere face the same tensions. They are closest to constituents yet usually have the least revenue, depending on national transfers for a large share of spending. Their formal powers may be wide on paper yet narrow in practice if higher levels appoint key officials, set binding laws, or audit budgets. Bangladesh illustrates one extreme: there are no directly elected officials at the divisional or district level, only at the union and ward level, while in South Africa municipalities can set far-reaching local bylaws but still operate under provincial and national law.
