# Business software

Business software is any computer program used by employees to perform business functions: increasing productivity, measuring it, and running operations accurately. The term overlaps with *business application* and is distinct from *commercial software* (sold to anyone) and *proprietary software* (whose source code is kept secret).

## Why most business software is custom

Each company has its own processes, so a generic package rarely fits perfectly. Off-the-shelf solutions save time and money but almost always need some customization. Exceptions exist, but businesses must research the choice between bespoke and ready-made software.

## How business software runs

Some applications are **interactive**: they have a graphical interface, let users query, input, and modify data, and produce results instantly. Others run in **batch mode**, executing on a schedule or in response to a trigger, with no human initiation. Applications may be developed in-house or bought from vendors, and run on personal workstations or central servers.

## Market segments

The market is commonly divided by company size:

- **Small business**: home accounting software and general office suites (LibreOffice, Microsoft Office, Google Workspace).
- **Small and medium-sized enterprise (SME)**: accounting, groupware (software for collaborative team work), customer relationship management (CRM), human resource management, outsourcing management, loan origination, shopping cart, and field service software.
- **Large enterprise**: enterprise resource planning (ERP), enterprise content management (ECM), business process management (BPM), and product lifecycle management. These platforms are modular and often incorporate third-party components.

## Common categories

- **Digital dashboards** (also called business intelligence or executive dashboards): visual summaries of metrics and key performance indicators (KPIs).
- **Online analytical processing (OLAP)**: interactive examination of large datasets from many perspectives. Variants include HOLAP (hybrid), ROLAP (relational), and MOLAP (multidimensional).
- **Reporting software**: aggregated views that keep management informed.
- **Procurement software**: automates purchasing.
- **Data mining**: extracts previously unknown patterns from large datasets, using statistical methods and neural networks.
- **Customer Relationship Management (CRM)**: e.g., Yesware.
- **Document management**: organizes and secures business documents.
- **Employee scheduling**: creates and tracks work schedules and hours.
- **Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP)**: integrates many operational functions and acts as the organization's system of record, the authoritative single source of data.

## History

Early business automation ran on large **mainframe computers** that handled clerical work such as bank cheque clearing and factory accounting. Factory accounting software was among the first widely adopted business tools, automating general ledgers (a company's core financial accounts), fixed-asset inventories, cost ledgers, accounts receivable, accounts payable, payroll, and insurance records. One computer could replace many administrative staff, which made early adoption extremely profitable.

Firms then demanded more from suppliers like IBM and Hewlett-Packard. **Computer-aided drafting for computer-aided manufacturing (CAD-CAM)** appeared in the early 1980s, and project management software became so valued it cost up to $500,000 per copy.

The **word processor** was the most visible transformation: it displaced the IBM typewriter in the 1980s through Word Perfect and later Microsoft Word. Mathematical spreadsheets, Lotus 1-2-3 and then Microsoft Excel, spread alongside it.

In the 1990s globalization and the internet drove adoption of **SAP** software, which coordinates supply chains (the network of vendors, factories, and distributors that move a product from raw material to customer) to streamline manufacturing.

The current phase is shaped by **robotic process automation (RPA)**, which automates highly repetitive tasks to cut costs, speed operations, and reduce human error. RPA is most advanced in insurance, banking, financial services, law, and healthcare.

## How business software fails and how it is fixed

Applications run smoothly until business requirements change, underlying transactions change, or something in the technical environment breaks: hardware, networks, disks, power, middleware (software that lets different programs communicate and share data), databases, or program code. Failures originate from bad data, infrastructure problems, programming bugs, human mistakes, or flawed workflows.

When a failure occurs, a **support process** typically unfolds:

1. The user contacts the support team by phone or email with the error details, which are entered into a tracking system that issues a request number.
2. For critical failures, an email alerts the whole organization or affected teams, with an estimated recovery time.
3. The assigned support engineer gathers the relevant data, reviews the application for programming errors, and consults the knowledge base. New errors generate new resolution steps that are recorded for the future.
4. Fixes are routed to the right team: programming errors go to developers, workflow or data-entry errors trigger user retraining and revised instructions, and infrastructure problems go to the infrastructure team for permanent repair.
5. The tracking system is reviewed periodically (daily, weekly, monthly) to monitor resolved, recurring, and pending issues, with reports going to IT management.
