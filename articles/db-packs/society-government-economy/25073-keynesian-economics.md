# Keynesian economics

Keynesian economics is the body of macroeconomic theory built around the claim that aggregate demand, the total spending in an economy, drives output, employment, and inflation, and that governments and central banks can stabilise the business cycle when this demand is volatile. The school is named after John Maynard Keynes, who set out its foundations in *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936), written during the Great Depression, when unemployment in the United States reached 25% and as high as 33% in some countries.

Classical economics had rested on Say's law, that "supply creates its own demand," and on the assumption that wages would fall to clear the labour market. Keynes rejected both. He argued that spending decisions could be weak or erratic, leaving demand below the economy's productive capacity, a situation the classical tradition called a "general glut." When entrepreneurs respond by laying off workers, demand falls further and the slump deepens. The classical "first postulate" that the wage equals the marginal product of labour could not, Keynes argued, explain the persistent involuntary unemployment of the 1930s.

## The core model

In Keynes's system, the level of income is set where saving equals investment, written I(r) = S(Y). The incentive to invest depends on the interest rate r through the marginal efficiency of capital. The incentive to save depends on income Y. Because the two sides are decided by independent forces, they need not match at full employment.

The interest rate itself is set by liquidity preference, the demand for money. In Keynes's simplest account the demand for money depends only on r, so that L(r) equals the fixed money supply M̂. The textbook IS–LM model, developed by John Hicks in 1937, generalises this by allowing money demand to depend on income as well as the interest rate, plotting the IS curve I(Y, r) = S(Y, r) against the LM curve L(Y, r) = M̂. Equilibrium income Ŷ and interest rate r̂ are given by their intersection.

The multiplier, formalised by Richard Kahn in 1931, gives the response of income to an initial spending injection. With a marginal propensity to consume of 2/3, an initial outlay of $1,000 sets off a chain of respending, $666.67, then $444.44, and so on, so total income rises by more than the initial spending. Keynes's investment multiplier equals 1 divided by the marginal propensity to save, k = 1/S′(Y).

When the liquidity preference curve becomes nearly vertical, as can happen at very low interest rates, extra money supply no longer pushes r down. This is the liquidity trap, a term coined by Dennis Robertson and developed by Hicks, and described by Paul Krugman as the condition that trapped Japan's economy around 2000, when short rates near zero failed to lift investment.

## Policy

Keynesian policy treats fiscal action, government spending and taxation, and monetary action, interest rates set by a central bank, as tools for smoothing the cycle. Lower interest rates stimulate borrowing for big purchases and capital investment; expansionary fiscal policy, meaning higher net public spending, financed if necessary by borrowing, raises aggregate demand. Counter-cyclical policy means stimulus in slumps and restraint in booms, and does not require deficits. A constant deficit of 10% of GDP is neutral; a shift from a 10% deficit to 5% is contractionary.

Keynesians reply to the classical "crowding out" objection, that government borrowing raises interest rates and starves private investment, by pointing to slack: with unemployment above the non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment (NAIRU), stimulus can "crowd in" private spending through higher profits and the accelerator effect, and can fund public goods such as basic research, education, and infrastructure that raise potential output. The liquidity trap sets a floor on monetary policy and gives fiscal policy the leading role.

Keynes also advocated managed trade. As leader of the British delegation to the 1944 Bretton Woods conference, he proposed the Keynes Plan, an International Clearing Union issuing a unit called the bancor, with the principle that surplus countries exert a "negative externality" on trading partners and bear equal responsibility with debtors for restoring balance.

## History and variants

Blended with neoclassical economics into the "neoclassical synthesis," Keynesian theory was the dominant framework in industrialised economies from the late Depression through the post-war expansion of 1945–1973, a period of low and stable unemployment and modest inflation called the Golden Age of Capitalism.

The 1973 oil shock and the stagflation that followed, with unemployment and inflation rising together in defiance of the Phillips curve, broke the consensus. Monetarism, supply-side economics, and new classical macroeconomics, based on rational expectations and the Lucas critique, displaced Keynesian policy. By the late 1980s New Keynesian economics had revived the tradition on more rigorous microfoundations, fusing Keynesian elements with new classical ones into the "new neoclassical synthesis," today's mainstream. Post-Keynesian economics remains a heterodox school that rejects the neoclassical synthesis and emphasises distributional and institutional features. Governments returned to Keynesian-style stimulus during the 2008 financial crisis in what has been called the 2008–2009 Keynesian resurgence.

## Criticism

Austrian-school economists Friedrich Hayek and Ludwig von Mises accused Keynesianism of distorting price signals through artificial credit expansion and deficit spending, producing malinvestment, inflation, and unsustainable debt. Monetarists, above all Milton Friedman, accepted that demand matters but argued that the economy tends toward equilibrium if monetary policy follows stable rules rather than discretion. By the 1980s, debate converged on the view that central banks should bear primary responsibility for stabilisation, with monetary policy guided by rules such as the Taylor rule; the 2008 crisis revived interest in fiscal intervention precisely because interest rates were already at the liquidity-trap floor. James M. Buchanan criticised Keynesianism from public-choice theory, arguing that democratic politics would turn short-run stimulus into permanent fiscal indiscipline; Kalecki warned that sustained full employment would erode business elites' political power and provoke a backlash against the policy itself.
