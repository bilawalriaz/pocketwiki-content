# Common good (economics)

A **common good** is a resource that is **rivalrous** (one person's use diminishes what's left for others) and **non-excludable** (no practical way to keep outsiders from using it). Those two traits place common goods in one of the four canonical categories of economic goods, alongside private goods, club goods, and public goods.

Wild fish illustrate the combination: anyone can cast a net, yet every fish caught is gone for everyone else. Other examples are clean water, climate stability, ocean fish stocks, grazing land, timber, oil, and freely accessible crops.

## Overexploitation

Because nobody can be excluded, each user captures the full benefit of what they take while bearing only a fraction of the cost of depletion. Acting on that self-interest, individuals extract more than the resource can renew, leaving less for everyone. Aristotle described the underlying problem: "What is common to many is taken least care of, for all men have greater regard for what is their own than for what they possess in common with others."

A congested road shows how the same asset can switch categories. With light traffic a road is non-rivalrous and behaves like a public good. Once congested, an extra driver slows everyone else, a *negative externality* appears, and the road becomes a common good.

## Tragedy of the commons

The standard name for this depletion pattern is the **tragedy of the commons**, a phrase from an 1833 pamphlet by the Victorian economist William Forster Lloyd. He imagined a shared grazing pasture open to every villager; each herdsman adds cattle because the gain is his while the overgrazing is shared, so the field is stripped bare. Unregulated international fisheries follow the same logic: unrestricted withdrawal driven by commercial value can collapse a stock.

The mechanism rests on three ingredients: no barriers to entry, demand running above sustainable yield, and no way to enforce restraint. *Free riders* who keep extracting while hoping others will cut back lock the group into over-consumption even when each person privately prefers a smaller, sustainable herd.

## Keeping common goods sustainable

Two conditions keep extraction within a resource's regenerative capacity:

1. Total withdrawals stay below the threshold at which future yields start to fall.
2. A community-level institution restricts who may use the common good and caps how much each member may take. Resources managed this way are called **common-pool resources**.

Pre-enclosure English villages applied that logic through seasonal grazing, crop rotation, and enforceable sanctions.

## Responses to overuse

When self-regulation fails, three broad remedies exist:

- **Assign property rights**, converting the common-pool resource into a private good whose owner has a personal stake in keeping the stock healthy.
- **Government intervention**: allocate use rights, cap herd sizes, or tax the externality so private costs align with social cost.
- **Collective agreements** among users, like the medieval grazing pacts, that bind participants to limits backed by sanctions.

| | Excludable | Non-excludable |
|---|---|---|
| **Rivalrous** | Private goods (food, clothing) | Common-pool resources (fish stocks, timber) |
| **Non-rivalrous** | Club goods (cinemas, software) | Public goods (national defense, free-to-air TV) |

Common goods sit in the top-right cell: competitive and open. Public goods raise no rivalry concern at all, which is why an unmanaged fishery collapses while national defense does not.

## Relation to normal goods

A **normal good** has demand that rises with consumer income and a negative price elasticity of demand, so quantity demanded moves opposite to price, with substitution and income effects reinforcing each other. Because that demand pattern matches the behaviour of a common good, a normal good in this technical sense is also classified as a common good. The two terms describe overlapping slices of the same four-quadrant taxonomy.
