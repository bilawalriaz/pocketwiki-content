# Civil and political rights

Civil and political rights are a class of rights that shield individuals from infringement by governments, social organizations, and private actors, and that guarantee participation in civic and political life. They form the original core of international human rights law and are usually classed as "first-generation" and "negative" rights: they restrain the state and others from acting against the individual, rather than obligating it to provide resources.

**What the two halves cover.** Civil rights protect personal integrity, life, and safety, freedom from discrimination, the right to privacy, and freedoms of thought, speech, religion, press, assembly, and movement. Political rights protect fair treatment by the legal system and participation in public life: natural justice (procedural fairness), due process, rights of the accused including a fair trial, the right to seek a legal remedy, freedom of association, the right to assemble and petition, self-defence, and voting. These rights must have the force of law and fit within administrative justice; a state that better guarantees political rights tends to have better relations with its citizens.

**Historical roots.** The phrase descends from Latin *jus civis*, the right of the citizen. Roman law distinguished free citizens (*libertas*) from slaves (*servitus*); the Edict of Milan (313) added freedom of religion, and the Edict of Thessalonica (380) imposed Nicene Christianity on all subjects. After Roman doctrine was lost in the Middle Ages, universal rights claims were argued from Christian doctrine, as in Kett's Rebellion (1549), where rebels declared God had made all free.

The modern lineage runs through Sir Edward Coke's seventeenth-century revival of citizenship-based rights under English common law, the English Bill of Rights (1689), George Mason's and James Madison's Virginia Declaration of Rights (1776), and the U.S. Bill of Rights (1789), with the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen following in 1789. In early nineteenth-century Britain, "civil rights" mainly meant the legal disabilities imposed on Catholics, ended by the Roman Catholic Relief Act 1829. In the United States, the term later became tied to the 1954–1968 civil rights movement, which fought racism, segregation, and Jim Crow laws in streets, courts, and government, and extended to transgender and LGBTQ access to public spaces.

**Protection and universality.** T. H. Marshall observed that civil rights were codified first, political rights next, and social rights last. Most democracies write them into constitutions or bills of rights, and they appear in international instruments: Articles 3–21 of the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the 1966 International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, followed in Europe by the European Convention on Human Rights in 1953. Codification is not strictly required, and unenumerated rights can also be recognised, as the U.S. Ninth Amendment acknowledges. Thomas Jefferson held that such rights derive from natural law, not from the grant of any magistrate.

A persistent controversy is whether these rights attach to citizenship or to personhood. Although some states grant citizens stronger protections than non-citizens, civil and political rights are usually treated as universal. Political science evidence indicates that populations denied political rights, including racial, ethnic, tribal, and religious minorities, face a higher risk of political violence, so extending equal rights functions as a tool of stability.

**Movements and enforcement.** Civil rights guarantee equal protection under the law; when guarantees exist only on paper, legal action and unrest tend to follow. In the United States the Declaration of Rights and Sentiments, adopted at the Seneca Falls Convention on July 19–20, 1848, launched the women's movement. Between roughly 1950 and 1980, equality-before-the-law movements appeared worldwide, including the U.S. civil rights movement, the Northern Ireland Civil Rights Association formed in 1967, and dissent movements such as the Prague Spring and Charter 77 in Czechoslovakia and the Hungarian uprisings. Most relied on nonviolent civil resistance, though some were accompanied by armed rebellion; many did not fully achieve their aims. Theorists including Wesley Newcomb Hohfeld, Robert Nozick in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974), and John Rawls in *A Theory of Justice* (1971) offered competing accounts of how such rights should be justified and limited. Organisations that monitor or defend these rights include the ACLU (founded 1920) and the NAACP (1909).
