# Inflation

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level of goods and services, which reduces the purchasing power of money. Each unit of currency buys less than before. The annual percentage change in a price index, usually the consumer price index (CPI), is called the inflation rate. Its opposite is deflation; disinflation is a slowdown in the rate of inflation.

## What causes inflation

Economists identify three main drivers in the "triangle model": demand-pull, where aggregate demand grows faster than aggregate supply, often from expansionary fiscal or monetary policy; cost-push, where aggregate supply contracts, as when an oil shock or natural disaster raises production costs that pass through to prices; and built-in, where workers and firms expect continued inflation and set wages and prices to keep pace, producing a self-reinforcing wage-price spiral.

The quantity theory of money restates this in one identity, MV = PQ, where M is the money supply, V is its velocity, P is the price level, and Q is real output. If V and Q are stable, changes in M translate directly into changes in P. Milton Friedman summarized the implication: "Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon."

Hyperinflation occurs when the currency loses value so rapidly that prices may rise more than 50 percent per month. Venezuela reached 833,997% annually by October 2018. When confidence collapses, people hoard goods, switch to foreign currencies, or abandon domestic money altogether.

## Why expectations matter

Inflation expectations are central to actual inflation. They enter nominal interest rates (the Fisher equation: nominal rate ≈ real rate + expected inflation) and wage contracts. A central bank seen as "tough" anchors expectations, making disinflation cheaper; a bank seen as "soft" allows expectations to drift upward, which becomes self-fulfilling.

The Phillips curve describes a short-run tradeoff between unemployment and inflation, but the relationship breaks down once expectations adjust, as stagflation in the 1970s demonstrated. There is a natural rate of unemployment (NAIRU) at which inflation is stable; pushing unemployment below it accelerates inflation, pushing it above it decelerates inflation.

## How inflation is measured

Price indices track a representative basket of goods. The CPI is the most widely cited; the GDP deflator covers all domestically produced goods; the producer price index (PPI) tracks wholesale prices and tends to lead the CPI. Core inflation strips out volatile food and energy to reveal the underlying trend that central banks use as a policy guide.

The annual rate is the percentage change in the index. Basket weights are updated regularly because consumer behavior shifts; the COVID-19 lockdowns showed how quickly a basket can become unrepresentative.

## Effects of inflation

Negative effects include erosion of fixed incomes, since pensions and wages indexed only occasionally lose real value; redistribution from lenders to borrowers (real interest rate ≈ nominal rate minus inflation, or R = N − I); uncertainty that discourages investment and saving; shoe-leather costs from frequent cash withdrawals; menu costs from repricing; and bracket creep as inflation pushes taxpayers into higher brackets without real income gains. High inflation obscures relative price signals, reducing allocative efficiency. Historically, food-price spikes have helped trigger unrest, from the French Revolution and Germany's 1920s hyperinflation to the 2011 Arab Spring uprisings.

Positive effects include allowing real wages to fall without nominal cuts, which speeds labor-market adjustment since nominal wages are sticky downward; giving central banks room to cut nominal rates in a downturn rather than hitting the zero lower bound (the liquidity trap); and the Mundell–Tobin effect, in which moderate inflation discourages hoarding and encourages investment in productive capital.

Most economists favor a low but positive rate, around 2% in most OECD countries, rather than zero. Low positive inflation keeps expectations anchored, avoids the liquidity trap, and reduces the risk that a shock pushes the economy into deflation, whose dynamics are harder to escape.

## Controlling inflation

The dominant tool is monetary policy. Most central banks practice inflation targeting, adjusting the policy interest rate to steer inflation toward an explicit goal. New Zealand adopted the first official target in 1990, and as of 2023 all G7 central banks follow versions of this approach. The monetary transmission mechanism runs from interest rates through credit conditions to aggregate demand and finally to prices.

Earlier regimes included the gold standard (abandoned during the Great Depression), the Bretton Woods fixed-exchange-rate system (collapsed in the 1970s), and monetarist money-growth targeting (abandoned because the link between monetary aggregates and prices proved unstable). Fixed exchange rates remain common in emerging economies; they import the anchor currency's inflation rate but sacrifice independent monetary policy. Price controls have a poor historical record outside wartime rationing.

High government debt raises inflation risk through fiscal dominance, because governments face pressure to erode its real value via inflation, and a permanent 1% of GDP rise in the primary deficit has been linked, after five years, to several hundred to about $1,250 in lost purchasing power per U.S. household.
