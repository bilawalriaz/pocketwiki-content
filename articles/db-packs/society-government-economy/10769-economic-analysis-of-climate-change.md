# Economic analysis of climate change

Economic analysis of climate change uses economic tools to measure the damages caused by warming, weigh the costs of action against inaction, and guide policy. Warming alters markets (food, energy, insurance, labor) and damages ecosystems that lack market prices, so climate science and economics are combined to value impacts in money. The main purposes are estimating global and sectoral damage, evaluating mitigation and adaptation policies, calculating the social cost of carbon (damage per extra tonne of CO₂ emitted), and informing policy at national and international levels.

## What damages look like

Damages rise non-linearly with temperature. The 2022 IPCC report estimated that under high warming (~4°C) with little adaptation, annual global GDP could fall 10 to 23 percent by 2100; under low warming with more adaptation the range is 1 to 8 percent. These figures exclude tipping points (critical thresholds where the climate system shifts abruptly), conflict, migration, biodiversity loss, and inequality effects, all of which would push damages higher. One 2018 study found that omitting tipping points understates global impacts by a factor of two to eight.

Losses are already measurable. Direct disaster losses averaged over US$330 billion per year from 2015 to 2021, a sevenfold increase since the 1970s, driven mostly by greater exposure but partly by human-caused warming. A 2025 econometric study that added cross-border trade effects raised projected 2100 damage estimates from 11 percent to 40 percent of GDP under a very high emission scenario.

Distribution matters. Rich countries emit more CO₂ per person, but warming impacts are unequally distributed. Wealthy countries in colder regions have felt little impact or benefited, while poor countries in hotter regions have grown less than they would have without warming. Agriculture, fisheries, and outdoor labor in low-income tropical economies are most exposed. Climate change deepens inequality both between and within countries.

Inflation is a second channel. Climate impacts add 0.32 to 1.18 percentage points to annual inflation, and 0.92 to 3.23 points to food inflation, through higher energy, food, and insurance costs. About 3.5 million people die prematurely each year from fossil fuel air pollution, a non-market cost of current energy systems.

## Modeling approaches

Three families of models dominate:

- **Process-based integrated assessment models (IAMs)** link detailed representations of energy, land, and climate systems to explore emissions pathways compatible with the Paris Agreement's 1.5°C and 2°C targets. Examples: IMAGE, MESSAGEix, AIM/GCE, GCAM, REMIND-MAgPIE, WITCH-GLOBIOM.
- **Computable general equilibrium (CGE) models** use real economic data to estimate how policy changes ripple through prices, trade, and output. They assume cost-minimizing producers and optimizing households.
- **Econometric (statistical) models** correlate historical weather variation with economic outcomes to estimate how temperature, rainfall, and storms affect agriculture, productivity, and growth.

**Aggregate cost-benefit IAMs** are a simpler class that combines climate science and economics into a single welfare calculation in money, identifying the emissions path that maximizes net benefit. They lack the sectoral detail of process-based IAMs.

**Damage functions** quantify how a hazard (heat, flood, crop loss) translates into economic loss given exposure and vulnerability. They are central to every cost estimate and a major source of disagreement across studies.

## Analytical frameworks

**Cost-benefit analysis (CBA)** translates impacts into money using willingness to pay (the maximum a person would pay for a benefit) or willingness to accept compensation, discounts future values to the present, and accepts a project if winners can in principle compensate losers with surplus remaining, the Kaldor-Hicks criterion. A core difficulty: future generations cannot compensate the current generation for mitigation costs, and the current generation cannot compensate future generations for damages. CBA also struggles to value biodiversity, health, and catastrophic risk.

**Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA)** ranks policy options by cost per unit of outcome (dollars per tonne of CO₂ avoided, or cost per hectare of land protected from sea-level rise) without judging whether the goal is worth pursuing.

**Risk and portfolio approaches** treat climate as an uncertain distribution of outcomes. Iterative risk management makes decisions sequentially, preserving options as information improves; near-term emission cuts buy flexibility for tougher long-term targets such as 450 ppm CO₂ stabilization. Portfolio analysis spreads investment across options suited to different future climates.

**Scenarios** (such as the IPCC's Shared Socioeconomic Pathways) are stories about possible futures, not predictions, providing baselines against which policies are compared. A business-as-usual baseline assumes no new policies beyond those already in place.

## Mitigation costs

Economists estimate the cost of mitigation at 1 to 2 percent of global GDP per year, while the benefits of keeping warming under 2°C exceed the costs. The IMF estimates global fossil fuel subsidies exceed $5 trillion per year, so the net cost of climate action is small compared to existing flows in the opposite direction. Early, well-planned action minimizes costs; delaying raises them.

Two main policy tools exist: **carbon taxes** (price certainty, simple, can be regressive without redistribution) and **emissions trading** (quantity certainty, market-based, but price can be volatile). Carbon pricing is the most cost-effective lever, though pricing alone cannot stabilize the climate and must pair with other measures.

**Discount rates** heavily influence results. High rates (5 percent or more) make future damages look small and discourage action; low rates (such as the near-zero pure rate of time preference in the Stern Review) treat future welfare as nearly equal to present welfare and favor strong mitigation. The choice between a descriptive rate (based on market returns, Nordhaus's approach) and a prescriptive rate (based on ethics toward future generations, Stern's approach) is a major driver of disagreement on optimal policy.

## Adaptation costs

Adaptation (sea walls, resilient infrastructure, drought-resistant crops) is cheaper than rebuilding after disasters. Every US$1 billion invested in coastal flood adaptation avoids roughly US$14 billion in damages, and resilient infrastructure in developing countries returns about $4 for each $1 invested. The UN Environment Programme estimated developing-country adaptation costs at about US$215 billion per year through 2030, with figures rising after 2050. A 2023 analysis of national plans put annual needs closer to US$387 billion.

## Who pays

No consensus exists on how to share the burden. Competing principles include equal per-person rights to the atmosphere, basic needs, the polluter-pays principle (two-thirds of historical GHG stock came from industrialized countries), ability to pay, and grandfathering current emissions. The choice is ultimately ethical, not purely economic. Wealthy countries have contributed most emissions while facing smaller impacts, and poor countries face the largest damages with the least capacity to respond, a pattern that deepens global inequality and can push millions into poverty.
