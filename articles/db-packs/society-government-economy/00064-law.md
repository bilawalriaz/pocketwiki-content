# Law

Law is a set of rules created and enforced by social or governmental institutions to regulate behavior. The word *law* comes from Old Norse *lǫg* (plural), meaning "something laid or fixed." Its substance is split into public law (constitutional, administrative, criminal) and private law (contracts, property, torts, commercial law).

## What law is

Jurisprudence, the philosophy of law, asks what law is and what it should be. Hart argued law is a system of rules: primary rules governing conduct and secondary rules addressed to officials (adjudication, change, recognition). Austin called law "the command of a sovereign, backed by the threat of a sanction." Dworkin described law as an interpretive concept used to achieve justice. Raz argued law is authority identifiable through social sources, not morality. Holmes defined law as "the prophecies of what the courts will do in fact." Kelsen's Pure Theory of Law treats each system as grounded in a basic norm (*Grundnorm*) instructing obedience.

Two camps divide these views. Positivists (Bentham, Austin, Kelsen, Raz) hold that real law is separate from morality. Natural lawyers (Aquinas, Rousseau, Grotius) argue law reflects moral and unchangeable laws of nature. Kant held that a moral imperative requires laws to be chosen as if they should hold as universal laws of nature.

## How legal systems differ

**Civil law** treats legislation and custom as primary sources. It derives from Justinian I's codification of Roman law in 529–534 AD, rediscovered in 11th-century Italy. France's Code Civil and Germany's Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch modernized it in the 19th century, influencing Japan and Korea. A central doctrine is the *Rechtsstaat*: everyone, including governments, is subject to the law. Civil law reasoning relies on legal syllogism, applying general rules to particular cases.

**Common law** treats court decisions as law on equal footing with statutes. The doctrine of precedent (*stare decisis*) binds lower courts to higher court decisions. Common law originated in late-12th-century England under Henry II; the Magna Carta (1215) required royal judges to hold courts at fixed places. When common law grew inflexible, the Lord Chancellor developed equity as a parallel system; the two merged in 19th-century England and in the U.S. in 1937. Common law reasoning relies on analogy, especially in the U.S.

**Religious law** is based on religious precepts: Jewish Halakha, Islamic Sharia, Christian canon law. The Quran generates further law through *Qiyas* (analogy), *Ijma* (consensus), and precedent, collected in Sharia and Fiqh. Saudi Arabia recognizes the Quran as its constitution. The Catholic Church has the oldest continuously functioning legal system in the Western world, governed today by the 1983 Code of Canon Law.

**Socialist law** describes communist-state systems like the Soviet Union and the People's Republic of China. It deviates from civil law by subordinating the judiciary to the ruling party; scholars disagree on whether this makes it a separate system. China's 1999 contract code marked a shift away from administrative domination.

## Legal institutions

Industrialized states share core institutions: independent courts, representative parliaments, an accountable executive, the military and police, bureaucratic administration, the legal profession, and civil society. Locke and Montesquieu advocated separation of powers. Most legislatures are bicameral; bills require majority support in both houses.

Weber argued the state holds a monopoly on the legitimate use of force, and a "failed state" cannot implement its policies. He also saw bureaucratic administration, with officials bound by rules and recruited as career experts, as a defining feature of a developed state. A legal profession autonomous enough to invoke an independent judiciary is a corollary of the rule of law; practice is overseen by a bar association or equivalent, and lawyers must hold a recognized qualification.

## Core areas of law

**Constitutional and administrative law** governs state affairs. Constitutional law defines relationships between branches and protects individual rights: individuals may do anything not forbidden, while the state may do only what is authorized. Administrative law lets people hold state bodies accountable; the first specialist administrative court was France's Conseil d'État, established in 1799.

**Criminal law** defines offences and penalties. Most crimes require proof beyond reasonable doubt of both *actus reus* (guilty act) and *mens rea* (guilty mind); strict liability crimes require only the act. The state prosecutes; punishments include imprisonment, fines, supervision, or community service. The International Criminal Court tries people for crimes against humanity, with 111 member states.

**Contract law** concerns enforceable promises (*pacta sunt servanda*: agreements must be kept). Common law requires offer, acceptance, consideration, and intention to create legal relations. Civil law incorporates more mandatory terms, gives courts greater interpretive latitude, and imposes a stronger duty of good faith without requiring consideration. Germany's *Abstraktionsprinzip* separates a seller's personal obligation from the title of property being transferred.

**Torts and delicts** are civil wrongs giving injured parties a right to compensation. The most common is negligence, defined by four principles: a duty of care, a breach of that duty, harm that would not have occurred but for the breach, and the breach as proximate cause. These derive from *Donoghue v Stevenson*, where a manufacturer was held liable for illness caused by a decomposing snail in a ginger beer bottle.

**Property law** governs ownership and possession. Real property is land and what is attached; personal property is everything else. A right *in rem* is a right to a specific piece of property; a right *in personam* allows compensation for a loss. Locke argued lives, liberties, and estates are property because people own their bodies and mix their labor with their surroundings.

**Equity and trusts** developed when English common law became inflexible. Trustees hold and manage property while beneficiaries retain equitable ownership. The Hague Trust Convention of 1985 sets international norms for recognition.

**Corporate law** governs formation, governance, operation, and dissolution of incorporated entities, setting rights and duties of shareholders, directors, officers, and stakeholders.

**Evidence law** sets rules for proving facts in court, deciding what material a judge or jury may consider.

## Intersection with other fields

In economics, Coase argued in *The Problem of Social Cost* (1960) that without transaction costs, people would bargain to the same outcome regardless of court rulings, making liability rules often irrelevant to resource allocation. In sociology, Weber defined the "legal rational form" of domination, built on abstract norms, as developing in parallel with capitalism. Durkheim argued that as society becomes more complex, restitution-focused civil law grows at the expense of criminal law.
