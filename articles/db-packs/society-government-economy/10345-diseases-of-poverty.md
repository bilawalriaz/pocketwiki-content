# Diseases of poverty

Diseases of poverty, also called poverty-related diseases (PRDs), are illnesses that disproportionately strike low-income populations. They include infectious diseases (HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, malaria, diarrheal diseases, respiratory infections) and conditions linked to malnutrition, unsafe water, poor housing, and limited healthcare. The World Health Report (2002) attributes 45% of disease burden in high-poverty countries to PRDs that are preventable or treatable with existing interventions. Together, the infectious PRDs kill roughly 14 million people each year.

## The central mechanism: a feedback loop

Poverty creates the conditions that produce these diseases, and the diseases in turn deepen poverty. Lack of shelter, safe water, nutritious food, sanitation, and health services increases exposure and susceptibility. Sickness then reduces income, drains household savings, removes caregivers from work, and pulls children out of school, transmitting disadvantage across generations. This is the cycle of poverty: the same conditions recur in families and communities for decades.

## The big three infectious diseases

HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis (TB), and malaria are the dominant infectious PRDs, together causing about 10% of global mortality and 18% of disease in poor countries. The geographic concentration is extreme: 95% of HIV cases and 98% of active TB infections are in developing countries, and roughly 90% of malaria deaths occur in Africa. In Africa, over 80% of the world's 300–500 million annual malaria infections occur, and about one million children under five die of malaria each year.

HIV targets T-cells, the immune cells that defend against uncommon infections and cancers, and is managed with antiretroviral drugs. It spreads through sexual contact, contaminated needles, transfusion, and mother-to-child transmission, with a long latent period that allows silent spread. TB, identified by Robert Koch in 1882, causes fever, weight loss, poor appetite, and night sweats. A vaccine was introduced in 1906, yet TB remains the world's leading infectious killer and is rising about 6% per year in sub-Saharan Africa, where it is the top cause of death in people with HIV. Malaria, now limited to warm developing regions, is spread by mosquitoes and disproportionately kills the rural poor and children of mothers with little education.

These three diseases reinforce one another biologically. Malaria raises HIV viral load seven- to tenfold, increasing sexual transmission, and HIV weakens immunity against malaria. Genital schistosomiasis produces lesions that attract the CD4 cells HIV targets, roughly tripling HIV acquisition risk in affected women.

## Why poverty changes the body

Malnutrition operates in a self-reinforcing cycle with infection. Deficiencies in macronutrients (protein, energy) and micronutrients (iron, zinc, vitamin A) weaken immunity, raise susceptibility to HIV, and increase mother-to-child HIV transmission. Vitamin A deficiency and anemia independently raise viral shedding in the birth canal. Meanwhile, infections depress appetite and alter metabolism of carbohydrates, proteins, and fats, worsening nutritional status. Over 35% of sub-Saharan African children under five show signs of malnutrition, and UNICEF reports 3,000 children die daily from contaminated water and poor sanitation.

## Environmental and social drivers

| Factor | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Contaminated water | Spreads E. coli, cholera, hepatitis A, schistosomiasis; 783 million people still rely on unsafe sources |
| Indoor air pollution | Open-fire cooking exposes 2.4 billion people, causing 3.2 million deaths yearly (2020), mostly women and children |
| Poor sanitation | 2.3 billion lack basic sanitation; 432,000 deaths yearly in low- and middle-income countries |
| Inadequate housing | Lead, mold, and rodents cause chronic illness; in Flint, Michigan, lead leaching from pipes caused a public health crisis beginning in 2014 |
| Limited healthcare | About 30% of the global population lacks regular access to medicines, rising to 50% in the poorest parts of Africa and Asia |

Mental health follows the same pattern: 31% of people in poverty report a depression diagnosis versus 15.8% of those not in poverty. Chronic stress raises cortisol, which contributes to hypertension, diabetes, osteoporosis, and infections. The link is bidirectional: poverty worsens mental health, and mental illness reduces earning capacity, deepening poverty.

## Neglected diseases

A subset called neglected tropical diseases receives little research funding despite massive burden. Of 1,393 new drugs developed over 25 years (1975–1999), fewer than 1% targeted these conditions. They include African trypanosomiasis, Chagas disease, leishmaniasis, lymphatic filariasis, dracunculiasis, onchocerciasis, and schistosomiasis. Schistosomiasis alone affects over 200 million people, more than 80% in sub-Saharan Africa. Transmitted by freshwater snails, it damages intestines, bladder, and other organs, and is a co-factor in HIV transmission. Intestinal parasites such as hookworms and roundworms compound malnutrition by consuming blood and nutrients, while activating the immune system in ways that increase HIV susceptibility.

## Noncommunicable diseases

Noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) such as cardiovascular disease, chronic respiratory disease, cancer, and diabetes are no longer confined to wealthy populations. They cause 43 million deaths annually, with 73% in low- and middle-income countries, and 82% of premature NCD deaths (under age 70) now occur in those settings. Poverty drives NCDs through poor diet, limited exercise access, chronic stress, environmental pollutants, and infrequent screening. Obesity clusters in food deserts, areas lacking supermarkets and dominated by processed food, and cancer death rates run 12% higher in the poorest countries. Two out of three cancer deaths globally stem from lifestyle and behavioral risks that are themselves shaped by socioeconomic conditions.

## Why women and girls bear extra risk

Women and girls often face specific exposure pathways: collecting water from contaminated sources, washing clothes in schistosome-infested streams, and receiving more injections during pregnancy and childbirth, sometimes with unsterilized equipment. Female genital schistosomiasis triples HIV risk. When HIV-positive women disclose their status, they frequently face accusations of infidelity, abandonment, or violence, even when their own infection was non-sexual.

## Research and policy

Only about 10% of global health research addresses the diseases that make up 90% of the global disease burden, a gap known as the 10/90 gap. The Millennium Development Goal of halving unsafe-water access was met five years early, in 2010, and the UN declared water access a human right the same year. The WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health recommends three actions: improve daily living conditions, especially for women, girls, and young children; redistribute money, power, and resources; and measure outcomes to guide policy. Low-cost interventions show measurable impact: vitamin A capsules cost about $0.02 each when given twice yearly, iron supplements $0.02–$0.08 per child per week, and deworming treatments $0.02–$0.25 twice a year, with mosquito nets offering cheap malaria prevention.
