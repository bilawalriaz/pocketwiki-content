# Sociology

Sociology is the scientific study of human society: how people interact, form groups, build institutions, and produce culture. It uses empirical methods, both quantitative (surveys, statistics, experiments) and qualitative (ethnography, interviews, archival work), to build and test theories about social order and social change. Subject matter ranges from micro-level face-to-face interaction to macro-level structures like class, law, religion, and the state.

The discipline sits inside the broader social sciences, sharing methods with economics, political science, anthropology, and psychology. Its distinctive claim is that social phenomena have causes and patterns that can be studied scientifically, rather than left to philosophy or common sense.

## Core Questions

Classical traditions converge on a few persistent dichotomies:

- Subjectivity vs. objectivity: an observer who belongs to the society she studies must examine her own assumptions, a practice called reflexivity.
- Structure vs. agency: do structures like class, norms, and institutions determine behaviour, or do individuals make free choices that change those structures? The puzzle of social reproduction, how inequality persists through individual decisions, sits here.
- Synchrony vs. diachrony: survey research captures snapshots of social life, while historical sociology traces change over time.

## Origins and Founders

The word combines Latin *socius* (companion) with Greek *-logia* (study of). Coined in 1780 by Emmanuel-Joseph Sieyès, the discipline is usually dated to Auguste Comte (1798–1857), who proposed treating society scientifically through *positivism* (applying natural-science methods to social phenomena).

Three figures are typically named as principal architects. Émile Durkheim (1858–1917) defined social facts as a distinct empirical object; his statistical study *Suicide* (1897) showed Catholic and Protestant suicide rates differ due to social, not psychological, causes. Karl Marx (1818–1883) saw history shaped by class conflict, rejected positivism, but built a "science of society" focused on economic structures. Max Weber (1864–1920) argued sociology requires *Verstehen* (interpretive understanding of subjective meaning) and developed ideal types (simplified models used for comparison) and multi-dimensional stratification.

Earlier predecessors include Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406), whose *Muqaddimah* analysed social cohesion and conflict. Harriet Martineau is often cited as the first female sociologist.

Institutional milestones: Chicago's Department of Sociology (1892, Albion Small), the American Journal of Sociology (1895), the London School of Economics department (1904), the German Sociological Association (1909), and the Frankfurt School's Institute for Social Research (1923).

## Major Theoretical Traditions

Randall Collins groups classical theory into four:

- Functionalism treats society as an interdependent system whose parts sustain the whole. Associated with Durkheim and Herbert Spencer's organic analogy; dominant in US sociology c. 1881–1915.
- Conflict theory sees society shaped by inequality and group struggle. Marx is the prototype; by the 1980s conflict approaches had largely replaced functionalism in Europe.
- Symbolic interactionism views society as the product of everyday symbolic exchanges. Emerged from the Chicago School of the 1920s–1930s (George Herbert Mead, Charles Cooley), with later contributions from Erving Goffman.
- Utilitarianism / rational choice treats individuals as strategic actors maximising preferences. Revitalised in sociology by James Coleman.

Twentieth-century sociology split between Weber's interpretive tradition and a quantitatively dominant instrumental positivism, often traced to Paul Lazarsfeld's survey methods. Talcott Parsons synthesised European theory into structural-functionalism, which dominated US sociology through the 1950s before declining.

## Research Methodology

Sociology combines qualitative and quantitative designs. Quantitative methods, dominant in the US, use surveys, experiments, and statistics to generalise across populations; "methodology" is often used as a synonym for statistics. Qualitative methods (ethnography, participant observation, in-depth interviews, content analysis) aim for contextual depth. Most studies now *triangulate*, mixing methods.

Since the late 20th century, computational sociology has grown: agent-based modelling (simulating many individual actors to see how macro patterns emerge), text mining, social network analysis, and AI-assisted simulation study complex emergent processes. Social network analysis treats ties between individuals as the basic unit, rather than individuals themselves.

## Subfields and Applications

Modern sociology splits into dozens of subfields, each applying the discipline's tools to a domain. Examples: economic sociology (Granovetter's 1985 "embeddedness" thesis, that economic action is shaped by existing social relations); sociology of education (the 1966 Coleman Report on 150,000 US students showed student background outweighed school resources); sociology of religion (Weber's *Protestant Ethic*, 1905: religious ideas shaped modern capitalism; Durkheim: religion as the source of social solidarity); sociology of stratification (Weber extended Marx by adding status and party/power to economic class; Bourdieu added cultural and symbolic capital, or non-financial assets like credentials and prestige); urban sociology (the Chicago School; Saskia Sassen's "global city"); and sociology of health, family, gender, race, law, environment, and digital media.

Applied sociology feeds policy, education, public administration, business, and social work. Public sociology, associated with Michael Burawoy, addresses audiences beyond academia.
