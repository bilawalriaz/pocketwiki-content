# Poverty

Poverty is the condition of lacking the financial resources needed for a basic standard of living. It arises from intersecting environmental, legal, social, economic, and political forces, and it propagates across generations and regions, shaping health, education, housing, and economic participation.

## Two Core Definitions

Specialists separate poverty into two complementary types.

**Absolute poverty** means income falls below what is needed for basic needs such as food, shelter, and clothing. The World Bank's most widely cited line is **$1.90 per day in 2011 purchasing power parity (PPP)**, an exchange-rate adjustment that lets one dollar buy equivalent goods in different countries. A higher $7.40/day line has been proposed by critics who argue $1.90 understates real deprivation.

**Relative poverty** means income is too low to afford the living standard normal in one's own society, usually defined in OECD and EU contexts as below 60% of median earnings. It tracks social exclusion and inequality rather than survival.

The UN frames poverty more broadly as a denial of choices and opportunities that violates human dignity.

## Headline Numbers

About 10% of the world's population survived on under $1.90/day as of 2019, and conflict-affected regions host more than 40% of the extremely poor. Global extreme poverty fell from 37% in 1990 to 9.6% in 2015, but progress has slowed, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa. China lifted hundreds of millions out through economic reform, while parts of Africa saw both rates and absolute numbers rise. The COVID-19 pandemic reversed some gains; Oxfam estimated around five billion people became poorer between 2020 and its report date.

## Causes and Cycles

Poverty is driven by structural inequalities, weak governance, conflict, and historical legacies including colonialism. Political responses split between **neoliberal** approaches (private investment, market liberalisation) and **socialist** approaches (redistribution). Cultural narratives often blame the poor for their circumstances, yet evidence shows most are trapped by systemic barriers, compounded by dynastic politics and corruption.

Cycles operate at individual, community, and global scales. Poor health limits education, which lowers future income. Discrimination on the basis of gender, race, disability, or caste intensifies vulnerability. Women and children bear disproportionate burdens; girls face early marriage and restricted schooling.

## Impacts

About one-third of global deaths each year are attributable to poverty-related causes, and malnutrition contributes to roughly half of all child mortality. Chronic stress, trauma, and the cognitive load of financial insecurity produce mental health problems. Children in poverty show delayed cognitive development and weaker academic performance, then face higher dropout rates, unsafe schools, and limited parental support.

Housing instability raises exposure to violence and disease; slum conditions often exceed rural hardship. Gentrification displaces vulnerable groups without removing root causes.

## Reduction Strategies

Efforts cluster into two strategies: increasing access to basic needs, and raising personal income.

*Access to basic needs.* Post-Industrial-Revolution fertilisers and irrigation expanded food supply; medical advances cut maternal and child deaths. Universal healthcare lowers costs, and targeted subsidies reach the poor directly, though corruption and debt servicing divert public money. Infrastructure such as roads connects remote areas to markets, and microfranchising distributes goods efficiently. Family-planning education eases population pressure on resources.

*Personal income.* Universal basic income and negative income taxes provide direct cash; transfers improve consumption, schooling, and nutrition. Secure land rights, market access, and remittances boost rural and migrant incomes. Mobile banking and postal savings reach the unbanked, while microcredit shows mixed results because of concerns about high interest rates. Free schooling, vocational training, school feeding, and deworming programmes yield high returns.

## Institutions and Ethics

The UN coordinates global goals through the **Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)**, adopted in 2015, with the first goal being "No Poverty." The World Bank and IMF fund development but attach **structural adjustment conditions** that require fiscal austerity and privatisation. NGOs deliver locally but face coordination problems and donor dependency. Critics call the surrounding infrastructure the "poverty industrial complex," arguing that profit motives can override service quality.

Poverty is widely treated as a human-rights violation, and environmental-justice movements note that the Global South suffers disproportionate climate impacts, with projections warning that millions more may fall below the extreme-poverty line by 2030.

## Open Debates

Whether $1.90/day is too low remains contested, as does whether wealthy nations should track absolute or relative measures. The poverty-reducing effect of neoliberal reform varies by region, and disagreement persists over how much technology and globalisation can substitute for structural change. Cultural values may shape economic behaviour, but opportunity structures explain most outcomes. Cash transfers appear more effective than in-kind aid despite political resistance, and debt relief and land reform have strong advocates but uncertain feasibility.
