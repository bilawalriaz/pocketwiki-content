# History of political thought

Political thought is the long conversation about why people obey governments, what justifies power, and how a just society should be organised. It began independently in several ancient civilisations, travelled through religious synthesis, returned to secular reasoning during the Renaissance and Enlightenment, and then fragmented into the competing modern ideologies of liberalism, conservatism, socialism, and anarchism. Each generation answered the same core question — what gives the state the right to rule — using the tools of its own time.

## Independent beginnings, three traditions

Around 770 BCE in China, the Hundred Schools of Thought produced rival answers to the same problem. **Confucianism** argued for hierarchical but meritocratic rule, with the ruler setting a moral example. **Legalism** rejected moral suasion in favour of strict law and harsh punishment. **Taoism** went further, favouring minimal government or none at all. In India, **Chanakya's *Arthashastra*** (4th century BCE) treated politics as a craft: a manual of statecraft, foreign policy, and espionage that mixed virtue with pragmatic, even ruthless, tactics. In the Greek world, thinkers such as **Plato and Aristotle** (5th–4th centuries BCE) analysed the *polis* (city-state) as the natural setting for the good life. Plato proposed rule by philosopher-kings; Aristotle grounded politics in the observation that humans are social animals who flourish only in community.

## Revelation, reason, and Islamic synthesis

In medieval Europe, political philosophy worked inside Christian theology, trying to harmonise reason with scripture. **Augustine** drew a sharp line between the earthly city and the City of God. **Thomas Aquinas** (13th century) merged Aristotle with Christian doctrine, sorting law into eternal, divine, natural, and human kinds. In the Islamic world, a parallel debate took shape between the **Mutazilites**, who subordinated scripture to reason, and the **Asharites**, who subordinated reason to scripture. **Ibn Khaldun** (14th century) brought historical sociology to bear on the problem, treating government as a necessary evil that prevents worse injustice but is itself prone to decay.

## From Machiavelli to the social contract

The Renaissance broke the theological frame. **Machiavelli** (*The Prince*, c. 1513) deliberately separated politics from Christian morality, advising rulers to prioritise keeping power over being virtuous. The Enlightenment then rebuilt political legitimacy on consent rather than divine right. **Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau** all asked what rational individuals would agree to before forming a state. Locke (1689) used the contract to ground natural rights — life, liberty, property — and to justify resisting governments that violated them. **Rousseau** (*The Social Contract*, 1762) recast the contract as the collective creation of the general will. Critics like **Hume** attacked the contract as historical fiction, while **Adam Smith** analysed the unintended social benefits of self-interest, and **Kant** grounded politics in moral duty and the goal of perpetual peace.

## Modern ideologies and their disagreements

The Industrial Revolution created new categories of conflict. **Marx and Engels** (*The Communist Manifesto*, 1848) argued that capitalism produces class exploitation and would be overthrown by the proletariat, a line later extended by Leninism and Maoism. **Burke** founded conservatism on tradition and gradual reform. **Mill** defended individual liberty, including gender equality. **Proudhon and Kropotkin** argued that legitimate authority does not exist, pushing for stateless societies. In the 20th century, **Rawls** (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971) revived social contract theory for a pluralist age, while **Nozick** defended a minimal libertarian state. Communitarians criticised the liberal focus on isolated individuals, and feminist and postcolonial thinkers exposed the contract tradition's exclusions, naming the "sexual contract" and "racial contract" that shaped who counted as a contracting citizen.
