# Power (political science)

Power is the capacity to direct other people's actions. In political life it usually travels through government authority over a society or state, and it rests on a mix of coercion and quieter institutional influence. What separates power from brute force is legitimacy: authority is power that the surrounding social order treats as rightful.

Power can be wielded by one person, a democratic majority, a narrow elite, or an authoritarian regime. A special case is minoritarianism, in which a minority controls legislative or judicial decision-making, often by leaning on ethnic, religious, or linguistic identity.

## What Power Does to People

Approach/inhibition theory (Keltner and colleagues) holds that having power pushes people toward action, reward-seeking, and optimism, while losing power pulls them toward vigilance and reduced activity. The downside is more risk-taking, more unethical decisions, more stereotyping of subordinates, and less attention to others.

Gene Sharp turned the usual picture on its head: power does not live in rulers, it lives in the obedience of subjects. Pull that obedience away and the ruler has nothing. Sharp's writings are widely credited with shaping the nonviolent overthrow of Slobodan Milošević, the 2011 Arab Spring, and other movements that defeated opponents without armies.

## Frameworks for Sources of Power

Several thinkers have tried to list where power actually comes from.

- **French and Raven (1959)** identified five key sources, including expert power (specialized knowledge the organisation needs), reward power (the ability to grant benefits), and coercive power (the application of negative consequences, including withheld rewards).
- **Gareth Morgan (1986)** proposed additional bases in *Images of Organization*.
- **John Kenneth Galbraith** sorted power into three kinds: condign (based on force), compensatory (based on resources such as money or jobs), and conditioned (based on persuasion that changes what people want). The underlying sources are personality, property, and organisational position.
- **Stewart Clegg's circuits model** adds a spatial layer. Episodic circuits sit inside micro-level interactions, dispositional circuits sit inside macro-level rules, and facilitative circuits sit inside macro-level technology and structure. They meet at *obligatory passage points* where the three scales have to coordinate.
- **Björn Kraus's relational constructivism** draws a line between instructive power, which determines another person's actions and thoughts and can be refused, and destructive power, which simply shrinks someone's opportunities and cannot be refused. The split matters because it lets analysts weigh proportions of power and assign responsibility more honestly.
- **Michel Foucault** described power as something spread through institutions and *apparatuses*, working best when its agents do not see it, and producing *docile bodies* that can be shaped, used, and improved.
- **Antonio Gramsci** used the idea of *cultural hegemony*: ruling classes keep their position through both coercion (the "beast") and consent (the "human face"). The Western European mix of consent and coercion, he argued, is why capitalism there avoided the violent revolutions that hit Russia.

## Resistance and the Limits of Hard Power

Harsh tactics such as punishment and sanctions tend to backfire, generating hostility and conflict, and are generally less effective than soft tactics built on expertise and personal rewards. When a high-status member is publicly reprimanded, disruptive contagion spreads the same behaviour through the group.

Herbert Kelman's conversion theory separates three depths of submission: compliance (superficial obedience), identification (copying the authority), and internalization (adopting the authority's values as one's own). Resistance usually appears when low-power members build a shared identity, and it hardens into revolutionary coalitions when an authority lacks referent power and falls back on coercion. The broader term for oppositional force from below is *counter-power*, a label that re-entered public debate through the global justice movement of the 1990s and Tim Gee's 2011 book *Counterpower*.

A persistent weakness is *power literacy*, the ability to read and understand how power is formed, accumulated, and controlled. It stays low because power carries a "negative moral valence," so the public pays it little attention and the advantage stays concentrated among elites. Open questions remain: whether power is best treated as a thing rulers possess or as a relation that depends on obedience, how Foucault's dispersed model squares with older sovereignty ideas, and how the balance of consent and coercion shifts the chance of revolution.
