# Corporate law

Corporate law (also called company law or enterprise law) is the body of law governing the rights, relations, and conduct of persons, companies, organizations, and businesses. It covers the full life cycle of a corporation: formation, funding, governance, and dissolution. The discipline responds to three recurring opportunism problems: conflicts between managers and shareholders, between controlling and non-controlling shareholders, and between shareholders and their contractual counterparts such as creditors and employees.

## The defining features of a corporation

Academics identify five legal characteristics that distinguish a corporation from looser associations:

- **Separate legal personality**: the corporation is a legal person in its own right, able to sue, be sued, own property, and enter contracts independent of its owners.
- **Limited liability**: a shareholder's personal exposure is capped at the value of their investment; if the company is liquidated, unpaid creditors cannot pursue the shareholder's other assets.
- **Transferable shares**: ownership interests can be bought and sold without dissolving the firm, which lets public companies trade on stock exchanges.
- **Delegated management**: shareholders elect a board of directors, which in turn appoints executives who run day-to-day operations.
- **Shared ownership**: stock can be distributed among many investors, though a single owner is permitted.

A company is not necessarily a corporation; in the United States, "company" is often used interchangeably with "firm" or "business," whereas incorporation specifically produces the legal personality above.

## Origins and history

Recognizable ancestors of the modern company appeared in the 16th century, when European monarchs granted royal charters to trading ventures such as the English East India Company (1600) and the Dutch East India Company (1602). A late benefit of joint-stock arrangements was that a member's stock could not be seized for that member's personal debts.

Two 17th-century speculation collapses, the South Sea Bubble in England and the Tulip Bulb Bubble in the Dutch Republic, set company-law development back in public estimation. England responded with the Bubble Act 1720 (repealed 1825), and the Joint Stock Companies Act 1844 introduced registration-based incorporation. The Limited Liability Act 1855 capped shareholder exposure, and the Joint Stock Companies Act 1856 codified both rules. *Salomon v Salomon & Co.* (1897) confirmed that a one-person company, once incorporated, had rights and liabilities separate from its sole shareholder.

## Constitutional sources

A corporation's governing rules come from two layers: mandatory statutes such as the Delaware General Corporation Law in the United States, the Companies Act 2006 in the United Kingdom, and the Aktiengesetz and GmbH-Gesetz in Germany; and a private constitution, often called articles of association or bylaws, that members can usually tailor. Common-law jurisdictions historically split this into a memorandum of association (external-facing objects and authorised capital) and articles of association (internal procedures), but most have since consolidated them. Shareholders' agreements frequently supplement the constitution in private, since they are not publicly filed.

## Governance and the balance of power

The board of directors manages the company, while shareholders retain fundamental rights: in the UK they may remove directors by simple majority, require a meeting with 10% of shares, and amend the constitution with 75% support. Germany layers a supervisory board over a management board and uses co-determination, where employees elect representatives to the supervisory board. In the United States, Delaware law grants directors substantial autonomy: any constitutional amendment requires a director resolution first, and classified boards make removal harder.

Directors owe strict duties of good faith, care, and skill to the company. They must not let personal interests conflict with their duty, may be forced to disgorge any gain from a hypothetical conflict, and must exercise powers for a proper purpose; issuing shares to defeat a takeover bid is improper. Continuing to trade while insolvent can make directors personally liable for resulting losses.

## Piercing the corporate veil

Separate legal personality can produce harsh results: a sole shareholder who insured timber in his own name after transferring it to his company could not claim when it burned, because he no longer had an insurable interest, and victims of an American subsidiary could not sue its English parent in tort. Courts look past the corporate form only in narrow circumstances: where the company is a façade, is the agent of its controllers, has been used to perpetrate fraud, or where statute specifically imposes liability, for example for environmental violations.

## Capacity and human agency

Because a company is an artificial person, it can act only through human agents, principally the board. Historically, companies were confined to their stated objects, with acts beyond them declared *ultra vires* and void; most jurisdictions have widened capacity to match that of a natural person. Third parties dealing with the company can rely on the *ostensible authority* of its agents, codified from *Royal British Bank v Turquand* into modern statutes.

## Corporate finance

Companies raise capital through equity (issuing shares), debt (loans and bonds), or retained profits. Interest on debt is tax-deductible while dividends are not, which biases financing toward debt. Shares carry voting, dividend, and capital-return rights; companies may issue multiple classes with different privileges. Most jurisdictions regulate minimum capital, restrict distributions that would leave the firm undercapitalised, and often prohibit a company from financially assisting the purchase of its own shares.

## Insolvency and dissolution

A corporation may end through compulsory liquidation (driven by creditors when debts go unpaid), voluntary liquidation (members decide to wind up, sometimes on just and equitable grounds), merger or acquisition, or dissolution following criminal misconduct. Where rescue is possible, UK insolvency law first attempts administration; otherwise a liquidator gathers assets, pays creditors, and distributes any surplus to members.

Insider trading, the buying or selling of securities while in possession of material non-public information, is prohibited in most jurisdictions because it raises the cost of capital. Trades by officers and significant shareholders (in the US, beneficial owners of 10% or more) must be reported, and disclosed trades are widely studied for signals they may convey.

## Variations by jurisdiction

Most publicly traded US companies incorporate in Delaware because of favourable corporate tax treatment, managerial flexibility under the DGCL, and the specialised Delaware Court of Chancery. The United Kingdom was the first country to draft modern corporation statutes and remains influential across the Commonwealth. Germany, the European Union (with its Societas Europaea form), and other jurisdictions each operate distinct company laws, though the five defining features of the corporation remain broadly comparable across all of them.
