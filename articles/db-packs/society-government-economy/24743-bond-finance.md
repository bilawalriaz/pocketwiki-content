# Bond (finance)

A bond is a security in which the issuer (the borrower) promises to pay the holder (the lender) a stream of cash flows, almost always a fixed interest payment called a coupon at regular intervals, plus the full principal at a stated maturity date. A bond is a formalised loan or IOU, traded as a security.

The contrast with equity is the first thing to fix. A shareholder owns part of the company; a bondholder is a creditor. In bankruptcy, bondholders are paid ahead of shareholders but behind secured creditors such as banks. Bonds have a defined term, after which the issuer redeems them, whereas shares typically have no maturity. Perpetuities, or irredeemable bonds, are the exception and never mature.

Cash flow varies, which produces different bond types. A fixed-rate bond pays a constant coupon, usually semi-annually or annually. A floating-rate note ties the coupon to a money-market reference rate, historically LIBOR and now SOFR. A zero-coupon bond pays no periodic interest but is sold at a discount to par, with the full principal returned at maturity. An inflation-indexed bond adjusts both principal and coupon to consumer prices; the UK issued the first sovereign inflation-linked gilts in the 1980s, and US examples are TIPS and I-bonds.

The principal is the nominal, par, or face amount on which interest is calculated and which is repaid at maturity, unless the bond specifies a different redemption amount. Maturity is the date that ends the issuer's obligations. Most bonds mature within 30 years. The US Treasury divides its issues into bills (under one year), notes (1–10), bonds (10–30), and perpetuals, and does not currently issue beyond 30 years, which limits reference pricing for very long corporate issues.

The yield is the return an investor earns. Current yield is the annual coupon divided by today's market price. Yield to maturity (YTM) is the more complete measure: the single discount rate at which the bond's price equals the present value of every remaining coupon plus the principal at maturity. YTM assumes the investor reinvests every coupon at the YTM itself; realised returns can differ, as Annette Thau and Frank Fabozzi have noted.

Market price is the present value of those future cash flows discounted at the prevailing yield for comparable bonds. Yields and prices move in opposite directions: when market interest rates rise, existing fixed-coupon bond prices fall, and vice versa. Prices are quoted as a percentage of par, with 100 meaning "at par". The clean price excludes accrued interest since the last coupon; the dirty price includes it. As a bond approaches maturity, assuming no default, its price converges to par, an effect called pull to par.

Credit quality is the probability the issuer will pay on time. Rating agencies grade this risk; bonds rated below investment grade are called high-yield or junk bonds and must offer higher yields to compensate. Bonds are also ranked by seniority: in default, senior bonds are repaid before subordinated ones, and asset-backed issues are sliced into tranches that are paid in order.

Several optional features change the basic picture. A callable bond lets the issuer redeem early, usually at par, which hurts holders when interest rates fall because the holder must reinvest at lower rates. A putable bond lets the holder force early repayment. A convertible bond can be exchanged for a fixed number of the issuer's shares, blending debt and equity. Indentures and covenants set the issuer's obligations and the bondholders' rights, and amendments usually require a super-majority vote of holders.

Bonds are commonly identified by a 12-character ISIN and issued either through underwriting, where a bank syndicate buys the issue and resells it, or at auction, the method most governments use. They trade mainly in decentralised over-the-counter dealer markets rather than on a central exchange. Institutions such as central banks, pension funds, insurance companies, and banks are the dominant holders, since their liabilities are predictable and bond cash flows can be matched to them. Retail investors usually buy through funds.

The principal risks are interest-rate risk (price falls when rates rise), credit risk (a downgrade or default reduces value), reinvestment risk (callable bonds force reinvestment at lower rates), inflation risk, and exchange-rate risk for foreign-currency bonds. Quantifying interest-rate risk leads to the measure called duration. After WorldCom's 2004 Chapter 11 bankruptcy, its bondholders recovered 35.7 cents on the dollar, illustrating that "safer than equity" is relative, not absolute.
