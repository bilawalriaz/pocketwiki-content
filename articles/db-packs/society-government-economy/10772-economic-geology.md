# Economic geology

Economic geology is a subdiscipline of the geosciences that studies earth materials of economic and industrial value. These materials include precious and base metals, nonmetallic minerals, and construction-grade stone. Lindgren (1933) defined it as "the application of geology": the scientific study of the Earth's mineral raw material sources and the practical application of that knowledge.

The field draws on techniques from geochemistry, mineralogy, geophysics, petrology, paleontology, and structural geology to understand, describe, and exploit ore deposits. Economic geology is practiced by geologists and is also of interest to engineers, environmental scientists, and conservationists because mining affects society, the economy, and the environment.

## Purpose of study

The central purpose is to understand ore genesis, the crustal processes that form and concentrate ore minerals into economically viable quantities, and ore localization, the reason particular deposits occur where they do. Associated minerals are studied because they reveal the conditions under which a deposit formed and guide exploration for similar deposits.

Metals, minerals, and other geologic commodities are non-renewable on human timescales. Scarcity has historically driven innovation, leading to replacement commodities when a material becomes too expensive. Despite this, the fixed stock of most mineral commodities in the Earth's crust is large. At current rates of consumption, known copper resources would last more than 100 million years. Economic geologists continue to expand and refine known mineral resources through exploration.

## Mineral resources

Mineral resources are concentrations of minerals significant for current and future societal needs. Ore is the subset of mineralization that is economically and technically feasible to extract. Not all mineralization qualifies; geology, grade, geometry, and market conditions all filter what becomes ore. The economic categories, from least to most developed, are:

- Mineral occurrences or prospects: geological features of interest but not necessarily of economic value.
- Mineral resources: deposits that are potentially or actually economically and technically feasible, including sub-economic material.
- Ore reserves: the fraction of mineral resources that is confirmed to be economically and technically feasible to extract under current conditions.

## Ore

Ore deposits are studied through ore genesis, which examines the crustal processes that concentrate ore minerals into economically viable quantities. Studying metallic ore deposits uses structural geology, geochemistry, metamorphism and its processes, and metasomatism, the chemical replacement of one mineral assemblage by another driven by fluid flow.

Ore bodies are delineated by mineral exploration, which combines geochemical prospecting, drilling, and resource estimation through geostatistics. The aim is mining.

## Coal and petroleum

Coal and petroleum are studied through petroleum geology, in which sedimentology is the primary tool. Sedimentology, the study of sediments and sedimentary rocks, is essential for delineating economic reserves of these energy resources, because their occurrence depends on depositional environment, burial history, and the geometry of sedimentary basins.

## Related fields

Mineral economics, mineral resource classification, ore genesis, and valuation of mining projects all connect to economic geology. The discipline sits at the boundary between pure earth science and extractive industry, applying geological knowledge to the discovery and exploitation of finite mineral resources.
