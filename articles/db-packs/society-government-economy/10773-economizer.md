# Economizer

An economizer is a heat exchanger that captures heat from a stream that would otherwise be wasted and uses it to preheat something that will later need heating. The name fits because the device saves fuel. The same idea appears in Stirling engines, boilers, power plants, HVAC systems, and refrigeration, and in every case the trick is the same: move heat from a place that would lose it to a place that would otherwise have to be paid for.

## Stirling engines

Robert Stirling's 1816 "Economiser," later renamed the **regenerator**, was the first device called an economizer. As air inside a Stirling engine moved from the hot side to the cold side, it passed through a porous matrix that stored part of its heat. When the air returned to the hot side, the matrix gave that heat back. The regenerator raised efficiency enough to make Stirling engines commercially useful in some applications, and every air engine now called a Stirling still has one.

## Boilers and power plants

In a boiler, an economizer recovers enthalpy from exhaust flue gas. Burning fuel heats combustion gases to very high temperatures; the gases boil and superheat water, then leave the stack still hot. An economizer sits in that exhaust path and transfers the leftover heat to the incoming feedwater before it enters the steam drum. Pulling feedwater up toward boiling temperature with waste gas shrinks the load on the burner, because heating water to boiling absorbs a large share of a boiler's fuel.

In a coal-fired power plant the economizer is often called a **feedwater heater**. In a combined-cycle plant it is the first stage of the heat recovery steam generator (HRSG), sitting before the evaporator and superheater. It also prevents liquid water that is too cold from flooding the boiler at its design flow rate.

The arrangement is the mirror image of a fire-tube boiler. A fire-tube boiler has hot gases inside tubes surrounded by water; an economizer has water inside tubes surrounded by hot gases, with fins on the gas side to increase the surface area. Edward Green's 1845 patent for this "Green's economiser" introduced the layout, and its defining feature was a mechanical scraper that kept soot off the tubes. Green's design spread to nearly every stationary steam engine within decades.

Two practical limits apply. First, the gases can only be cooled to roughly the feedwater inlet temperature, because otherwise the economizer would be transferring heat from a cooler stream to a warmer one. Second, cooling the gas too far can condense acidic combustion products and corrode the tubes; material choice (aluminium-stainless alloys are common) and control of excess air both manage this. A condensing economizer deliberately drops the gas below the dew point and recovers both sensible and latent heat, lifting overall boiler efficiency from roughly 80% historically to above 95% in modern condensing units.

## HVAC

In a building's heating and cooling system, an **air-side economizer** is a set of dampers that brings in cool outside air instead of recirculating warm indoor air. When the outdoor air is both cool and dry enough that its enthalpy is acceptable, no mechanical cooling is needed; this mode is called **free cooling**. Air-side economizers save energy in cold and temperate climates and can improve indoor air quality, but they are usually unsuitable in hot humid climates because the outdoor air carries too much moisture.

A **water-side economizer** does the same job with water. When the wet-bulb temperature is low enough, water cooled by a cooling tower or dry cooler is sent through the building's cooling coils directly, bypassing the chiller. The arrangement is historically called the "strainer cycle," though it is not a true thermodynamic cycle. Modern systems usually insert a plate-and-frame heat exchanger between the tower loop and the building loop to avoid fouling the building's cooling coils.

## Refrigeration

In refrigeration, "economizer" means something different: a design that lets a compressor do part of its work at higher pressure and temperature, where it is more efficient. A gas compressor's power draw scales with both the ratio and the difference between its suction and discharge pressures. In a low-temperature system like a walk-in freezer held at −20 °F (−29 °C), the evaporator may sit near −35 °F (−37 °C), and the compressor is forced to lift pressure from a very cold, very low-density suction, which it handles inefficiently.

An economizer splits the compression. A portion of refrigerant is drawn from the condensed liquid line (often around 60 °F / 16 °C, far warmer than the evaporator), throttled, and flashed into gas that is fed to an **interstage** port of a two-stage or screw compressor. That extra gas is compressed only from the interstage pressure up to discharge, not all the way from suction, so each stage runs at a smaller pressure ratio where it works more efficiently. The result is either more cooling from the same compressor or the same cooling with less power.

Two hardware variants exist. A two-stage booster with a **subcooling heat exchanger (SHX)** uses the interstage refrigerant to cool the main liquid line. A flash economizer uses a flash tank instead of a heat exchanger: refrigerant that flashes to gas leaves the liquid colder, and that flash gas is fed to the economizer port of a screw compressor. Manufacturers such as Refcomp, Mycom, Bitzer, and York build single-screw machines that combine both compression stages into one body, with a main suction port and an interstage port.

A simpler form of economizer is **subcooling the liquid line** by any other means, such as a heat exchanger that uses the cold liquid to preheat domestic hot water. Internal heat exchangers also subcool the high-pressure liquid using the cold suction gas leaving the evaporator. That protects the compressor from liquid slugs and shifts more of the heat exchange into the phase-change region, where vapor-compression cycles move the most energy.

A walk-in cooler can act as a refrigeration economizer in a more literal way as well. When outside air is at least about 5 °F cooler than the air inside the cooler, ducting outside air in and warm air out provides free cooling that replaces compressor work, provided the temperature difference is large enough to overcome the box's heat load.
