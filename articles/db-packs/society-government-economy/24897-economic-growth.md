# Economic growth

Economic growth is an increase in the quantity and quality of goods and services a society produces, measured as the rise in inflation-adjusted output (real GDP) over time. The standard rate is the real GDP per capita growth rate, since it strips out population changes and price distortion. Economists distinguish intensive growth (more output per worker) from extensive growth (more output because more workers or inputs exist). Productivity, defined as output per unit of labor, capital, energy, or materials, has historically been the dominant driver of per-capita growth. MIT's Robert Solow estimated that technological progress accounted for about 80% of the long-term rise in U.S. per-capita income, with capital investment explaining the rest.

## Escape from the Malthusian trap

For most of human history, technological progress raised output but also raised population, leaving per-capita income near subsistence. Food supply and other resources capped living standards, a state known as the Malthusian trap. The Industrial Revolution broke this pattern by delivering productivity growth faster than population growth. Industrialising countries then passed through a demographic transition: birth rates fell, workforces shifted from agriculture to manufacturing and then services, and education expanded. In the U.S., about 60% of consumer spending in 2013 went to goods and services that did not exist in 1869, showing how much modern growth rests on new products rather than more of the old ones.

## Determinants

Growth depends on four factors that multiply together: labor productivity, hours worked, the share of the working-age population that is employed, and the share of working-age people in the total population. Capital accumulation (more machines, structures, equipment per worker) raises output but is subject to diminishing returns: each extra unit of capital adds less output than the last, and depreciation eats into the stock. Once the share of GDP devoted to investment just offsets depreciation, the economy reaches a steady state where capital per worker stops rising. That is why, in the long run, only technological progress can keep raising output per worker.

Human capital, the skills of the workforce built through schooling, on-the-job training, and health, matters as much as physical capital. Eric Hanushek and Dennis Kimko showed that student math and science scores correlate strongly with growth. Joerg Baten and Jan Luiten van Zanden used book production per capita as a proxy for literacy and found that 18th-century human-capital levels predicted which countries industrialised in the 19th. Health is a complement: longer life expectancy raises the return on human-capital investment.

Political institutions shape growth by altering incentives. Secure property rights, the rule of law, and constraints on executive power, as after England's Glorious Revolution of 1688, are associated with stronger fiscal capacity and investment. Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, and James Robinson argued that colonial powers built extractive institutions where disease made settlement impossible, and inclusive institutions where they could stay; these differences persisted after independence.

## Theories

Adam Smith in *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) named division of labour and capital accumulation, limited by the size of the market, as the engines of growth.

The Malthusian theory predicts that population growth absorbs technological gains, leaving per-capita income flat, a prediction that holds across 1–1500 CE but breaks afterwards.

The Solow–Swan model (Robert Solow, Trevor Swan, 1956) added diminishing returns to capital. In the long run, output per worker grows only at the rate of technological progress, which the model treats as exogenous (determined outside the model). The model predicts conditional convergence: poorer countries with similar investment rates and access to technology should catch up to richer ones.

Endogenous growth theory (Paul Romer, Robert Lucas, 1980s) tried to explain technological progress from within the model by adding human capital and knowledge as inputs that do not suffer diminishing returns. The Schumpeterian branch, advanced by Philippe Aghion and Peter Howitt, frames growth as creative destruction: new products displace old ones, generating temporary monopoly profits for innovators.

Unified growth theory (Oded Galor) stitches these together into a single historical path: the Malthusian epoch, escape into sustained growth through the demographic transition, and the divergence of countries caused by differences in the timing of that transition.

## Inequality

Classical economists treated inequality as growth-promoting because the wealthy save more. Modern theory, starting with Galor and Zeira (1993), argues the opposite once credit markets are imperfect: inequality blocks human-capital investment by the poor and slows growth. Empirical work by the IMF (Andrew Berg and Jonathan Ostry) finds that lower net inequality is robustly correlated with faster, more durable growth. Brückner and Lederman confirmed a Galor-Zeira prediction: a one-point rise in the Gini coefficient raises income per capita by 2.3% in the poorest quartile of countries but cuts it by 5.3% in the richest. In *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2013), Thomas Piketty argued that when the rate of return on capital (r) exceeds the growth rate of the economy (g), wealth concentration rises mechanically.

## Long-run effects

Growth compounds. The rule of 72 says any rate doubles income in 72/rate years, so a 2% rate doubles GDP in 36 years and an 8% rate in nine. The U.S. grew at 1.80% per year from 1870 to 2008 while the U.K. grew at 1.47%; that 0.33-point gap turned a 20% U.S. deficit into a 30% U.S. lead. The U.S. productivity slowdown after 1973 was partially reversed in 1996–2004 (the Moore's-law era), then returned to lower rates. South Korea's per-capita GDP, lower than Ghana's in 1957, was 17 times Ghana's by 2008.

## Costs and limits

Growth correlates closely with carbon dioxide emissions and resource use. A 2019 statement signed by 11,000 scientists warned that economic growth drives excessive extraction and ecosystem overexploitation, and called for shifting goals from GDP to sustainability and well-being. In 2021, Partha Dasgupta's UK Treasury review concluded that humanity's demand exceeds 1.6 Earths' biocapacity. Earlier depletion fears (Malthus 1798, the 1980 Simon–Ehrlich wager) have so far been averted by substitution and technology, though ore grades for copper have fallen sharply and extraction costs for shale gas and offshore oil have risen. Whether innovation can continue to substitute for scarce resources, or whether biological and climatic limits will eventually bind, is the central open question of growth economics.
