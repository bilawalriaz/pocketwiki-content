# Politics

Politics is the activity of deciding how an organised society handles its shared affairs, most often through a government. The word comes from the Greek *polis* ("city-state"); Aristotle's *Politiká* meant "affairs of the cities," and the English singular "politic" was first recorded in 1430. Politics operates at every scale, from clans to sovereign states to the international system, and its academic study is political science, which divides into comparative politics, international relations, and political philosophy.

## How politics is understood

Definitions split on three axes. On scope, Adrian Leftwich distinguished an *extensive* view, which sees politics in every human social relation, from a *limited* view that restricts it to governance. A feminist perspective argues the household is political too, captured in "the personal is political." On ethics versus power, *political moralism*, in the tradition of Hannah Arendt, treats politics as a normative function settled "through words and persuasion and not through violence," while *political realism*, in Machiavelli, Hobbes, and Lasswell, treats it as power driven by the desire to rule. On conflict versus cooperation, agonism holds politics is essentially conflict (Schmitt's friend/foe distinction); Michael Laver offered a middle view: politics is "the characteristic blend of conflict and co-operation. Pure conflict is war. Pure co-operation is true love."

## How states arose

Early bands and tribes had no centralised authority, making them *stateless societies* ranging from egalitarian bands to stratified chiefdoms. Around 3000 BC, the Uruk-period Sumerians and Predynastic Egyptians built the first states with defined borders, replacing the open frontiers of earlier settlements.

State formation has competing theories. *Voluntary theories* hold that states emerged from shared rational interest, often tied to agriculture. The *hydraulic hypothesis* holds that large-scale irrigation required central coordination. *Conflict theories* argue that states arose when one group oppressed another, with warfare sometimes decisive. Egyptians, Romans, and Greeks were the first to analyse institutions rationally; earlier states justified themselves through religious myth.

The Peace of Westphalia (1648) began the modern international system, in which external powers avoid interference in domestic affairs, a *Westphalian* principle Emer de Vattel formalised in the mid-18th century. The 19th-century rise of nationalism reinforced it by linking legitimate states to nations, and the nation-state spread through state-mandated education, mass literacy, and mass media. Germany and Italy unified partly through nationalist campaigns, the German case preceded by the Zollverein customs union. After WWI, Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points promoted national self-determination, dissolving the Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman Empires; decolonisation later created new nation states across the Third World. In the 20th century, *political globalisation* advanced through intergovernmental organisations, from the League of Nations to the United Nations, with regional blocs such as the European Union, ASEAN, the African Union, BRICS, and Mercosur, and institutions like the IMF, WTO, and International Criminal Court governing cross-border issues.

## How political systems work

David Easton defined a political system as "the interactions through which values are authoritatively allocated for a society." Governments are typically monarchies or republics (presidential, semi-presidential, or parliamentary); by who rules, regimes are democracies (many), oligarchies (few), or autocracies (one); by vertical integration, confederations, federations, or unitary states.

A sovereign state, in Max Weber's definition, holds a monopoly on violence within its territory; the Montevideo Convention adds a defined territory, permanent population, government, and capacity for international relations. Constitutions are written documents that limit government power; England's Civil War-era attempt was abandoned after the Restoration, but written constitutions were later taken up by the American colonies, post-revolutionary France, and the rest of Europe. *Political culture*, in Lucian Pye's phrase, is "the set of attitudes, beliefs, and sentiments which give order and meaning to a political process"; *postmaterialism* describes cultures oriented toward human rights and environmentalism rather than economic security.

## Levels and parties

Macropolitics covers whole systems and their interactions, from *global politics* across borders to domestic politics carried out by state bureaucracy. *International relations* ranges from diplomacy to war; superpowers shape a balance of power that defines world order. Mesopolitics covers intermediary structures, especially political parties, which pursue power through campaigns, education, or protest around an ideology and a written platform. *Duverger's law* holds that first-past-the-post systems tend toward two-party systems, while proportional representation tends toward multiparty ones.

## Political values

Democracy is a system whose outcomes depend on participants' actions, with inherent uncertainty. *Aggregative democracy* collects citizen preferences mainly through voting; Joseph Schumpeter doubted citizens can truly "rule," and Robert Dahl's *polyarchy* requires free elections that select representatives, though poverty can block full democracy. *Deliberative democracy* requires authentic deliberation free from unequal power before majority voting. Equality means equal social status, including human rights, dignity, and access to social goods, with no legally enforced class, caste, or identity-based discrimination.

The left–right spectrum dates from the French Revolution, when republicans sat on the left and monarchists on the right. The left is broadly progressivist: the far-left supports revolutionary change (Communism, Marxism), the centre-left favours reform (social democracy). The right is conservative; the far-right is reactionary (Fascism, Nazism). Political freedom has two sides: *negative liberty*, freedom from coercion, and *positive liberty*, the absence of disabling conditions and the presence of enabling ones. Authoritarianism subordinates individual rights to group goals; libertarianism treats the individual as sovereign, with anarchists seeking total abolition of the state. Classical liberalism, in Locke and Adam Smith, stresses individual freedom, property rights, free markets, and limited government.
