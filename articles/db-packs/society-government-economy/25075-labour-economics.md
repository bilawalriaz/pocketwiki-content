# Labour economics

Labour economics studies the markets where workers supply effort and firms hire them as an input to production. Wages and employment are set by supply and demand, but labour markets differ from goods markets because of labour laws, persistent unemployment, and persistent compensating differentials between similar workers.

## Measuring the labour market

The labour force (LF) is everyone of working age who is employed or actively job-hunting. The labour force participation rate is LF divided by the adult civilian noninstitutional population. The unemployment rate is the unemployed divided by the labour force. Stock variables measure a quantity at a point in time, such as the employment level or unfilled vacancies; flow variables measure quantities over time. Unemployment changes through inflows into job-search minus outflows into jobs or out of the labour force.

## Types of unemployment

Economists split unemployment into natural and cyclical. Natural unemployment has three components:

- Frictional: time spent matching workers to suitable jobs. Internet job boards have reduced this cost.
- Structural: a mismatch between workers' skills or location and available jobs.
- Seasonal: predictable swings in demand, such as post-holiday retail.

The natural rate is frictional plus structural, the floor a stable economy reaches even in good times. Estimates range from 1% to 5% and vary across countries and periods; some economists associate the natural rate with non-accelerating inflation.

Cyclical, or demand-deficient, unemployment is the unemployment above the natural rate caused by insufficient aggregate demand during recessions. Aggregate expenditure equals consumption plus investment plus government spending plus exports, so each channel is a potential policy lever.

## Labour supply: the leisure–income trade-off

Workers maximise utility by trading income against leisure, given a fixed time budget. With w as the hourly wage, k as total hours, L as hours worked, π as non-labour income, and A as leisure, the problem is to maximise U(wL + π, A) subject to L + A ≤ k. The optimum is where the wage equals the marginal rate of substitution of leisure for income.

A wage rise pivots the budget constraint outward. The income effect raises demand for leisure, a normal good; the substitution effect makes leisure more expensive in foregone wages and reduces it. When the substitution effect dominates, hours worked rise with wages; once the income effect takes over at higher wages, the labour supply curve bends backward. Other variables that shift supply include taxation, welfare, and work environment.

## Labour demand: marginal revenue product

A firm's labour demand rests on the marginal revenue product (MRP), the extra revenue from hiring one more worker. With a competitive goods market, MRP equals the output price times the marginal physical product of labour. Firms hire up to the point where MRP equals the marginal cost of labour, which under perfect competition equals the wage. Labour demand is a derived demand, valued because it produces goods, not for its own sake.

Diminishing returns, meaning the marginal product of labour falls as more workers are added, gives the demand curve its downward slope. Greater physical or human capital raises MRP and therefore wages.

Wage differences across occupations reflect these mechanics. A doctor earns more than a port cleaner because the doctor's MRP is higher, the training required is costly and long, and the supply of doctors is therefore less elastic, while demand for medical care is inelastic because it is a necessity.

## Imperfect competition: monopsony and oligopsony

Real labour markets often deviate from perfect competition. A monopsony is a market with a single employer; an oligopsony has a few employers with disproportionate wage-setting power. Both concentrated structures produce lower wages and lower employment than the competitive benchmark, and can lead to poverty, underinvestment in human capital, regional inequality, and weak innovation.

## Asymmetric information and search

Employers cannot perfectly observe worker effort or ability. Moral hazard arises when workers shirk because monitoring is costly; the firm's response can be to hire more workers at lower effort, pushing down average productivity. Stock options and flexible contracts are partial solutions. Adverse selection arises when a firm pays an average wage, driving away high-ability workers and attracting low-ability ones, sometimes causing the market to collapse. Signalling, associated with Michael Spence, uses observable traits such as education to convey ability, though the signal may not reflect true productivity.

Search-and-matching models developed during the 1990–2010 period treat how workers and firms find each other as a dynamic process with frictions; this work contributed to the 2010 Nobel Prize in Economics.

## Discrimination and inequality

Inequality in labour economics refers to unequal earnings across households, often measured with the Gini coefficient. Inequality has generally risen as demand for skilled workers has outpaced supply, technology has complemented skilled labour, union power has weakened, real minimum wages have fallen, and tax cuts have favoured high earners.

Discrimination is the pay difference attributable to demographic characteristics such as gender or race, independent of productivity. The Oaxaca decomposition splits observed wage gaps between groups into a component due to differences in skills and a component due to differences in returns to those skills, with the latter interpreted as discrimination. Gary Becker's taste models describe employer discrimination, where prejudiced employers avoid minority hires; employee discrimination, which segregates the workforce because prejudiced workers demand a premium to work alongside those they dislike; and customer discrimination, where employers fear prejudiced customers and adjust hiring accordingly.
