# Atlantic slave trade

The Atlantic slave trade was the forced transport of roughly 12.75 million enslaved Africans to the Americas by European and New World merchants between about 1440 and the 1860s. It killed an estimated 1.5–2 million captives on the Middle Passage, the transatlantic sea voyage from Africa to the Americas, and supplied the labour that made New World plantation economies possible. Its most distinctive feature was treating human beings as chattel property (movable possessions that could be bought and sold) with status inherited by their children.

## Why it began

Three pressures converged. Portuguese maritime expansion after the 1440s reached the West African coast, originally seeking gold and a sea route to Asia. Atlantic island and later American plantation agriculture needed enormous labour, and indigenous populations had collapsed from disease and overwork while European indentured servants were too few and too mobile to fill the gap. African slavery already existed through Trans-Saharan, Red Sea, and Indian Ocean networks, so European demand expanded an existing market.

In 1452, Pope Nicholas V issued *Dum Diversas*, granting Portugal the right to enslave non-Christians. The 1494 Treaty of Tordesillas divided the Atlantic between Spain and Portugal; Spain, excluded from African ports, bought captives through monopoly contracts called *asiento* (a license to supply a fixed quota of enslaved people to Spanish colonies). Early European coastal raiding gave way to commercial purchase from African intermediaries after African naval resistance made raiding costly.

## The supply side

Captives came overwhelmingly from endemic warfare between African states. Rulers sold war prisoners, usually from other ethnic groups, for firearms, textiles, alcohol, and gold. Kingdoms such as Dahomey, Oyo, and Asante militarised to supply the trade; Dahomey made it a state monopoly and built fortified holding pens (*barracoons*) and roads to move captives to the coast. Coastal kingdoms such as the Loango states barred European forts entirely. On the Gold Coast, the *cassare* system of intermarriage between European traders and African elite women bound the two sides commercially.

Walter Rodney argued the trade was a structurally unequal exchange that underdeveloped Africa. John Thornton countered that African manufacturing competed with pre-industrial Europe and that elites participated voluntarily for political gain. The trade plainly gave some African states short-term power, but it also fed a gun-slave cycle: rulers bought firearms by selling people, a feedback loop that reshaped African politics for centuries.

## The Middle Passage and shipping

The classic "triangular trade" pattern, in which European manufactures went to Africa, enslaved Africans to the Americas, and American sugar, cotton, tobacco, and coffee back to Europe, was common early on. Direct two-leg voyages from American ports such as Rio de Janeiro, Salvador, Recife, and Havana soon dominated total volume. Middle Passage mortality averaged 10–20%, declining as voyages shortened from about 2.5 months in the 18th century to roughly two months by the 19th. Crews died at similar or higher rates from disease, flogging, and revolt. Roughly one voyage in ten saw a shipboard rebellion; captains used shackles, heavy armament, netting, and a force-feeding tool called the *speculum oris* to suppress resistance.

## How slavery became racial and hereditary

Enslaved Africans were defined as chattel under the legal principle *partus sequitur ventrem* (the child follows the mother), making the status permanent and hereditary and creating a racial caste rather than the older European pattern of time-limited servitude. Iberian colonies used *limpieza de sangre* (blood purity) statutes to racialize Africans and Native Americans; British and French slave codes stripped legal personhood outright. Planters forced reproduction, in Brazil by promising freedom after a set number of births, in the U.S. South through coercive "breeding" practices. New arrivals were sent to Caribbean "seasoning camps," where about a third died within two to three years from dysentery, brutality, and deliberate identity erasure. In the U.S. South, enslaved life expectancy was 21–22 years, with child mortality about double that of white children.

## Abolition and suppression

Black former captives such as Olaudah Equiano and Ottobah Cugoano led moral campaigns and petitioned Parliament; British Quakers and Evangelicals, most famously William Wilberforce, drove the legislative fight. Denmark banned the trade in 1792 (effective 1803), the first nation to do so. Britain followed in 1807, declaring slaving piracy, and in 1808 established the West Africa Squadron, a Royal Navy patrol that between 1808 and 1867 seized 1,596 ships and freed about 175,000 Africans. The United States banned imports in 1808 but kept a massive internal trade. Brazil, under British pressure, passed an ineffective ban in 1831 and an enforced one in 1850 (the Eusébio de Queirós Law). The last documented transatlantic voyage reached Cuba in 1867; Cuban slavery ended in 1886 and Brazilian slavery in 1888.

## Economic and demographic effects

Eric Williams argued in *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944) that slave-trade profits financed Britain's Industrial Revolution. Later economists, including David Richardson and Stanley Engerman, calculated the trade at under 1–5% of British national investment and pointed to sugar-plantation wealth, not the trade itself, as the main driver. Seymour Drescher argued the trade remained profitable to the end, so abolition was driven by moral reform, not economic decline. Patrick Manning estimates roughly 12 million entered the Atlantic trade, about 4 million died in Africa after capture, around 6 million were absorbed by Arab and Indian Ocean markets, and about 8 million were retained within Africa. Africa's share of world population fell from about 18% in 1600 to 6% in 1900, with severe gender imbalances along the coast.

## Legacies

Enslaved Africans built Creole cultures, blended African, European, and American languages and societies, such as Gullah in the Sea Islands, Haitian Kreyòl, and Papiamento, sometimes using them as coded languages of resistance during the Haitian Revolution. Freed Africans and African Americans founded Sierra Leone in 1787 and Liberia in 1820, projects that prominent Black abolitionists including Frederick Douglass and James Forten criticised as alternatives to genuine emancipation. Formal apologies remain rare: the Netherlands expressed regret in 2023 without reparations, the UK voiced "profound sorrow" in 2007, and the U.S. House apologised in 2008 without presidential endorsement. CARICOM advances a 10-point reparations plan focused on wealth extraction and systemic racism, while historians such as Nigel Biggar and Henry Louis Gates Jr. contest its legal and historical basis.
