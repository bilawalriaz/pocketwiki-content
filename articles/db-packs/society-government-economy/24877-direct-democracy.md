# Direct democracy

In direct democracy, the electorate decides policy itself rather than through elected proxies. Most modern states instead run representative democracies, where people vote for legislators who enact laws. The two can blend: in a semi-direct democracy, representatives administer day-to-day governance but citizens remain sovereign through popular votes, initiatives, and recall. As of 2019, thirty countries allowed some form of national-level popular vote.

A referendum is a popular vote on a law or policy. It may be mandatory (required by law, as for Swiss constitutional amendments), optional (triggered by petition), or citizen-initiated. An initiative is a citizen-proposed draft law or constitutional change. Direct initiatives go straight to the ballot once the petition succeeds, as in California. Indirect initiatives go first to the legislature, which can act or, failing that, must send them to popular vote, the route for constitutional amendments in Switzerland, Liechtenstein, and Uruguay. A recall lets citizens remove an elected official before their term ends. Sortition, selecting officials by lot rather than election, is another direct-democratic tool. Adding a quorum requirement to a vote shifts simple majority rule toward consensus.

Popular assemblies, open meetings of citizens in a locality, were the highest decision-making body in Athens and survive in Switzerland's Landsgemeinden and New England's town meetings. Modern local consultations usually lack binding power, and political scientist Graham Smith argues they are a poor substitute for the Athenian model: self-selection produces unequal participation, and there is too little time for considered judgement. Above the local level, a full assembly of all citizens is impractical for a modern state.

## Historical roots

The earliest well-documented direct democracy was 5th-century BC Athens. Power sat in the assembly of male citizens, a 500-member council (boulê), and large juries chosen by lot. Several thousand of about 30,000 male citizens were politically active each year. The system was exclusionary: in mid-4th-century Athens, only about 40,000 of the roughly 260,000 residents, limited to adult men born to Athenian parents, could participate.

Republican Rome blended direct and indirect elements. The Senate advised but did not legislate; popular assemblies, organised by centuriae or tribes, voted on laws and officials proposed by elected magistrates. Some classicists describe the Roman Republic as a democracy with universal male suffrage, popular sovereignty, and open deliberation. The lex Titia of 27 November 43 BC removed many oversight provisions and is often used to mark the Republic's end.

## Modern practice

Switzerland is the clearest modern case. Citizens vote roughly four times a year on issues from school budgets to foreign policy, using postal ballots without registration. Pure direct democracy survives only in the cantons of Appenzell Innerrhoden and Glarus; elsewhere Switzerland is a semi-direct democracy. The 1848 federal constitution introduced the statute referendum; the 1891 constitutional amendment initiative added the right to propose changes. Over the past 120 years, more than 240 national initiatives have reached referendum, but only about 10% of those that survive parliamentary negotiation are approved by voters, who often choose the government's rewritten version. Federal constitutional changes require a double majority, approval by both a majority of individuals voting and a majority of cantons, an idea Switzerland adopted from the U.S. Congress in 1890. Between January 1995 and June 2005, Swiss citizens voted 31 times on 103 federal questions, while French citizens voted twice.

In Liechtenstein, 1,000 citizens can trigger a referendum on any law, but votes to suspend parliament or amend the constitution need at least 1,500 affirmative votes, so they fail under low turnout even when the percentage threshold is met.

In the United States, town meetings in New England predate the republic and remain the oldest American form. The framers deliberately rejected direct democracy at the national level. James Madison argued in *Federalist No. 10* that a pure democracy permits "the mischiefs of faction," allowing a majority to sacrifice the weaker party, and that a constitutional republic better protects individual and property rights. Despite this, ballot measures are now widely used at state level: constitutional amendment initiatives exist in nineteen states, statute law initiatives in twenty-one, statute law referendums in twenty-four, and recalls in nineteen states plus the District of Columbia. Theodore Roosevelt's 1912 "Charter of Democracy" framed the initiative and referendum as tools to correct, not replace, representative government.

The V-Dem Citizen-Initiated Component of Direct Popular Vote Index for 2021 ranks Switzerland at 0.841 and Uruguay at 0.766 at the top, followed by Taiwan (0.445), Slovenia (0.444), and Romania (0.416).

## The reform trilemma

Democratic theorists describe a trilemma: a direct-democratic system can deliver at most two of three desirable properties: participation (many people involved), deliberation (reasoned weighing of evidence), and equality (everyone's views count equally). Participatory democracy achieves participation and deliberation but attracts self-selected, unrepresentative participants. Deliberative democracy, in James Fishkin's sense, achieves deliberation and equality by drawing a random representative sample, but cannot include everyone who wants to take part. Fishkin argues random sampling is the realistic route toward escaping the trilemma, though doing so fully would require radical reforms.
