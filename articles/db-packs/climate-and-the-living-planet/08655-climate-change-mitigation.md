# Climate change mitigation

Climate change mitigation is human action to cut greenhouse gas emissions and to remove carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, slowing the warming those gases cause. The dominant gas is carbon dioxide, produced mainly by burning coal, oil, and natural gas for energy. Coal-fired power is the single largest source, at about 20% of emissions. After carbon dioxide, methane is the most important, with livestock and leaks from oil and gas wells as leading sources. Methane leaves the atmosphere in about a decade, while carbon dioxide persists for millennia, so cutting methane gives fast but temporary relief.

The 2022 IPCC assessment warned that global emissions must peak before 2025 and fall by about 43% by 2030 to keep warming near 1.5 °C. Current national policies still put the world on track for roughly 2.7 °C of warming by 2100, well above the Paris Agreement goal of holding warming well below 2 °C. The cheapest way to cut emissions is to replace fossil-fuel energy with clean electricity. Onshore wind and solar photovoltaic power are now the lowest-cost options for new bulk electricity in many regions, with solar's levelized cost falling to roughly $0.04 per kWh. Their main weakness is variability, since the sun does not always shine and the wind does not always blow. Three fixes are widely used: long-distance transmission that links sunny and windy regions, energy storage such as batteries and pumped hydro, and demand management that shifts electricity use to times when generation is high. Wind and solar complement each other seasonally, with wind stronger in winter when solar output is low. Nuclear power can also supply low-carbon electricity, but new reactors take about a decade to build, slowing its contribution to near-term cuts. Coal-to-gas switching reduces emissions in the short term because gas emits about half the carbon dioxide of coal per unit of electricity, but methane leaks during extraction can erase that advantage, and new gas infrastructure risks locking in future emissions.

After electricity, the largest emissions come from transport, buildings, and industry. In transport, the main lever is electrification, including electric cars, buses, and rail, with hydrogen a possible option for long-distance heavy freight. In buildings, insulation, efficient heat pumps, and passive design cut demand for heating and cooling, which together make up about half of building energy use. Industrial emissions are the hardest to eliminate because cement, steel, and some chemical processes chemically require high-temperature heat. Carbon capture and storage applied to factory smokestacks, and direct air capture, which pulls CO₂ from ambient air, are the main options for these difficult-to-decarbonise sectors, but both remain expensive and unproven at scale.

Land and food systems account for roughly a quarter of all emissions. Cattle are a major methane source, and tropical deforestation releases carbon stored in old forests. Mitigation here has four components: demand-side changes such as eating less meat and wasting less food; farm-level changes such as better livestock breeding, manure management, and rice water management that can cut methane by up to 90% relative to flooded paddies; ecosystem protection, especially halting tropical deforestation; and restoring natural sinks through reforestation, wetland restoration, and better soil management. Soils hold more carbon than the atmosphere, and practices like cover cropping, reduced tillage, and biochar can sequester additional carbon, though measuring soil carbon gains is difficult. The richest 10% of people emit about half of lifestyle emissions, so high-consumption choices, especially frequent flying, large cars, and meat-heavy diets, carry the largest individual leverage.

Removing carbon already in the atmosphere will also be necessary. Nature-based approaches include regrowing forests, restoring coastal wetlands like mangroves that store carbon much faster than tropical forests, and protecting peatlands, which hold 42% of all soil carbon despite covering only 3% of land area. Technological approaches include bioenergy with carbon capture and storage, known as BECCS, and direct air capture. Both cost $30 to $500 per tonne of CO₂ removed and exist only at small scale.

Policies fall into three families. Market-based instruments put a price on emissions through a carbon tax or cap-and-trade systems where permits are bought and sold. By 2021 about 21% of global emissions were covered by a carbon price, though most prices were too low to change behaviour, with the European Union's price near €80 per tonne by 2022 being a high exception. Non-market instruments set standards, such as efficiency requirements for appliances, buildings, or vehicles, and these tend to work best in developing countries. Other policies reform fossil-fuel subsidies, which the IMF values at more than $5 trillion per year, and redirect that support to clean energy. International coordination under the UNFCCC began with the 1997 Kyoto Protocol, which set binding targets for wealthy countries, and continued with the 2015 Paris Agreement, under which nearly all countries submitted their own pledges.

The benefits of mitigation extend beyond avoided warming. Replacing fossil fuels cuts the air pollution that causes an estimated 5 to 8.7 million premature deaths per year. Economists estimate that keeping warming below 2 °C would cost about 1 to 2% of global GDP each year, far less than the 5% or more that the Stern Review estimated the damages of inaction would impose. The chief barriers to faster action are political, including fossil-fuel industry lobbying, public concern about local impacts of mines, wind farms, and transmission lines, and the difficulty of distributing costs fairly between wealthy and developing countries, with developing nations needing financial and technical support to pursue low-carbon development at the same time.
