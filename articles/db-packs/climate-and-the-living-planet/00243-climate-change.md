# Climate change

Climate change means a long-term shift in global temperatures and weather patterns. Although natural cycles do exist, the warming observed since the Industrial Revolution is driven primarily by human activity, especially the burning of fossil fuels (coal, oil, and gas), which releases greenhouse gases that trap heat in the lower atmosphere. The result is global warming and a destabilised climate system with severe environmental, social, and economic consequences.

## How the warming works

The core mechanism is the greenhouse effect. Gases such as carbon dioxide, methane, and nitrous oxide are largely transparent to incoming sunlight but absorb the infrared radiation that Earth's surface emits back toward space. By re-radiating some of that energy downward, they warm the lower atmosphere. Since the pre-industrial era, atmospheric CO₂ has risen by roughly 50%, to levels not seen for millions of years, while methane and nitrous oxide have also climbed sharply. Natural influences such as solar variability and volcanic aerosols have had a negligible effect on the long-term warming trend over this period.

## The scientific timeline

Understanding developed in stages. Eunice Newton Foote demonstrated that CO₂ absorbs heat in 1856, and John Tyndall identified the specific gases responsible in 1859. Svante Arrhenius published the first climate model projecting CO₂-driven warming in 1896. Gilbert Plass built an early computer model in the 1950s, and Charles Keeling began the continuous atmospheric CO₂ record in 1958 (the "Keeling Curve"). The 1979 Charney Report affirmed the accuracy of those forecasts, and in 1988 James Hansen testified to the U.S. Senate on human-caused warming in the same year the IPCC was established.

## What has already changed

The decade 2016–2025 averaged about 1.26 °C [1.13–1.36 °C] above the pre-industrial baseline, and 2024 was the warmest year on record at roughly +1.60 °C. Land is warming nearly twice as fast as the oceans, and the Arctic three to four times faster than the global average, a pattern known as Arctic amplification and linked in part to declining albedo (a surface's reflectivity; as bright ice gives way to dark ocean, more sunlight is absorbed). Observable consequences include more frequent and intense heatwaves, droughts, wildfires, and heavy precipitation, along with sea-level rise of about 4.8 cm per decade between 2014–2023 from thermal expansion of seawater and melting ice. Oceans are also warming and acidifying as they absorb CO₂, ecosystems are being disrupted (coral bleaching, species migration), and the human costs fall disproportionately on communities that contributed least to the emissions.

## Where the climate is heading

The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (2021) projects that by 2100 warming is very likely to reach 1.0–1.8 °C under a very-low-emissions pathway or 2.1–3.5 °C under an intermediate pathway. Crossing certain thresholds, known as tipping points, could trigger irreversible changes; for example, the Greenland ice sheet may pass a tipping point somewhere between 1.7 °C and 2.3 °C of warming, and major ocean currents could also collapse. Because CO₂ persists in the atmosphere for centuries to millennia, some consequences, particularly sea-level rise and ocean acidification, will continue long after emissions fall.

## Responding through mitigation and adaptation

Mitigation means cutting greenhouse-gas emissions and strengthening natural carbon sinks. The main strategies are transitioning to renewable energy (solar, wind, hydro, and nuclear), improving energy efficiency, and protecting or restoring forests. Carbon dioxide removal (CDR), from reforestation to direct air capture, can supplement these efforts. Adaptation means adjusting to the warming already locked in: building flood defences, developing drought-resistant crops, and improving early-warning systems. The UNFCCC (adopted 1992, in force from 1994) set the diplomatic framework, the 1997 Kyoto Protocol imposed binding targets on developed nations, and the 2015 Paris Agreement aims to hold warming "well below 2 °C." Current national pledges, however, put the world on track for roughly 2.8 °C of warming.

## What remains uncertain

Three questions dominate. First, feedbacks, especially the behaviour of clouds, still limit the precision of future projections. Second, the feasibility, scalability, and side effects of large-scale CDR and of solar radiation modification are heavily debated. Third, who should pay remains contested: developed nations have emitted the most historically, developing nations face the worst impacts, and "loss and damage" for impacts that cannot be adapted to is still unresolved.
