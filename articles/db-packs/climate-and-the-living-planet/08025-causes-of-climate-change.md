# Causes of climate change

Human activity is the dominant cause of the warming observed since the Industrial Revolution began around 1750. The underlying physical principle is the greenhouse effect: greenhouse gases (water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, and several artificial gases) let incoming sunlight pass through the atmosphere and reach the surface, but absorb a portion of the outgoing infrared radiation, slowing heat loss to space. Sunlight enters, heat gets partly trapped.

A *forcing* is any externally imposed change to the planet's energy balance. Between 1750 and 2005, the rise in atmospheric CO₂ alone produced a positive radiative forcing of about 1.66 W/m² averaged over Earth's surface. The most important human forcings are greenhouse gases; the most important human offsets of warming are aerosol particles, which reflect sunlight and have a net cooling effect.

Since 1750, atmospheric CO₂ has risen about 48% and methane about 160% by 2019. Ice cores show current CO₂ levels are higher than at any point in the last 2 million years, and current methane levels are far higher than at any point in the last 800,000 years. Direct measurements since 1958 (the Keeling Curve) have documented the year-by-year rise from 313 ppm in 1958 to 415 ppm in May 2019. The 2025 Global Carbon Budget reported fossil-fuel CO₂ emissions of 38.1 billion tonnes, a record high. By 2023 Earth's energy imbalance (the difference between absorbed sunlight and outgoing heat) was more than double the IPCC's best estimate.

The dominant gas is CO₂, about 75% of the 59 billion tonnes of CO₂-equivalent greenhouse gases emitted in 2019. CO₂ comes mainly from burning coal, oil, and natural gas for transport, manufacturing, electricity, and heating, with additional contributions from deforestation, which both releases stored carbon and removes a future sink, and from industrial chemistry that makes cement, steel, aluminium, and fertiliser. Methane (about 18%) comes from livestock, manure, rice paddies, landfills, wastewater, coal mining, and oil and gas extraction. Nitrous oxide (about 4%) comes mostly from microbial breakdown of fertiliser. Artificial fluorinated gases (CFCs, HFCs, PFCs, SF₆) add about 2%. The livestock sector alone accounts for over 18% of total emissions: roughly 9% of human CO₂, 35–40% of human methane, and 64% of human nitrous oxide.

Sulphate aerosols from burning sulphur-rich coal and bunker fuel have partially offset greenhouse warming through global dimming, scattering sunlight and altering cloud properties so that clouds contain more numerous but smaller droplets that reflect more light. Since 1990, pollution controls have reduced aerosol emissions, weakening this masking and allowing more underlying greenhouse warming to show through. Black carbon (soot) is an exception: it absorbs sunlight, darkens snow and ice, accelerates melt, and adds to warming.

The warming response of the climate is shaped by *feedbacks*, which either amplify (positive) or dampen (negative) an initial change. The main amplifying feedbacks are the water-vapour feedback (warmer air holds more moisture, and water vapour is itself a greenhouse gas), the ice–albedo feedback (melting snow and sea ice expose darker ocean and land that absorb more sunlight; sea ice normally reflects 50–70% of incoming sunlight, while open ocean reflects about 6%), thawing permafrost (which releases additional CO₂ and methane), and net cloud changes. The principal balancing mechanism is radiative cooling: a warmer surface radiates more heat to space. Feedbacks, especially cloud behaviour, are the largest source of uncertainty in climate projections. Since 1979 the warming influence of greenhouse gases has nearly doubled.

The land and oceans together act as carbon sinks, absorbing roughly half of every year's CO₂ emissions. Land plants and soils remove about 29%; the oceans absorb 20–30% by dissolving CO₂ at the surface and carrying it into the deep via the overturning circulation. This fraction is not fixed. As emissions rise, the fraction absorbed falls below 40%, because droughts and heat suppress plant growth, soils release carbon as they warm, the ocean becomes more acidic, and changes in thermohaline circulation and phytoplankton populations reduce uptake.

Land-use change has two opposing effects. Replacing dark forest with lighter cropland raises surface *albedo* (reflectivity), which has a slight global cooling influence, but tropical deforestation also reduces evapotranspiration, shifting local climate toward warming. So far, the net global effect of historical land-use change has been a small cooling, dominated by albedo. Forest loss between 2001 and 2018 broke down into 27% permanent clearing for agriculture, 24% shifting cultivation, 26% logging, and 23% wildfire, with global tree-cover loss roughly doubling since 2001.

Natural drivers play a much smaller role. Volcanic eruptions inject aerosols and ash that cool the surface for a few years, but total volcanic forcing since 1750 has been negligible relative to human forcing. Solar output has shown no upward trend since 1880, even though the lower atmosphere has warmed strongly, and between 1750 and 2007 solar forcing increased by at most 0.12 W/m² compared with about 1.6 W/m² from human activities. The stratosphere (the layer above the troposphere) has cooled over recent decades while the lower atmosphere has warmed, a vertical pattern inconsistent with stronger sunlight but consistent with greenhouse gases trapping outgoing heat near the surface.

Four independent lines of evidence identify humans as the cause: greenhouse gas concentrations and their warming properties are well understood; climate models cannot reproduce observed warming without including human emissions; natural drivers such as solar and volcanic changes are too small and move in the wrong direction; and the vertical pattern of warming (troposphere up, stratosphere down) carries a distinctive fingerprint that matches greenhouse forcing and rules out solar forcing. The likely range of human-induced surface warming by 2010–2019 compared to 1850–1900 is 0.8–1.3°C, with a best estimate of 1.07°C, while natural forcings and internal variability together contributed only about ±0.2°C.
