# Pollution

Pollution is the introduction of contaminants—substances or energy—into the natural environment that cause harm. It manifests as air, water, soil, noise, light, thermal, and chemical pollution, with severe consequences for human health and ecosystems. In 2019, pollution caused approximately nine million deaths globally, making it a critical challenge of the Anthropocene.

## Forms and Definitions

Pollution encompasses any contaminant—solid, liquid, gas, or radiant energy—that degrades environmental quality or poses health risks. The U.S. EPA defines it as materials that impair natural resources or offend senses. Major forms include air pollution from chemicals and particulates, water pollution from industrial discharge and sewage, soil contamination by heavy metals and hydrocarbons, and emerging concerns like plastic pollution, electromagnetic radiation, and light pollution.

Chemical pollution involves novel entities such as PFAS ("forever chemicals"), synthetic compounds resistant to degradation and linked to cancers, thyroid disease, and immune suppression. In 2022, chemical pollution was recognized as a planetary boundary, a threshold defining a safe operating limit for an Earth system process. Other forms include smog, an air pollution mixture of smoke and fog, often photochemical, formed when sunlight drives reactions among vehicle and industrial emissions.

## Sources

Natural events contribute. Volcanic eruptions release CO₂, SO₂, and hydrogen halides, contributing to acid rain, and emit fine particles containing arsenic and mercury. Wildfires produce CO, CO₂, and fine particulates harmful to animals and humans.

Human activities dominate. Motor vehicles and coal-fired power plants are top contributors to air pollution. Industrial sectors—chemical plants, refineries, mining—emit hazardous wastes, heavy metals, and persistent compounds. Agriculture contributes via pesticide spraying, livestock emissions, and runoff. Plastic production has generated massive waste, with most plastics persisting for centuries. Greenhouse gas emissions drive climate change, linking air and water pollution through ocean acidification, the process by which the ocean absorbs CO₂ and becomes more acidic, disrupting marine life.

Pollution is classified by source. Point source pollution originates from a single identifiable location, such as a factory pipe. Nonpoint source pollution is diffuse, originating from many dispersed origins, such as agricultural runoff across a watershed.

## Health and Ecosystem Effects

Pollution impacts human health broadly. Air pollution caused 1.67 million deaths in India in 2019 and over 50,000 annually in the U.S., primarily from PM2.5, fine particles 2.5 micrometers or smaller that penetrate deep into the lungs, and fossil fuel combustion. Water pollution leads to 1.4 million premature deaths yearly, especially in regions lacking sanitation. Exposure increases risks of respiratory disease, cancer, neurological damage, and developmental disorders. Vulnerable groups—children, elderly, and marginalized communities—are disproportionately affected.

Environmentally, pollution disrupts ecosystems. Biomagnification, the increase in toxin concentration as it moves up the food chain, concentrates pollutants in predators. Ocean acidification threatens marine life, smog reduces plant photosynthesis, and nitrogen deposition alters soil chemistry. Invasive species spread via plastic debris, forming garbage patches like the Great Pacific Garbage Patch.

## Regulation and Cost

Pollution control aims to reduce emissions and effluents, the liquid waste discharged into water bodies, into air, water, and soil. Prevention and waste minimization are preferred over end-of-pipe solutions. Policies include life-cycle assessments, evaluations of environmental impacts throughout a product's existence, transparency measures, and economic instruments like Pigouvian taxes, levies on goods that generate negative externalities, and cap-and-trade systems, where regulators set a total emissions cap and issue tradable permits. International coordination occurs through UNEP and treaty bodies. In 2023, a Global Framework on Chemicals was adopted with 28 targets, including phasing out hazardous pesticides by 2035.

Pollution imposes significant societal costs by acting as a negative externality, a cost imposed on third parties not involved in a market transaction. Firms do not bear full environmental damage, so free markets often overproduce polluting goods. Economists advocate for internalizing these costs through taxation or regulation to balance utility gains against environmental harm. U.S. abatement costs reached nearly $27 billion in 2005.

## History

Pollution has paralleled civilization since prehistoric fire use. The Industrial Revolution intensified contamination, with coal-powered cities facing severe air and water quality issues. Early regulations emerged in medieval Europe. In 1306, King Edward I banned coal burning in London due to smoke. The 1858 Great Stink on the Thames prompted London's sewer system. The 1952 Great Smog of London killed at least 4,000 and led to the U.K. Clean Air Act. The U.S. followed with the Clean Air Act, Clean Water Act, and NEPA between 1963 and 1970, the 1980 Superfund legislation passed after the Love Canal and Valley of the Drums scandals, and the 2007 IPCC report confirming human role in global warming since 1950. Nuclear accidents and large-scale disasters fostered international cooperation on transboundary risks.

Legacy pollution, persistent contamination remaining at sites long after original sources ceased, remains a long-term remediation challenge alongside ongoing emissions.
