# Climate sensitivity

Climate sensitivity measures how much Earth's surface warms for a doubling of atmospheric CO₂ above its pre-industrial concentration of 280 ppm. The formal definition is the change in surface temperature per unit change in radiative forcing, where radiative forcing is an imbalance, in watts per square metre, between incoming sunlight and outgoing heat radiation. Doubling CO₂ produces a forcing of about 3.7 W/m². The Stefan–Boltzmann law, which relates a body's radiated heat to its temperature, gives roughly 1°C of warming from this forcing alone. Actual warming is much larger because climate feedbacks amplify the initial response.

## Feedbacks

Feedbacks are secondary effects triggered by warming. The water vapour feedback dominates: a warmer atmosphere holds more moisture, and water vapour is itself a greenhouse gas. The ice–albedo feedback adds further warming: melting ice exposes darker ground, which absorbs more sunlight. Cloud feedback, lapse rate feedback (changes in the rate temperature falls with altitude), and carbon cycle feedbacks add complexity. Balancing feedbacks, such as increased heat radiation to space from a warmer surface, partially offset warming, but the net effect amplifies the initial forcing. The dominant source of uncertainty in climate sensitivity is feedback strength, especially cloud behaviour, not the basic physics of CO₂ absorption.

## Two key measures

Scientists distinguish two timescales. The **transient climate response (TCR)** is the warming at the moment of doubling in a scenario where CO₂ rises 1% per year, averaged over a 20-year window centred on doubling. TCR is "likely" between 1°C and 2.5°C. The **equilibrium climate sensitivity (ECS)** is the long-term warming once the climate fully adjusts, which can take centuries or millennia because the deep ocean slowly releases stored heat. ECS exceeds TCR for this reason.

The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (2021) gives ECS a "high confidence" range of 2.5°C to 4°C, with a best estimate of 3°C. ECS is extremely unlikely below 1°C and very unlikely above 6°C. The "likely" range has narrowed only modestly since the 1979 Charney Report estimate of 1.5–4.5°C, despite decades of model improvement.

A closely related measure, **Earth system sensitivity (ESS)**, extends ECS by including feedbacks that act over millennia, such as vegetation change and loss of continental ice. ESS may be roughly twice ECS, but the geological data needed to constrain it are limited and highly uncertain.

## Non-constant sensitivity

Climate sensitivity is defined relative to a doubling from pre-industrial levels, yet the climate system is not static. While significant ice remains, the ice–albedo feedback keeps sensitivity elevated. Paleoclimate data show higher sensitivity during glacial periods, when ice cover was extensive. During the Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum, roughly 55.5 million years ago, a large carbon release raised global temperatures by about 6°C, possibly under above-average sensitivity. Crossing tipping points, such as the loss of major ice sheets, can change sensitivity at the timescale of the affected subsystem.

## How sensitivity is estimated

Four approaches are used, each with different strengths and weaknesses.

**Industrial-age data (1750–present).** Observed temperature, ocean heat uptake, and estimated forcing are combined in an energy-balance model. Using 0.85°C of warming and 0.42 W/m² of ocean heat uptake against 2.2 W/m² of net forcing yields roughly 1.8°C, on the low side of other estimates.

**Paleoclimate reconstructions.** Past CO₂ levels, derived from air trapped in ice cores, and temperature reconstructions constrain sensitivity. The Last Glacial Maximum (~21,000 years ago) is the most studied period. A limitation is that the dominant feedbacks in a glacial climate may differ from those under a future CO₂ doubling, and continuous ice cores extend back less than one million years.

**Climate models.** Sensitivity is an emergent property of coupled atmosphere–ocean simulations, arising from the interaction of all modelled processes rather than being prescribed. The CMIP6 generation shifted the mean ECS upward from 3.2°C to 3.7°C, largely because improved cloud representations suggest fewer low clouds and more absorbed sunlight. About a fifth of CMIP6 models are "hot models" with ECS above 4.5°C; their projections are considered implausible because they fail to reproduce observed 20th-century warming.

**Natural experiments.** Solar-cycle variations and volcanic eruptions provide independent constraints, but the short timescales and confounding factors (such as volcanic aerosols contaminating the solar signal) limit them to bounds on transient response rather than precise estimates.

## Why the uncertainty matters

If ECS exceeds 3.4°C, emissions cannot be reduced fast enough to meet the Paris Agreement's 2°C target through conventional decarbonisation alone. Halving the uncertainty in TCR has been estimated to be worth trillions of dollars in mitigation planning. Higher sensitivity also increases decadal temperature variability, making both heat extremes and cold extremes more likely. Atmospheric CO₂ has already risen from 280 ppm in the 18th century to over 415 ppm by 2020, producing about 1°C of warming. Because CO₂'s incremental warming effect diminishes at higher concentrations, Earth was already more than halfway to doubled-CO₂ radiative forcing by 2016, and additional emissions continue to close that gap.
