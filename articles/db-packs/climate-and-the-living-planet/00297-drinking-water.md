# Drinking water

Drinking water, or potable water, is water safe for human consumption. Access and quality depend on infrastructure, treatment, and regulation. Unsafe water causes over half a million deaths per year, mostly in developing countries, from diseases such as cholera and diarrhea.

## Sources and supply

Potable water comes from groundwater (springs, aquifers), surface water (rivers, lakes, glaciers), rainwater harvesting, or desalinated seawater. Surface water and groundwater must be treated before consumption because they routinely contain pathogens, chemical contaminants, or suspended particles that make them unsafe. Piped delivery, or tap water, is the most efficient distribution method, but the systems are expensive. Replacement costs in industrialized countries reach about $200 billion per year, and urban leakage rates of 50% are common.

## Quantity and usage

Daily water needs vary by activity, age, and climate. In the United States the Adequate Intake is 4.0 liters per day for men and 3.0 liters per day for women, though thirst is a more reliable guide than any fixed amount. Active people in hot climates can need more than 6.0 liters per day. Most household water is used for toilets and washing rather than drinking, so indoor plumbing accounts for a much larger share of consumption than the drinking water itself. Water makes up about 60% of an adult male's body weight.

## Quality and health risks

Safe water is monitored through three categories of parameters. Microbiological parameters track bacteria such as *Escherichia coli* and viruses, the most common cause of waterborne disease. Chemical parameters track heavy metals, pesticides, and other toxic substances. Physical parameters track properties such as turbidity, the cloudiness caused by suspended particles, which signals how easily pathogens can travel through the water.

Two contaminants affect very large populations. Fluoride at high levels causes skeletal fluorosis, a condition that damages bones, in millions of people worldwide. Arsenic in groundwater affects around 140 million people globally, with Bangladesh suffering the largest mass poisoning of a population, beginning in the 1970s when wells were drilled to replace surface water contaminated with bacteria. Persistent organic pollutants such as PFAS are widespread, but their long-term health effects remain under study.

## Treatment and regulation

Standard treatment includes chlorination, which disinfects water using chlorine; filtration, which physically removes particles; and UV exposure, which inactivates microorganisms with ultraviolet light. Desalination removes salt from seawater and is used in dry coastal regions. Point-of-use methods such as solar disinfection, which uses sunlight to kill pathogens in clear bottles, are promoted in low-income settings. Fluoride is often added to municipal water to reduce tooth decay, though the practice remains controversial in some communities. Regulations vary: the European Union Water Framework Directive and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency set binding standards, while the WHO publishes global guidelines.

## Global access

In 2015, 91% of the global population had access to an improved water source, defined as one protected from outside contamination, such as a piped connection or a covered well. The remaining 1.8 billion people used unsafe sources, and access is lowest in Sub-Saharan Africa, where coverage ranges from 40% to 80%. Conflict can sharply reduce access. Even in developed nations, aging infrastructure and contamination incidents such as the 2014 Flint water crisis in Michigan, where lead leached from pipes into the drinking water, show that near-universal coverage does not guarantee safety. The WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme tracks progress toward universal access.

## Key events

In 1854, John Snow traced a cholera outbreak in London to a contaminated water pump, a finding that founded the field of epidemiology. In 1993, a *Cryptosporidium* outbreak in Milwaukee sickened many people, demonstrating that even treated water can fail when pathogens slip through filtration. In 2000, an *Escherichia coli* outbreak in Walkerton, Ontario, killed seven people. The 2010 UN Millennium Development Goal for drinking water access was met five years early, and by 2015, 91% global access was recorded. In 2022, a water crisis in Jackson, Mississippi, again showed how infrastructure failure in wealthy countries can cut off safe water.

## Open questions

The long-term health effects of most PFAS compounds remain unclear. Water fluoridation is still debated in some communities. The common advice to drink eight glasses of water a day has no strong scientific basis.
