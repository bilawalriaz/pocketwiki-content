# Aquatic ecosystem

An aquatic ecosystem is a community of organisms living in and around a body of water, together with the non-living components of that environment. It is structured by interactions among organisms and by physical and chemical conditions such as temperature, light, salinity, flow, and nutrient availability.

The two main types are marine ecosystems, defined by high salt content, and freshwater ecosystems, defined by low salt content. Marine waters cover more than 70% of Earth's surface and hold more than 97% of its water, with an average salinity of 35 parts per thousand. Freshwater habitats divide into lentic systems (still water: pools, ponds, lakes), lotic systems (flowing water: streams, rivers), and wetlands, where soil is saturated for at least part of the year.

## Habitats and zones

In the ocean, the oceanic zone is open water inhabited by whales, sharks, and tuna; the benthic zone is the substrate below, where many invertebrates live; and the intertidal zone lies between high and low tides. Near-shore habitats include coral reefs, mangroves, kelp forests, salt marshes, and seagrass meadows. In the deep ocean, hydrothermal vents support communities based on chemosynthetic bacteria that metabolize hydrogen sulfide from volcanic vents, instead of sunlight.

Lakes have three zones: the pelagic open water, the benthic bottom and shore, and the profundal deep region that light cannot reach. A practical distinction is that ponds and pools have their entire bottom exposed to light, while lakes do not.

Rivers are structured by gradient and current velocity. Faster, turbulent water carries more dissolved oxygen and supports greater biodiversity, dividing rivers into upland and lowland types. River ecosystems form a watershed network, in which headwater streams feed progressively larger rivers. Streams shaded by a forest canopy derive most of their food base from leaf litter, while wider, unshaded streams depend more on algae. Anadromous fish, which migrate from sea to river, are an important nutrient source.

Wetlands are transitional zones between waterbodies and dry land. Their oxygen-poor, saturated soils force plant roots to adapt, and the wetlands rank among the most biologically diverse ecosystems while removing excess nutrients such as nitrates and phosphorus.

## Biotic and abiotic controls

The living component divides into autotrophs, which produce organic matter from inorganic material, and heterotrophs, which consume other organisms. In sunlit waters, algae are usually the most important autotrophs; in shallow water, rooted and floating vascular plants add further biomass, and the combination drives the high production of estuaries and wetlands. Salt tolerance separates the systems: euryhaline organisms tolerate a wide salinity range, while stenohaline organisms tolerate only a narrow range, so most freshwater species cannot survive in salt water and vice versa.

The non-living factors that structure these ecosystems include substrate, depth, nutrients, temperature, salinity, and flow, and they often form feedback loops; for example, sediment influences whether aquatic plants establish, but those plants trap more sediment and add organic matter to it. Dissolved oxygen is often the single most important variable. Fish depend on it, and in severe depletion some gulp air at the surface; plants respond with aerenchyma, a spongy tissue that carries oxygen to roots. Anaerobic bacteria are killed by oxygen.

Nutrients, especially the relative amounts of nitrogen and phosphorus, control which algae dominate. When nutrient input is excessive, algal blooms develop; when the algae decay, oxygen is consumed and fish decline, and in coastal waters such as the Gulf of Mexico this has created a hypoxic dead zone. Salinity determines which species are present and what kind of wetland forms in an estuary, and dams that reduce spring flooding and sediment delivery can cause saltwater intrusion in coastal wetlands.

## Threats

Ecosystem health degrades when a stress exceeds the system's capacity to absorb it. Physical stresses include changes in temperature, flow, and light; chemical stresses include nutrient loading, oxygen-consuming waste, and toxins; biological stresses include over-harvesting and introduced species. Climate change is shifting species distributions and damaging deep-sea biodiversity, coastal fish, crustaceans, and coral reefs. Human-made systems such as drainage ditches and irrigation channels often trade biodiversity for their intended function. The Great Lakes show the combined effects of pollution, over-harvesting, and invasive species on a freshwater system.
