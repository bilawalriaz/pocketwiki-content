# Solar energy

Solar energy is radiant light and heat from the Sun captured by technologies that convert sunlight into heat, electricity, or chemical fuel. In 2021, Lazard reported that building new utility-scale solar plants became cheaper than building new coal or gas plants.

## The size of the resource

Earth's upper atmosphere receives about 174 petawatts (1 PW = 10¹⁵ W) of solar radiation; roughly 30% is reflected, and about 122 PW is absorbed by land, oceans, and atmosphere. That absorbed energy drives weather, the water cycle, and photosynthesis, and it is the origin of essentially all fossil fuel reserves. The solar energy reaching Earth each year equals 3,850,000 exajoules, more than humanity consumes in a single hour.

Usable potential depends on cloud cover, land availability, and geography. A 2021 Carbon Tracker Initiative estimate found that meeting total global energy demand with solar would require only about 450,000 km² of land, roughly the area of Sweden.

## How sunlight is captured

Solar technologies split into two families. Active solar uses mechanical devices such as pumps and fans; passive solar uses building design, including orientation toward the Sun and thermal mass (stone, water, or concrete) that absorbs, stores, and slowly releases heat.

For heating, solar thermal collectors absorb sunlight to warm water or air. As of 2015, China led deployment with 309 GWth of solar thermal capacity. The three main collector types are evacuated tubes (44% of the market), glazed flat plates (34%), and unglazed plastic collectors (21%), used almost entirely for swimming pool heating.

For electricity, photovoltaic (PV) cells convert sunlight directly into electric current. Concentrated solar power (CSP) systems use mirrors or lenses to focus sunlight onto a small area, producing high-temperature heat that drives a conventional turbine. The main CSP designs are parabolic troughs, solar towers, linear Fresnel reflectors, and Stirling dishes. Because CSP produces heat, it can store energy in molten salt and keep generating electricity through cloud cover or after sunset. The Solar Two project (1995–1999) demonstrated molten-salt storage with 99% annual efficiency, and Chile's Cerro Dominador plant can run for up to 17.5 continuous hours without sunlight.

## Architecture, agriculture, and transport

Passive solar design dates to the Greeks and Chinese, who oriented buildings southward to capture winter sun. Modern practice adds computer modelling, selective shading, and thermal mass to control daylight, heating, and ventilation. Cities built with dark surfaces such as asphalt become warmer than the surrounding countryside, an urban heat island effect reduced by painting roofs white and planting trees; a Los Angeles study projected a 3°C cooling at a cost of $1 billion, returning about $530 million per year in benefits.

In farming, row orientation, timed planting, and mixed cropping adjust how much light crops intercept. Greenhouses, originally used by Romans for year-round cucumbers, now grow species outside their native climate, and solar panels increasingly power farm equipment such as grape presses.

Solar transport has progressed since the 1980s. Average speeds in the World Solar Challenge across Australia rose from 67 km/h in 1987 to over 90 km/h by 2007. The 2015–2016 Solar Impulse 2 flight completed the first solar-powered circumnavigation of the globe.

## Fuels and storage

Because sunlight is intermittent, storing its energy is central to large-scale use. Options include thermal mass, phase-change materials such as paraffin wax, molten-salt tanks, battery banks, pumped hydro, and grid-tied net metering, where utilities credit customers for excess solar electricity exported to the grid.

Solar fuel research aims to turn sunlight into storable chemical energy. Artificial photosynthesis tries to reduce CO₂ to methanol, while high-temperature solar concentrators at 2,300–2,600°C can split water to make hydrogen. Israel's Weizmann Institute runs a 1 MW solar furnace that decomposes zinc oxide to produce hydrogen, a process known as Solzinc.

## A short history

Solar technology surged in the late 19th century: Augustin Mouchot demonstrated a solar steam engine at the 1878 Paris Universal Exposition, and Frank Shuman built the first solar thermal power station in Maadi, Egypt, in 1912–1913. Progress then stalled as coal and oil became cheap, and only revived after the 1973 oil embargo. Government programmes in the United States and Japan drove down costs, and by 2007 global solar water heating had reached 154 GW.

The IEA has projected that solar could supply one-third of global energy by 2060, but only with sustained political commitment to climate limits and the build-out of storage and grid infrastructure to handle the Sun's variability.
