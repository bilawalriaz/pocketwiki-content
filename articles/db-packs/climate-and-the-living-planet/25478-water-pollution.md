# Water pollution

Water pollution is the contamination of lakes, rivers, oceans, aquifers, and groundwater by substances or energy that impair legitimate uses such as drinking, irrigation, or supporting aquatic life. It is caused almost entirely by human activities.

## Sources and pathways

Four source categories release contaminants into water: sewage (municipal and domestic wastewater), industrial activities (factories, refineries, power plants), agriculture (fertilizer, pesticides, livestock waste), and urban runoff from roads, rooftops, and parking lots.

Inputs are classified as point sources, which have a single identifiable origin such as a pipe or oil spill, or nonpoint sources, which are diffuse contributions such as agricultural runoff or atmospheric deposition that cannot be traced to one location. Nonpoint pollution is the cumulative result of many small inputs across a landscape and is harder to regulate than a single pipe.

About 80% of marine pollution originates on land, reaching the ocean through rivers, sewage outflows, wind-blown debris, and atmospheric fallout.

## Categories of pollutants

Pollutants fall into five overlapping classes:

- Pathogens: bacteria (E. coli, Salmonella), viruses (Hepatovirus A, norovirus), protozoa (Giardia, Cryptosporidium), and parasitic worms. They cause waterborne disease, and coliforms are used as indicator organisms because they are easier to detect than the pathogens they co-occur with.
- Organic chemicals: petroleum hydrocarbons, PCBs, pesticides, PFAS, and pharmaceutical residues. Many are persistent organic pollutants that resist degradation and accumulate in tissue.
- Inorganic chemicals: heavy metals (mercury, lead, chromium), ammonia, nitrates, and phosphates. Nutrients drive eutrophication, in which nitrogen and phosphorus from fertilizer and sewage trigger algal blooms whose decomposition strips dissolved oxygen from the water.
- Solids and plastics: suspended sediment, and microplastics from synthetic textiles, tire wear, and urban dust. Microplastics reach oceans through stormwater and sewage.
- Physical changes: elevated temperature, pH shifts, and salinity changes. Thermal pollution lowers dissolved oxygen, killing fish; salinity shifts harm freshwater ecosystems.

Pathogens are the direct link to human disease. The 2017 estimate that polluted water killed 1.8 million people reflects the combined effect of microbial and chemical exposure.

## Atmospheric contributions

Air pollution becomes water pollution when airborne pollutants settle. Sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides form acid rain that damages aquatic ecosystems. Atmospheric CO₂ dissolves in seawater, lowering pH through ocean acidification. Mercury from coal combustion and industrial emissions accumulates in fish.

## Thermal pollution

Power plants and factories use water as a coolant and discharge it warmer than ambient. Because warm water holds less dissolved oxygen, this stresses fish and shifts communities toward heat-tolerant species. Cold water released from deep reservoirs can shock downstream ecosystems with the opposite extreme.

## Groundwater

About a quarter of the world's population depends on groundwater for drinking. Contamination enters through leaking septic systems, landfill leachate, agricultural fertilizers, and industrial sites, and aquifers are slow to recover once polluted. Naturally occurring arsenic and fluoride also exceed safe levels in many regions.

## Measurement

Pollution is assessed physically through temperature, turbidity, and conductivity; chemically through pH, dissolved oxygen, biochemical oxygen demand, and nutrient and metal concentrations; and biologically through bioindicator organisms such as copepods whose health reflects ecosystem condition. Fecal coliforms, especially E. coli, serve as the standard bacterial indicator of fecal contamination.

## Control

Effective control requires matched infrastructure and management. Well-designed sewage treatment plants remove over 90% of pollutant load; advanced stages strip nutrients and micropollutants but cost more energy. Industries often pre-treat waste before discharge to municipal systems. Agricultural controls limit fertilizer and pesticide runoff and use anaerobic lagoons for animal waste. Construction sites use erosion and sediment controls such as silt fences and sediment basins. Stormwater management uses green roofs, infiltration basins, constructed wetlands, and retention ponds that slow flow and filter runoff.

Combined sewer overflows during storms remain a persistent problem in older cities because raw sewage spills into waterways when capacity is exceeded. Many persistent organic pollutants, including PFAS and most pharmaceuticals, pass through conventional treatment and require a fourth treatment stage that few plants operate.

## Legal framework

The United States Clean Water Act of 1972, amended in 1987, authorizes the EPA to regulate surface water pollution and fund municipal sewage upgrades, and defines point sources for enforcement. The Philippine Clean Water Act of 2004 governs wastewater management there. A 2024 UK Royal Academy of Engineering report identified untreated sewage as a public health priority, drawing comparisons with the 19th-century campaign to eliminate cholera through sanitation. Heavy rainfall and low river flows concentrate sewage, a problem that disrupted open-water swimming at the 2024 Paris Olympics when the River Seine exceeded E. coli safety thresholds.

Plastic and microplastic pollution continues to rise because total plastic production keeps expanding. Synthetic textiles, tire wear, and urban dust together account for over 80% of microplastic contamination, and Europe and Central Asia contribute roughly 16% of global microplastic discharges to the sea. A 2022 study of pharmaceutical pollution across 258 rivers serving 470 million people found contamination threatened environmental or human health at more than a quarter of monitored sites, with the worst conditions in low- and middle-income regions lacking wastewater infrastructure.
