# Carbon dioxide

Carbon dioxide (CO₂) is a linear, three‑atom molecule: one carbon double‑bonded to two oxygens (O=C=O), with a C=O bond length of 116.3 pm. It is a trace atmospheric gas — about 428 ppm as of mid‑2025 — and it behaves as a greenhouse gas because its vibrational modes absorb infrared radiation. The shape, the double bond, and the infrared activity together explain most of what CO₂ does in nature, industry, and climate.

## Carbon cycle and life

CO₂ is the central currency of the global carbon cycle. Photoautotrophs (organisms that feed themselves using light) — plants, algae, and cyanobacteria — pull it from the air and, using sunlight, combine it with water to make sugars, releasing oxygen. The first step of that fixation, in which inorganic carbon is built into an organic molecule, is catalysed by the enzyme RuBisCO, thought to be the most abundant protein on Earth. Aerobic respiration runs the reaction in reverse: organisms oxidise sugars back to CO₂, and humans exhale roughly 1.0 kg per day. The same molecule is therefore both the carbon feedstock of life and the waste of the metabolism that consumes those sugars.

CO₂ moves continuously between four reservoirs: the atmosphere, the biosphere, the oceans, and the geosphere (rocks and fossil fuels). On long timescales it ends up locked in sediments and fossil deposits. On human timescales, roughly half of the CO₂ released by burning those deposits is re‑absorbed by land plants and the ocean; the rest accumulates in the air. Less than 1% of annual production is used commercially.

## Water, acid, and the ocean

When CO₂ dissolves in water it forms carbonic acid (H₂CO₃), a weak acid that partly dissociates into bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) and carbonate (CO₃²⁻) ions. In seawater, at pH 8.2–8.5, bicarbonate is the dominant dissolved form, not CO₂ itself. This is why the ocean holds a large inventory of inorganic carbon, and why adding more atmospheric CO₂ changes ocean chemistry: extra dissolved CO₂ shifts the equilibrium toward more carbonic acid, lowering pH. That is ocean acidification, and it matters because corals, shellfish, and other organisms that build calcium‑carbonate skeletons are sensitive to lower pH.

In human blood the same bicarbonate system carries most metabolic CO₂ — about 70–80% as HCO₃⁻, 5–10% dissolved in plasma, and 5–10% bound to haemoglobin. Blood CO₂ also drives breathing: rising concentrations trigger the urge to inhale. Above about 5% in inhaled air, CO₂ causes hypercapnia and respiratory acidosis; above 10% it can cause suffocation and death, which is why poorly ventilated indoor spaces affect both comfort and cognition.

## Physics of the pure substance

At normal pressure solid CO₂ does not melt — it sublimes directly to gas at −78.5 °C, which is why "dry ice" disappears as a vapour rather than a liquid. A liquid phase exists only above 5.11 atm, and above 30.98 °C and 72.81 atm CO₂ becomes a supercritical fluid, a state with no clear distinction between liquid and gas. These thresholds, not everyday behaviour, define how CO₂ is stored industrially.

## Climate

Pre‑industrial air held about 280 ppm of CO₂; in July 2025 it reached about 428 ppm. The rise is driven mainly by fossil‑fuel combustion, which alone emitted about 36.8 billion tonnes of CO₂ in 2023, with smaller contributions from cement production and land‑use change. Because CO₂ absorbs outgoing infrared radiation, more of it in the atmosphere means more retained heat, and the rise is the primary driver of modern anthropogenic climate change. The oceans absorb roughly a third of human emissions, the same process that acidifies them.

## Industrial use

Most CO₂ is emitted, not used. Captured CO₂ finds applications that exploit the same properties nature exploits: carbonation of drinks, fire suppression (CO₂ smothers flames and does not support combustion), refrigeration as R‑744 in solid or supercritical form, enhanced oil recovery (injecting CO₂ to thin residual oil and raise pressure), and urea fertiliser manufacture.
