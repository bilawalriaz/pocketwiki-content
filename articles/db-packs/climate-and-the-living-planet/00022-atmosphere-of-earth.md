# Atmosphere of Earth

Earth's atmosphere is the layer of mixed gases held by gravity around the planet. It shields the surface from most meteoroids and ultraviolet solar radiation, moderates day–night temperature swings, retains heat through the greenhouse effect, redistributes warmth and moisture via air currents, and supplies the chemical conditions that allow life to exist.

## Composition and mass

By mole fraction, dry air is 78.08% nitrogen, 20.95% oxygen, 0.93% argon, and 0.04% carbon dioxide, plus trace gases and variable water vapor (about 1% at sea level). Water vapor averages roughly 0.25% by mass, ranging from about 10 ppm by mole fraction in the coldest air to 5% in hot, humid air. Trace gases include greenhouse gases (carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, ozone) and noble gases (neon, helium, krypton, xenon). Xenon is much scarcer than other noble gases, a puzzle called the missing xenon problem, with proposals involving Xe–Fe oxides in the lower mantle, xenon dioxide in silica, and reactions with iron and nickel in the core, though no consensus exists. Aerosols include dust, pollen, sea spray, volcanic ash, and compounds of chlorine, fluorine, and sulfur. The average molecular weight of dry air is about 28.96 g/mol.

Total atmospheric mass is about 5.15×10¹⁸ kg, with three-quarters within roughly 11 km of the surface. There is no definite boundary to space, but the Kármán line at 100 km is the conventional edge. Up to about 100 km, turbulence keeps gas ratios uniform; above roughly 100 km, molecular diffusion takes over, forming the heterosphere where lighter gases grow more abundant with altitude.

## Stratification

The vertical temperature profile defines five layers. The troposphere (0–12 km; 9 km at the poles, 17 km at the equator) holds about 80% of the atmosphere's mass and nearly all its water vapor, so most weather occurs there; temperature falls with height because the surface heats the air from below. The stratosphere (12–50 km) hosts the ozone layer, with temperature rising as ozone absorbs UV; this stable layer is unique to Earth because Mars and Venus lack enough oxygen. The mesosphere (50–80 km) is the coldest region, averaging about −85 °C, where most meteors burn up. The thermosphere (80–700 km) reaches 1500 °C from absorbing ionizing UV and X-rays, though the air is too thin to feel hot; the International Space Station orbits between 370 and 460 km. The exosphere (500–1,000 km upward, possibly to 190,000 km) is so tenuous it grades into interplanetary space, with particles on ballistic trajectories; Earth loses roughly 3 kg of hydrogen and 50 g of helium per second.

Secondary features include the ozone layer (peak 15 ppm at 32 km, about 90% of atmospheric ozone), the ionosphere (50–1,000 km, affecting radio propagation), and the planetary boundary layer (the troposphere's surface-influenced base).

## Physical and optical properties

Mean sea-level pressure is 101,325 pascals. Pressure falls exponentially with altitude, governed by the scale height of about 5.5–6 km, the altitude step over which pressure drops by a factor of e (about 2.718). Half the atmosphere's mass lies below 5.6 km, 90% below 16 km, and 99.99997% below the Kármán line. Sea-level air density is about 1.29 kg/m³, and the global mean surface temperature is 14–15 °C.

Clouds reflect roughly 20% of incoming sunlight, contributing two-thirds of Earth's albedo, the fraction of sunlight reflected back to space. Rayleigh scattering preferentially redirects short blue wavelengths, making the sky blue and sunsets red, while O₂ and O₃ absorb nearly all radiation below 300 nm and water vapor absorbs strongly above 700 nm. An optical transparency window runs from about 300 to 1,100 nm, with infrared and radio windows at longer wavelengths. Without greenhouse gases, Earth's mean surface temperature would be −18 °C rather than 15 °C.

## Circulation

Large-scale air motion distributes heat. Three convection cells dominate: the Hadley cell at the equator, the Ferrel cell at mid-latitudes, and the Polar cell at high latitudes. Where cells meet, jet streams form as narrow west-to-east bands near 9,100 m, strongest in winter; their instabilities steer weather systems. Gravity waves, Rossby waves, and atmospheric tides also shape the flow.

## Evolution

The first atmosphere, in the Hadean eon, was solar nebula gas, mostly hydrogen and simple hydrides such as water vapor, methane, and ammonia, largely stripped by the Moon-forming impact with Theia. As the crust solidified, water vapor condensed into a superocean, and volcanism plus the Late Heavy Bombardment built a second atmosphere of nitrogen, carbon dioxide, methane, and inert gases. Carbon dioxide then dissolved and reacted with weathered minerals to form carbonate sediments, and by about 3.4 billion years ago nitrogen dominated. The faint young Sun paradox notes that the early Sun radiated about 30% less energy, yet liquid water persisted.

Cyanobacterial photosynthesis slowly released oxygen, and the Great Oxygenation Event around 2.4 billion years ago ended banded iron formation deposition. The Proterozoic later endured a billion-year interval of euxinia, anoxic sulfidic oceans, before eukaryotic algae raised oxygen further, enabling the Avalon explosion in the Ediacaran and the Cambrian explosion 539 million years ago. Oxygen fluctuated over the past 600 million years, peaking near 35% about 280 million years ago in the Carboniferous.

## Air pollution and climate change

Human activity since 1750 has increased greenhouse gas concentrations and added aerosols, causing stratospheric ozone depletion driven by chlorofluorocarbons and warming. Global mean surface temperature was 1.1 °C higher in 2011–2020 than in 1850, raising concerns about sea level rise, ocean acidification, glacial retreat, and extreme weather.
