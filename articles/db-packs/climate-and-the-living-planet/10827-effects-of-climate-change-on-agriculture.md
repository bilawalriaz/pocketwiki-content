# Effects of climate change on agriculture

Climate change affects agriculture through a few linked mechanisms: higher temperatures, altered precipitation, more frequent extremes, and elevated atmospheric CO₂. The global effect on food supply is negative, but uneven: tropical and low-income regions lose the most, while some high-latitude areas see short-term gains.

## Core mechanisms

**Heat damage.** Staple crops tolerate a narrow temperature range. Maize fails to reproduce above 35 °C; soybeans above 38.8 °C; potatoes suffer physiological damage above 30 °C. Holding other factors constant, warming reduces yields of maize by about 7.4%, wheat by about 6%, and rice and soybean by about 3% per 1 °C. A 2026 FAO/WMO report identified 25 °C as a threshold beyond which yields decline significantly, with effects persisting for up to a year.

**Precipitation disruption.** Warmer air holds about 7% more water per 1 °C, so rainfall becomes heavier and less predictable. Under the mid-range scenario SSP2-4.5, individual precipitation events grow 11.5% larger while the intervals between them lengthen 5.1%; under SSP5-8.5 the figures are 18.5% and 9.6%. At the same time, plant water loss through evapotranspiration rises. The result is more intense droughts and more destructive floods. The 2020–2023 Horn of Africa drought illustrates this: increased evapotranspiration worsened a period that would have been more manageable in a cooler climate.

**CO₂ fertilization is a partial, qualified offset.** Higher CO₂ speeds photosynthesis in C3 plants (wheat, rice, soybeans) and reduces water loss by partially closing stomata, the small pores on leaves that control gas exchange. A 2005 review of free-air CO₂ enrichment (FACE) experiments, which are open-field trials that artificially raise CO₂ around growing plants, found an average 17% yield gain. The benefit is uneven: C4 crops like maize respond weakly, and grain nutritional quality falls (3–17% less protein, iron, and zinc under projected 2050 CO₂ levels).

**Ozone damage.** Methane from agriculture reacts in sunlight to form ground-level ozone, an air pollutant that directly damages crop tissue. Ozone has cost the four major staples about 5% of yield and cancels out most of the CO₂ fertilization benefit.

## Pests, soil, and water

Warmer winters let insect pests survive and add breeding cycles, potentially causing 10–25% yield losses per 1 °C. Fungal diseases like wheat rusts and soybean rust are spreading poleward. Weeds, mostly C3 plants, often outcompete C4 crops under elevated CO₂. Soil loses carbon faster as it warms, reducing fertility. Glacier-fed irrigation, especially across the Hindu Kush-Himalayas, is threatened as ice mass declines, putting the 2.4 billion people in the Ganges, Indus, and related drainage basins at risk.

Coastal farmland faces saltwater intrusion as seas rise. In Vietnam's Mekong Delta, a one-metre sea-level rise would inundate large areas of rice paddies. The Arctic and Siberia may gain arable land as frozen ground thaws, though productivity gains are uncertain.

## Observed and projected yield changes

The IPCC's 2022 Sixth Assessment Report found high confidence that climate change to date has had primarily negative effects on yields, especially for maize and wheat in the tropics. A 2019 study tracking ten crops across roughly 20,000 political units found climate-driven yield changes ranging from −13.4% (oil palm) to +3.5% (soybean), with losses concentrated in Europe, sub-Saharan Africa, and Australia. Between 1961 and 2021, global agricultural productivity could have been about 21% higher without climate change.

By mid-century under high-emission scenarios, the four major staples (maize, rice, wheat, soybeans) could see 5–14% yield declines without adaptation. Under the high-emission scenario RCP8.5, which assumes no meaningful climate policy, global yields of these crops are projected to fall 11–25% by 2100, with losses exceeding 25% in current breadbaskets including the US, southern Europe, Brazil, and Australia. Maize is the most vulnerable, projected to fall 24% globally by 2100 under SSP5-8.5. Wheat is more resilient because CO₂ fertilization offsets part of the heat damage.

Synchronized crop failures across major producing regions are a particular risk. Under high emissions, the probability of breadbasket failures (defined as ≥10% yield loss) could rise 4.5-fold by 2030 and 25-fold by 2050. If regions with synchronized growing seasons all failed at once, losses of the four major crops could reach 17–34%.

## Livestock and people

Livestock prefer ambient temperatures of 10–30 °C. Under SSP5-8.5, animals in low latitudes could face 72–136 additional days per year of extreme heat stress. Heat stress lowers milk yields, impairs reproduction, and during heatwaves causes mass mortality. Animal parasites and vector-borne diseases are also spreading.

Labour capacity in Sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia could fall 30–50% under 3 °C of warming as outdoor heat-stress days multiply, potentially raising crop prices by around 5%.

## Food security and the limits of adaptation

The number of people at risk of hunger could rise by 8–80 million by 2050, nearly all in Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Central America, depending on warming and adaptation. In 2021, 720–811 million people were undernourished; about 200,000 faced catastrophic food insecurity. Cereal prices could rise 1–29% by 2050, putting 1–181 million additional low-income people at risk. Global food demand is projected to grow roughly 60% in coming decades, even as climate change works against that growth.

The Green Revolution lifted yields 250–300% since 1960, and continuing productivity gains plus expanded cropland are projected to keep widespread famine at bay through the 21st century. Climate change intensifies agriculture's existing harms, including soil loss, pollution, and greenhouse gas emissions, and land conversion to farming releases more carbon, creating a feedback loop. Without measures that both curb warming and build resilience in food systems, pressure on regional food supplies will grow most where smallholders depend on rain-fed agriculture.
