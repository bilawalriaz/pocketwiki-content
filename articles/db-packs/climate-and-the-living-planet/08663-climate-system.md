# Climate system

The climate system is the coupled set of five interacting components that together determine Earth's average weather over roughly 30 years: the **atmosphere** (air), **hydrosphere** (liquid water), **cryosphere** (ice and permafrost), **lithosphere** (the rocky upper Earth), and **biosphere** (living things). Humans are sometimes treated as a sixth, the **anthroposphere**, because of their outsized effect. Climate is a *statistical* property of this system, not a single day's weather, and emerges from the constant exchange of energy, water, and chemical elements among the five components.

## The five components

The **atmosphere** is mostly inert nitrogen (78%), oxygen (21%), and argon (0.9%), but trace gases do most of the climatic work. Water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, and nitrous oxide are **greenhouse gases**: visible sunlight passes through them and warms the surface, but the infrared radiation the surface emits is partly absorbed, raising surface temperature. Water vapour contributes about 50% of the present greenhouse effect, clouds about 25%, and CO₂ about 20%.

The **hydrosphere** is dominated by the ocean, which covers 71% of Earth's surface to an average depth of nearly 4 km and holds far more heat than the atmosphere. Seawater averages about 3.5% salt. Only 2.5% of all water is fresh, most locked as ice and snow.

The **cryosphere** includes sea ice, ice sheets, permafrost, and snow. The Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets, averaging about 2 km thick, hold the great majority of frozen water and flow slowly toward their margins. Because the Northern Hemisphere has more land, more of it is snow-covered, but both hemispheres hold similar amounts of sea ice.

The **lithosphere** changes on geological timescales and is treated as nearly static in climate models, yet mountains steer winds and rainfall, the position of continents sets ocean-basin geometry, and coastal land has more moderate climates than continental interiors.

The **biosphere** alters climate through its colour (vegetation may be darker or lighter than bare soil), its water uptake (roots capture water that would otherwise run off, releasing it to the air), and carbon exchange. Marine phytoplankton fix almost as much carbon as all land plants.

## How energy, water, and elements move

The Sun supplies nearly all the energy entering the system; the Earth's core and lunar tides contribute very little. Because the equator receives more sunlight than the poles, warm air rises there, flows poleward, cools, sinks, and returns equatorward. Earth's rotation deflects these motions (to the right in the Northern Hemisphere, left in the Southern), carving the atmosphere into large circulation cells. Monsoons arise because land heats and cools faster than the ocean, building seasonal pressure differences between land and sea.

The ocean moves heat by **thermohaline circulation**, driven by density differences from temperature and salinity, and by wind drag. Colder, saltier water sinks; warmer surface water moves toward the poles. Upwelling brings cold deep water to the surface, cooling the air above.

The **water cycle** is the other great energy mover. Evaporation takes substantial heat from the surface; condensation releases that **latent heat** (heat stored in water vapour and released when it condenses) higher in the atmosphere, and this is the atmosphere's main energy source. The tropics receive more rain than they lose to evaporation, while subtropical regions lose more than they receive.

**Biogeochemical cycles** shuffle elements among the five components. In the fast carbon cycle, plants take up CO₂ through photosynthesis and return it through respiration. In the slow cycle, volcanoes release CO₂ from the mantle, slightly acidic rain dissolves rock (weathering), and the resulting minerals are carried to the sea, where they can become carbonate sediments, locking carbon back into the lithosphere. The nitrogen cycle begins when microbes *fix* inert atmospheric N₂ into reactive forms usable by life.

## What changes the climate

Climate varies on every timescale from seasons to billions of years. **Internal variability** is fluctuation generated inside the system itself. Atmospheric pressure see-saws such as the North Atlantic Oscillation shift weather between Greenland, Europe, and Eurasia for days to decades. Coupled ocean–atmosphere oscillations such as El Niño–Southern Oscillation, the Pacific Decadal Oscillation, and the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation redistribute heat between the deep ocean and the surface, partly because the ocean's mass and heat capacity dwarf the atmosphere's.

**External forcings** are changes imposed from outside the five components, measured as **radiative forcing**, the change in Earth's energy balance. Natural forcings include variations in the Sun's output (including the 11-year solar cycle, which mostly affects the stratosphere) and the **Milankovitch cycles**: slow changes in orbital eccentricity, axial tilt, and precession that redistribute sunlight seasonally and are linked to the coming and going of ice ages. Large volcanic eruptions inject SO₂ into the stratosphere, where it forms sulphate aerosols that reflect sunlight and cool the surface for a year or more.

Human activities are now a dominant forcing. Burning fossil fuels has moved carbon from the lithosphere into the atmosphere as CO₂, raising concentrations of long-lived greenhouse gases. Human activity also injects **aerosols** (tiny liquid or solid particles) that reflect sunlight and cool the surface, but their net cooling effect is far smaller than the warming from greenhouse gases. Land-use change alters reflectivity and the water cycle; black carbon from fires darkens snow, helping it melt. Fertilizer use has greatly increased the amount of biologically active nitrogen.

## Feedbacks and the energy budget

A change in one component is amplified or damped by **feedbacks**. Higher CO₂ raises temperature, which raises atmospheric water vapour (itself a greenhouse gas), which raises temperature further: a positive feedback. Expanded snow and ice cover raises surface **albedo** (reflectivity), reflecting more sunlight and amplifying cooling, also positive. Observations and models indicate a net positive feedback amplifying today's global warming. The components also respond at very different speeds: the atmosphere adjusts in hours to weeks, while the deep ocean and ice sheets take centuries to millennia to reach a new equilibrium.

The whole system is governed by Earth's **energy budget**: incoming sunlight minus outgoing reflected light and infrared radiation. When incoming exceeds outgoing, the imbalance is positive and the climate warms; when outgoing exceeds incoming, it cools. Forcing, feedbacks, and the slow response of the ocean together set the pace and magnitude of every climate change the planet experiences.
